How to use this guide

Imagine a computer learning to make useful guesses from examples. In each topic, the definition is the formal idea, the example makes it concrete, and the trade-off tells you when the method can mislead you. Read the cards as a connected story—not as a list of magic algorithms.

Interview answer recipe: say what the method does, give a small real example, name its key assumption or trade-off, and explain how you would check whether it worked. A simple accurate explanation beats unexplained jargon.

The graphs below are teaching illustrations—not benchmark results. All explanatory text is part of the page; charts add a visual aid but are not required to understand the material.

1Fundamentals

First ask what kind of feedback a learner receives, then ask how complicated its guesses should be and what assumptions it uses to generalise.

Supervised, unsupervised, semi-supervised & reinforcement learning

Definition. Supervised learning learns from examples with answers (features X, label y). Unsupervised learning looks for structure without answer labels. Semi-supervised learning combines a small labelled set with many unlabelled examples. Reinforcement learning learns actions by trying them and receiving rewards or penalties over time.

India-context example. Predicting house prices from past sale records is supervised. Grouping districts by rainfall and soil is unsupervised. Labelling a few thousand Hindi support messages and using many unlabelled ones is semi-supervised. A warehouse robot learning a safe route using rewards is reinforcement learning.

Trade-off. Labels can be expensive and wrong; clusters need human interpretation; unlabelled data can reinforce bias; rewards can be hard to define and unsafe to explore. Do not call every trial-and-error system reinforcement learning—there must be actions, feedback, and a reward objective.

Bias–variance trade-off

Definition. Bias is error from a model being too simple to capture the real pattern. Variance is how much its learned rule changes if the training sample changes. Total prediction error often reflects both, plus unavoidable noise.

Example. Estimating monsoon crop yield using only state-wide average rainfall may miss soil and irrigation differences (high bias). A giant decision tree that memorises each farm's exact history may change wildly for a new sample (high variance).

Trade-off. More flexibility can reduce bias but increase variance. Use validation or cross-validation to find a useful middle ground. The simple “balance” picture is a guide, not a guarantee that every model follows a perfect U-shaped curve.

Overfitting and underfitting

Definition. Underfitting means the model fails to learn important patterns; overfitting means it learns training-specific noise as if it were a lasting pattern.

Example. A rule that says every loan applicant has the same risk underfits. A model that flags a person as risky only because a rare typo appeared in one training record overfits.

Check. Compare training and validation performance. Poor scores on both suggest underfitting or bad data/features. Great training performance but much worse validation performance suggests overfitting, leakage, or a distribution shift. Use regularisation, simpler models, more representative data, or early stopping—not simply more complexity.

Train / validation / test split

Definition. Training examples teach the model; validation examples help choose models and settings; the test set estimates performance after choices are complete.

Example. If predicting exam pass risk, train on older student cohorts, validate on a separate cohort to tune the model, then evaluate once on a final untouched cohort.

Trade-off. A split wastes some data for fitting but guards against self-congratulation. Keep related observations together (for example, all rows for one patient or household) and use time-based splits when future prediction is the real task. Never use test outcomes to tune a threshold and still call that score an untouched test result.

Cross-validation: k-fold, stratified, leave-one-out & time-series split

Definition. Cross-validation repeats training and validation on different folds, then averages results. In k-fold CV, partition into k folds and rotate the held-out fold. Stratified k-fold keeps class proportions similar. Leave-one-out (LOO) tests one observation at a time. Time-series split trains on the past and validates on later periods.

Example. For a rare disease classifier, stratification keeps enough positive cases in each fold. For next-month electricity demand, each split must train on earlier months and validate on later months.

Trade-off. More folds use data efficiently but cost more computation. LOO is expensive and can have high-variance estimates. Ordinary shuffled folds leak future information in time series. Keep preprocessing inside each fold, fit only on that fold's training portion.

Parametric vs non-parametric models

Definition. A parametric model has a fixed-form rule described by a fixed number of learned parameters (linear regression is a common example). A non-parametric model does not assume that fixed simple shape; its effective complexity can grow with data (such as a deep tree or nearest-neighbour model).

Example. A straight line predicts rent from floor area with a few coefficients. A nearest-neighbour predictor can follow local patterns among flats with similar area, neighbourhood, and age.

Trade-off. Parametric models can be compact, fast, and interpretable but miss complicated patterns. Flexible non-parametric models can adapt but need representative data and may be slower or overfit. “Non-parametric” does not mean “has no parameters.”

Generative vs discriminative models

Definition. A discriminative model estimates the label given features, often written P(y|x), or directly finds a decision boundary. A generative model describes how data and labels could be generated, often modelling P(x,y) or P(x|y), and may generate new samples.

Example. A spam classifier drawing a boundary between spam and ordinary mail is discriminative. A model learning what spam-like messages look like, or a language model generating a new message, is generative.

Trade-off. Discriminative methods often focus directly on classification accuracy. Generative assumptions can help with missing data or generation but may model data poorly. “Generative” does not automatically mean a model's output is true or factual.

Curse of dimensionality

Definition. As the number of input features grows, the space grows so quickly that available examples become sparse; distance and density estimates become less reliable.

Example. A search for similar farmers using rainfall, soil, crop, market distance, prices, dozens of weather summaries, and hundreds of irrelevant columns may have no truly close neighbours.

Response. Collect more relevant examples, select useful features, reduce dimensions, regularise, or use domain knowledge. High-dimensional data is not always bad—images and text naturally are high-dimensional—but it makes data efficiency and distance-based methods harder.

No Free Lunch theorem

Definition. Across all possible prediction problems, no learning algorithm wins everywhere. A method does well when its assumptions fit the problem.

Example. A linear trend may forecast a steadily growing series well, while missing Diwali sales spikes. A seasonal model may handle festival demand but add needless complexity to a stable process.

Use it. Treat it as a reason to compare sensible candidates on representative validation data, not as a reason to test every algorithm. The theorem concerns broad sets of possible tasks; real problems have structure we can use.

Inductive bias

Definition. Inductive bias is the set of assumptions that lets a model generalise beyond observed examples.

Example. A straight-line regression assumes a roughly linear relationship. A CNN assumes nearby pixels and repeated visual patterns matter. A tree assumes useful decisions can be made through feature-based splits.

Trade-off. A helpful bias means fewer examples are needed; a wrong bias blocks the true pattern. Choose assumptions based on the task and check them against held-out data and domain expertise.

2Data preprocessing & feature engineering

A model learns from the table it receives. Careful preparation means making useful information legible without letting future answers sneak into training.

Missing values: mean/median, KNN imputation & MICE

Definition. Missingness may mean “not measured,” “not applicable,” or “not recorded.” Mean imputation fills a numeric blank with the training mean; median is less pulled by extreme values. KNN imputation uses similar rows. MICE (Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations) repeatedly models each incomplete column using the others and can create multiple plausible datasets.

Example. If some crop plots lack a soil-moisture reading, median imputation is a simple baseline. KNN might use nearby plots with similar crop and soil. A missingness indicator can tell the model that a field inspection was skipped.

Trade-off. Simple filling shrinks variation and can distort relationships. KNN can be slow and needs comparable scaled features; MICE needs careful assumptions and more work. Fit imputers on training data only, then apply them to validation/test. Ask why a value is missing before choosing a fill.

Outliers: IQR, Z-score & Isolation Forest

Definition. An outlier is unusually far from a reference pattern—not automatically an error. IQR flags values below Q1 − 1.5×IQR or above Q3 + 1.5×IQR. Z-score measures standard deviations from a mean. Isolation Forest identifies points that are easy to separate using random splits.

Example. A ₹10 lakh UPI transaction might be a data-entry error, a business payment, or genuine fraud. Context decides whether it is suspicious.

Trade-off. IQR is robust for simple univariate checks; Z-scores work best for roughly bell-shaped data and are distorted by extremes. Isolation Forest handles multiple columns but can flag rare legitimate groups. Investigate before deleting; fraud and safety datasets may care most about rare extremes.

Feature scaling: Min–Max, Standardization & Robust scaler

Definition. Min–Max usually maps values to a set range such as 0–1. Standardization subtracts the training mean and divides by standard deviation. Robust scaling uses the median and interquartile range.

Example. Distance-based KNN should not let annual income measured in lakhs overwhelm age measured in years only because its numbers are bigger.

Use. Scaling often matters for KNN, SVM, PCA, and gradient-based models. Tree splits are usually insensitive to monotonic scaling. Min–Max is sensitive to extremes; standardization is also affected by outliers; robust scaling is steadier but not a universal cure. Learn scale parameters from training data only.

Categorical encoding: one-hot, label, target & frequency

Definition. One-hot creates an indicator column per category. Label encoding maps categories to integers. Target encoding replaces a category with a smoothed label statistic. Frequency encoding uses how often that category occurs.

Example. City names such as Pune, Patna, and Kochi have no natural numeric ranking, so one-hot can preserve “different” without implying order. For a truly ordered category such as low/medium/high risk band, an ordinal mapping may be meaningful.

Trade-off. One-hot can create many columns. Integer labels can accidentally suggest an order. Target encoding can leak target information, especially for rare categories; use out-of-fold encoding and smoothing. Unknown categories at prediction time need an explicit safe handling rule.

Feature selection: filter, wrapper & embedded methods

Definition. Filter methods rank variables using general statistics (correlation, mutual information). Wrapper methods repeatedly train and evaluate subsets (for example, recursive feature elimination). Embedded methods select or shrink features during model fitting (Lasso and tree-based importance are examples).

Example. For predicting bus delays, a domain expert might remove a unique trip ID, a filter could rank rainfall, and a wrapper could test useful subsets against validation accuracy.

Trade-off. Filters are fast but ignore model-specific interactions. Wrappers can be expensive and overfit the validation set. Embedded selection depends on the model and can be unstable with correlated features. Do selection inside training folds, never using the final test labels.

Feature extraction vs feature selection

Definition. Selection keeps a subset of original columns. Extraction transforms original data into a new representation, often with new combined dimensions (PCA components or image embeddings).

Example. Keeping rainfall and soil pH is selection; combining many correlated weather columns into a few principal components is extraction.

Trade-off. Selected features are often easier to explain. Extracted features may compress information but lose obvious meanings. Use extraction when compact representations matter; prefer selection when users need to act on identifiable inputs.

Imbalanced data: SMOTE, undersampling & class weights

Definition. Class imbalance means one label is much rarer than another. SMOTE makes synthetic minority examples by interpolating between minority neighbours. Undersampling removes some majority examples. Class weights make errors on rare examples count more during fitting.

Example. If 1 in 1000 card payments is fraudulent, a model that always says “genuine” is 99.9% accurate and still useless for catching fraud.

Trade-off. SMOTE may create unrealistic samples, especially across class boundaries; undersampling discards information; weights can increase false alarms. Split first, then apply resampling to training folds only. Choose precision/recall and thresholds from costs and operational capacity, not accuracy alone.

Data leakage

Definition. Leakage occurs when training or model selection uses information that would not honestly be available at prediction time, making evaluation too optimistic.

Example. Predicting loan default at application time using a column populated only after collection agents contacted the borrower is leakage. Scaling the full dataset before splitting lets test-set statistics influence training.

Prevent it. Define the prediction moment, audit every column's timestamp and meaning, split by person/time where needed, and fit preprocessing inside each training fold. Beware duplicates and near-duplicate records across train/test.

Multicollinearity & VIF

Definition. Multicollinearity means predictors carry overlapping information. VIF (variance inflation factor) estimates how much a coefficient's variance is increased because one predictor can be explained by the others; large VIF values are warning signs, not universal automatic deletion rules.

Example. Monthly salary and annual salary are nearly the same signal. A linear model may struggle to say which one individually deserves credit.

Effect. Predictions can still be good while individual coefficients become unstable and hard to interpret. Remove redundant variables, combine them, or use Ridge; do not blindly delete correlated inputs if they carry distinct useful meaning.

Binning & discretization

Definition. Binning turns a numeric value into intervals, such as age groups or rainfall bands. Intervals can be equal-width, equal-count (quantile), or chosen using meaningful domain cutoffs.

Example. Weekly rainfall might be labelled “dry,” “typical,” and “heavy” to make a farm advisory easier to communicate.

Trade-off. Bins simplify patterns and can handle nonlinear effects, but discard within-bin detail and add arbitrary boundaries. Fit data-driven cut points on training data and document meaningful policy thresholds separately from statistical ones.

Log & power transformations: Box–Cox and Yeo–Johnson

Definition. Transformations change a variable's scale or shape. A log compresses large positive values. Box–Cox chooses a power transformation for positive values; Yeo–Johnson can also handle zero and negative values.

Example. A few very expensive homes can stretch a price axis. Taking log(price) makes proportional differences easier to model than absolute rupee differences.

Trade-off. Transformations can reduce skew or stabilise variance but change interpretation and require careful conversion back (including bias considerations). Do not apply Box–Cox blindly to non-positive data; fit any learned transform on training data only.

Feature interactions & polynomial features

Definition. An interaction lets the effect of one feature depend on another. Polynomial features add powers or products, such as area² or area × distance, so a linear model can represent curves or combined effects.

Example. The benefit of irrigation may depend on rainfall: extra irrigation can help in a dry season but be unnecessary during heavy monsoon.

Trade-off. Interactions express useful domain knowledge but multiply feature count and overfitting risk. Scale appropriately and regularise. A polynomial model remains linear in its learned coefficients, even though its curve in the original input is nonlinear.

3Regression

Regression estimates a numeric quantity. The line or curve is only useful if its assumptions, residuals, and error measure fit the decision being made.

Linear regression & its assumptions

Definition. Linear regression predicts a number as a weighted sum, ŷ = β₀ + β₁x₁ + … + βₚxₚ. Common assumptions for trustworthy coefficient tests are: linearity in parameters, independent errors, constant error variance (homoscedasticity), and no perfect multicollinearity; normal residuals are mainly needed for some small-sample inference.

Example. Estimate a flat's rent from area, age, and distance to a metro station. A larger area may raise rent while greater travel distance lowers it, holding other model inputs fixed.

Trade-off. Fast and interpretable, but a straight relationship may be wrong and omitted factors can confound coefficients. Check residuals, unusual points, time/group structure, and whether each predictor would be known at prediction time. Association is not proof of causation.

Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) vs Gradient Descent

Definition. OLS finds coefficients minimising the sum of squared residuals, often with a direct matrix solution. Gradient descent starts with coefficients and repeatedly moves them opposite the loss gradient.

Example. OLS is convenient for a modest rent dataset. Gradient descent is useful when training a very large model in batches or when no convenient closed-form solution exists.

Trade-off. OLS can be exact and simple but matrix calculations can be expensive or unstable for huge, collinear data. Gradient descent needs scaling and a well-chosen learning rate; too high can diverge, too low is slow. They are alternative optimisation approaches, not different meanings of linear regression.

Ridge (L2), Lasso (L1) & Elastic Net

Definition. Regularisation adds a penalty for large coefficients. Ridge uses a sum of squares, Lasso a sum of absolute values, and Elastic Net combines both.

Example. When many weather measurements overlap, Ridge tends to share/shrink their coefficients. Lasso may set some to exactly zero, giving a shorter model. Elastic Net can be helpful when groups of correlated measurements occur.

Trade-off. Penalties can reduce variance but add bias; tune strength using cross-validation. Scaling is important. Lasso's selected variable can be unstable among correlated features; sparsity is not proof that excluded variables do not matter.

Polynomial regression

Definition. Polynomial regression adds powers of inputs to a linear model, allowing curved relationships while remaining linear in coefficients.

Example. Electricity demand may grow slowly at moderate heat and sharply when temperatures become very high; a squared temperature term can represent some curvature.

Trade-off. Higher degrees fit wiggles and outliers, often extrapolate wildly, and create correlated columns. Start with low degree, inspect plots, use validation and regularisation, and avoid assuming a curve remains plausible far beyond observed temperatures.

Logistic regression—and why it is a classifier

Definition. Logistic regression forms a weighted sum then passes it through a sigmoid to estimate a probability between 0 and 1; a threshold turns that probability into a class decision.

Example. It can estimate the chance that a transaction is fraud. An alert could be raised above a chosen risk threshold, which might be lower for a high-value transaction.

Why the name? It is called regression because it models the log-odds as a linear function, but it is commonly used for classification. It uses log loss because the loss matches probability prediction and strongly penalises confident wrong answers; MSE can give weak or poorly behaved gradients with a sigmoid. Calibration and threshold choice still need checking.

Heteroscedasticity & residual analysis

Definition. A residual is observed minus predicted value. Heteroscedasticity means residual spread changes with the prediction or an input, violating constant variance.

Example. Rent predictions may be tight for small towns but vary widely for luxury flats, creating a fan shape on a residual-versus-fitted plot.

Check. Plot residuals against fitted values, key features, and time; look for curvature, changing spread, clusters, and unusually large residuals. A log target, weighted regression, or different model may help. Residual patterns diagnose model mismatch; they are not a license to remove inconvenient cases.

R², Adjusted R², MSE, RMSE, MAE & MAPE

Definition. MSE averages squared errors; RMSE is its square root and uses the target's units; MAE averages absolute error. R² compares squared error with a mean baseline. Adjusted R² penalises adding predictors. MAPE averages absolute percentage error.

Example. For crop-yield forecasts in tonnes per hectare, RMSE tells a typical error scale in the same units; MAE is less dominated by a few extreme misses.

Choose carefully. MSE/RMSE punish large misses strongly; MAE is more robust. R² can be negative on test data and is not accuracy. MAPE breaks or explodes near zero, and percentage errors may be asymmetric. Report a baseline and metric that matches the real cost.

4Classification algorithms

A classifier assigns categories or scores. Understand what similarity, probability, or split rule it uses—and how the final decision gets made.

Naive Bayes & its “naive” assumption

Definition. Bayes' rule combines prior class frequency with how likely the observed features are under each class. Naive Bayes simplifies calculation by assuming features are conditionally independent given the class.

Example. A Hindi/English message containing “prize,” “click,” and a suspicious link may get a high spam score. The words are not truly independent, but the simplifying assumption can still classify well.

Trade-off. Very fast and useful with sparse text; probabilities can be poorly calibrated, and correlated features can make evidence seem over-counted. “Naive” describes the assumption, not the intelligence of the method.

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

Definition. KNN predicts from the labels or values of the k closest training examples according to a distance measure.

Example. To estimate a new shop's likely daily sales, find shops with similar location, size, footfall, and season, then average their sales.

Trade-off. Simple and flexible, but prediction can be slow, distance is sensitive to scaling and irrelevant columns, and high dimensions make “nearest” less meaningful. Small k is noisy; large k smooths over real local patterns.

Support Vector Machines: margin, soft margin & kernel trick

Definition. An SVM looks for a separating boundary with a wide margin between classes. A soft-margin SVM allows some violations, balanced by a penalty parameter. A kernel computes similarity as if data were mapped into a richer feature space without explicitly building every new feature.

Example. A linear SVM may separate ordinary and spam messages in TF-IDF space. A nonlinear kernel can model more curved boundaries for a smaller tabular problem.

Trade-off. Strong on some medium-sized, high-dimensional tasks, but scaling and tuning matter; kernel models can be slow on large datasets and less interpretable. A kernel is not magic: its similarity assumption must suit the data.

Decision trees: Gini, entropy & information gain

Definition. A tree repeatedly asks feature-based questions. Gini impurity and entropy measure how mixed the labels in a node are. Information gain measures the reduction in impurity after a split.

Example. “Is rainfall below 500 mm?” might divide crop-failure cases from successful harvests. The tree chooses candidate questions that make the resulting groups more orderly.

Trade-off. Trees handle nonlinear rules and are easy to explain at modest depth, but small data changes can alter splits and deep trees overfit. Gini and entropy often yield similar practical trees; an impurity score is not the same as causal importance.

Pruning decision trees

Definition. Pruning limits or removes branches that capture too much training-specific detail. Pre-pruning uses limits such as depth or minimum leaf size; post-pruning cuts branches after growing a larger tree, guided by validation or a complexity penalty.

Example. If a branch only explains one unusual loan record and does not help validation performance, pruning can leave a simpler risk rule.

Trade-off. Pruning reduces variance and improves readability but can erase a real rare pattern. Pick complexity from validation data, with special attention to minority-class performance.

Multiclass strategies: One-vs-Rest (OvR) & One-vs-One (OvO)

Definition. OvR trains one classifier per class against all others. OvO trains a classifier for each pair of classes and combines their votes or scores.

Example. For classifying road signs into 10 categories, OvR creates 10 “is this class?” models; OvO creates pairwise comparisons between each pair.

Trade-off. OvR needs fewer models and handles many classes simply, but classes can be uneven. OvO uses many smaller problems and may work well for some SVMs, but model count grows quickly. Some algorithms handle multiclass directly.

Probability calibration: Platt scaling & isotonic regression

Definition. A calibrated model's predictions near 0.7 should be correct about 70% of the time in comparable cases. Platt scaling fits a sigmoid to model scores; isotonic regression fits a flexible monotonic mapping.

Example. If a bank uses predicted default risk to decide how much capital to reserve, trustworthy risk probabilities matter—not just correct ranking.

Trade-off. Calibration needs held-out or cross-validated predictions and enough examples. Isotonic is flexible but can overfit small sets; Platt scaling is simpler. Check calibration by group and over time; calibration does not select the right action threshold.

5Ensemble methods

An ensemble combines learners. The key question is how the component models are trained and how their answers are combined.

Bagging

Definition. Bagging (bootstrap aggregating) trains many models on bootstrap samples—random samples drawn with replacement—and averages their predictions or votes.

Example. Ask a collection of independent student groups, each given a slightly different sample of past crop outcomes, to estimate next season's yield; average their guesses.

Trade-off. Averaging mainly reduces variance when learners make different errors. It is easy to parallelise but costs more inference and may not help highly correlated models. Bagging does not necessarily reduce bias much.

Random Forest: feature randomness & OOB error

Definition. A Random Forest builds many decision trees using bootstrap rows and random subsets of features at each split. Out-of-bag (OOB) rows—those not selected for a tree—can provide an internal estimate of performance.

Example. For predicting loan delay, every tree sees a different sample of applications and considers different candidate variables at each split; averaging softens the quirks of any one tree.

Trade-off. Usually robust and useful on tabular data, but larger and less transparent than one tree. OOB estimates are convenient, not a replacement for a realistic time/group holdout. Feature importance can be biased or misleading when inputs are correlated.

Boosting: AdaBoost & Gradient Boosting

Definition. Boosting trains learners in sequence so later learners focus on errors left by earlier ones. AdaBoost increases the influence of misclassified examples. Gradient Boosting fits new learners to gradients/residual-like signals of the current loss.

Example. A sequence of small trees might first catch very high-risk applications, then add corrections for combinations of repayment history and loan size.

Difference from bagging. Bagging trains largely independent models and averages; boosting builds a dependent sequence that corrects previous mistakes. Boosting can be accurate but sensitive to noisy labels and tuning. Use learning rate, shallow trees, subsampling, and early stopping thoughtfully.

XGBoost, LightGBM & CatBoost

Definition. These are efficient gradient-boosted tree libraries. XGBoost is known for regularisation and broad, robust tooling. LightGBM uses fast histogram techniques and leaf-wise growth. CatBoost offers ordered boosting and convenient handling of categorical features.

Example. A tabular model with many categorical product and region fields may benefit from CatBoost; a very large table might make LightGBM speed attractive; XGBoost is a strong general baseline.

Tuning. Compare validation performance and latency. Tune tree depth/leaves, learning rate, number of trees, row/column sampling, and regularisation; stop early. LightGBM's leaf-wise growth can overfit small data; categorical handling and missing-value defaults differ, so read the library's rules. No library always wins.

Stacking & blending

Definition. Stacking trains a meta-model to combine base-model predictions. Blending is a related approach that learns combination weights on a held-out blend set.

Example. Combine a calibrated logistic model, a forest, and a boosted tree to predict a delivery delay; a small meta-model learns when each one is useful.

Critical safeguard. Train the meta-model on out-of-fold predictions, not predictions from base models on the same rows they trained on. Otherwise leakage makes the stack look brilliant and fail later. More layers mean more complexity to validate and maintain.

Voting classifiers: hard vs soft

Definition. Hard voting chooses the class with the most model votes. Soft voting averages predicted class probabilities and chooses the largest average, often benefiting from well-calibrated scores.

Example. Three models independently judge whether a payment is suspicious: a majority vote decides, or their probabilities are averaged.

Trade-off. Voting is simple and can be stable when errors differ. Soft voting is harmed by incompatible or poorly calibrated probabilities; hard voting ignores confidence. Use a validation set to set weights and check whether the ensemble truly improves over its best member.

6Unsupervised learning

Without a known answer column, algorithms describe structure: groups, compressed directions, unusual points, or items that occur together.

K-Means: elbow, silhouette & K-Means++

Definition. K-Means assigns points to k groups, repeatedly updating each group's centre to minimise within-cluster squared distance. K-Means++ spreads initial centres apart. The elbow plot shows within-cluster error versus k; silhouette compares within-group closeness with separation from other groups.

Example. Group retail outlets by sales mix and footfall to plan service models—not to claim the data contains objectively true kinds of shops.

Trade-off. Fast and simple, but needs k, scaling, and roughly blob-like groups; outliers and initialisation matter. Run multiple starts. The elbow is subjective, and a high silhouette does not prove the segments are useful to the business.

Hierarchical clustering

Definition. Agglomerative clustering starts with each point alone and repeatedly merges groups; a dendrogram displays the merge tree. Linkage rules (single, complete, average, Ward) define how group-to-group distance is measured.

Example. Cluster districts by crop mix, rainfall, and market access, then inspect the dendrogram to see broad and fine groupings.

Trade-off. The tree of merges is informative and does not require fixing cluster count up front, but large datasets can be expensive. Results depend on scaling, distance, and linkage; once merged, a pair of groups generally cannot be split back apart.

DBSCAN

Definition. DBSCAN groups dense regions using a neighbourhood radius (epsilon) and minimum number of nearby points. It labels isolated points as noise and can find irregularly shaped clusters.

Example. Locate dense pickup hotspots and isolated courier trips in a city without telling the algorithm how many hubs to find.

Trade-off. Useful when clusters are dense and noise matters, but a single neighbourhood scale struggles when densities vary; high-dimensional distance weakens it. Scale features and inspect parameter sensitivity rather than treating “noise” as definitely erroneous data.

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM) & EM

Definition. A GMM represents data as a weighted mixture of Gaussian distributions and gives each point a probability of belonging to each component. Expectation-Maximisation alternates an E-step (estimate memberships) and M-step (update component parameters).

Example. A market's daily electricity use might be explained as a mixture of residential, office, and industrial usage patterns; a customer's membership can be partial.

Trade-off. Softer assignments and elliptical clusters can be more expressive than K-Means, but components can collapse or converge to local optima. Choose component count using validation/information criteria and multiple starts; a Gaussian-shaped component is an assumption, not a discovered human category.

PCA: Principal Component Analysis

Definition. PCA finds perpendicular directions that capture the greatest variance in numeric features, then projects data onto the top directions. It is an unsupervised, linear feature-extraction method.

Example. Many strongly related weather measures may be summarised as a few combined “hot/dry” or “wet” directions before clustering farms.

Trade-off. It can compress and de-correlate inputs, but components may be hard to interpret and low-variance features can still matter for the target. Scale when units differ, fit PCA only on training data, and remember PCA does not know which direction predicts your label.

t-SNE & UMAP

Definition. t-SNE and UMAP create low-dimensional views that try to keep important neighbourhood relationships visible. They are mainly visualisation tools for exploring complex data.

Example. Plot embeddings of Tamil, Hindi, and English support messages to see whether similar topics appear near one another.

Trade-off. Pictures can change with settings, random seed, sample, and preprocessing; axis distance and apparent island sizes may not have direct global meaning. Do not use a pretty 2-D map as proof of real clusters or as the sole input for downstream predictions.

LDA: Linear Discriminant Analysis

Definition. Linear Discriminant Analysis (LDA) is a supervised classifier that finds linear combinations separating labelled classes under assumptions about class distributions and shared covariance. In another context, “LDA” also means Latent Dirichlet Allocation for topic modelling; here it means Linear Discriminant Analysis.

Example. Use measurements of a flower or product to find a direction that separates its known quality categories.

Trade-off. LDA can be compact and effective with roughly Gaussian classes and enough samples, but shared-covariance and linear-boundary assumptions can fail. Do not confuse it with PCA: LDA uses labels and seeks separation; PCA ignores labels and seeks variance.

Anomaly detection

Definition. Anomaly detection finds examples that differ from a learned normal pattern, using methods such as isolation, density, reconstruction error, or one-class boundaries.

Example. Flag an unusual ATM withdrawal location/time combination for review, while allowing the bank to ask the customer rather than automatically block every unusual payment.

Trade-off. Rare does not mean harmful, and normal behaviour changes over time. Labels are often scarce; measure review precision and missed incidents, provide a human path for high-impact decisions, and update the reference population carefully.

Association rules: Apriori, support, confidence & lift

Definition. Association-rule mining finds item combinations. Support is the fraction of baskets containing a set; confidence is how often the right-hand item appears when the left-hand item appears; lift compares confidence with the item's baseline frequency. Apriori prunes itemsets using the fact that a frequent set's subsets must also be frequent.

Example. A kirana store could discover that customers buying tea often also buy biscuits, then test a shelf placement or offer.

Trade-off. Rules describe co-occurrence, not causation. High confidence may simply reflect a popular item; lift adds context but rare rules can be unstable. Evaluate on later baskets and consider seasonality, privacy, and whether an intervention actually helps.

7Model evaluation metrics

A metric translates predictions into a score. Choose the score that reflects the harm and benefit of mistakes in the real decision—not the one that looks largest.

Confusion matrix

Definition. For binary classification, a confusion matrix counts true positives (TP), false positives (FP), true negatives (TN), and false negatives (FN) against known labels.

Example. In disease screening, TP are patients correctly flagged, FN are missed cases, FP are people asked to take an unnecessary follow-up test, and TN are correctly not flagged.

Use it. Always state which class is “positive.” The matrix makes error types visible at one threshold, but does not show ranking across thresholds or whether scores are calibrated.

Precision, recall & F1-score

Definition. Precision = TP/(TP+FP), the share of alerts that are correct. Recall = TP/(TP+FN), the share of all positives caught. F1 is their harmonic mean.

Example. Fraud investigators with limited capacity may need high precision; a safety screen for a serious condition may prioritise recall so fewer true cases are missed.

Trade-off. Raising a threshold often increases precision and lowers recall. F1 treats precision and recall symmetrically and ignores true negatives and the unequal cost of errors; use F-beta or an explicit cost function when priorities differ.

ROC curve & AUC

Definition. A ROC curve plots true-positive rate against false-positive rate over thresholds. AUC summarises ranking ability; informally it is the chance a random positive receives a higher score than a random negative (with tie handling).

Example. Compare two fraud models across many thresholds before the bank decides how many alerts its team can review.

Trade-off. ROC/AUC can look strong when positives are extremely rare even if false alarms swamp operations. It ignores calibration and a chosen threshold. For severe imbalance, also inspect precision–recall and expected workload.

Precision–Recall curve—and when to prefer it to ROC

Definition. A PR curve plots precision against recall across thresholds. The baseline precision is approximately the positive-class prevalence.

Example. If only 1 in 1000 payments is fraudulent, the bank needs to know how many flagged transactions are actually fraud, not just how many genuine ones were correctly ignored.

Use it. PR is often more informative for a rare positive class and a limited investigation team. It depends on prevalence, so compare under the same population and report prevalence; ROC still offers a useful complementary view.

Log loss / cross-entropy

Definition. Log loss rewards probability assigned to the correct class and heavily penalises confident wrong probabilities. For a binary target, it is based on −[y log(p)+(1−y)log(1−p)].

Example. Saying “10% chance of default” and being right repeatedly is different from confidently claiming “0%” just before defaults occur; log loss notices that overconfidence.

Trade-off. Useful when probability quality matters, but sensitive to extreme wrong predictions and class imbalance. Clip numerical probabilities safely for computation and consider calibration; a low log loss is not automatically a good business decision.

Cohen's Kappa & Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC)

Definition. Cohen's Kappa measures agreement beyond chance expectation. MCC combines all four confusion-matrix counts into a balanced correlation-like score, often useful under imbalance.

Example. Compare a quality inspector's labels with an automated classifier when most products pass and a naïve agreement percentage would look high.

Trade-off. Both depend on class frequencies and can be less intuitive to non-technical audiences; explain the confusion matrix alongside the score. Handle zero denominators and define how multiclass averaging is done.

Ranking & recommender metrics: MAP and NDCG

Definition. Mean Average Precision (MAP) averages precision at the ranks where relevant results appear, across queries/users. Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain (NDCG) gives more credit to relevant results near the top and can use graded relevance.

Example. A learning app may care whether the most useful scholarship or lesson appears in the first few recommendations, not whether every possible item is classified.

Trade-off. Ranking scores depend on relevance labels, cut-off K, and candidate set. Offline metrics may miss user satisfaction, exposure fairness, and feedback loops; pair them with careful online tests and safety checks.

Choosing metrics for business problems

Definition. Translate the decision into error costs, capacity limits, and stakeholder outcomes, then select one primary metric plus guardrails.

Example. For 95%-genuine/5%-fraud payments, report PR-AUC, precision/recall at the review team's alert budget, fraud value caught, customer blocks, and performance across relevant groups—not only accuracy.

Rule. Ask “what is the costly mistake?”, “who bears it?”, “how many can operations handle?”, and “what baseline must we beat?” Make the threshold explicit and revisit it when prevalence or policy changes.

8Optimization & training

Training adjusts model parameters to reduce a loss. The path depends on how examples are sampled, the step size, the objective, and any constraint on complexity.

Gradient descent: batch, SGD & mini-batch

Definition. Gradient descent updates parameters opposite the gradient of a loss. Batch GD uses all training rows for each step; stochastic GD uses one row; mini-batch uses a small group.

Example. A large language model can estimate its next weight update from a mini-batch of text rather than recalculating over every sentence each time.

Trade-off. Batch gradients are stable but expensive per step; single-row SGD is noisy; mini-batch balances hardware efficiency and noise. The training loss may fluctuate. Normalise/scale features where appropriate and monitor held-out performance, not just gradient movement.

Optimizers: Momentum, RMSProp, Adam & AdamW

Definition. Momentum smooths gradients using a running direction. RMSProp adapts steps using recent squared gradients. Adam combines momentum and adaptive scaling. AdamW separates weight decay from Adam's gradient update.

Example. Imagine walking downhill: momentum avoids turning sharply at every pebble, while adaptive scaling adjusts step size by parameter history.

Trade-off. Adam is a strong deep-learning default, not a guarantee of best generalisation. Learning rate remains crucial. AdamW is commonly preferred when decoupled weight decay is intended; optimizer, decay, and schedule choices interact.

Learning rate & schedulers

Definition. Learning rate controls update size. A scheduler changes it during training: for example, step decay, cosine decay, warm-up, or reduction when validation stops improving.

Example. A huge step may jump past a good valley; a tiny step may crawl. Warm-up can avoid unstable early updates in a large transformer.

Check. If loss explodes, inspect learning rate and data/gradient bugs. If it moves very slowly, try a larger step or schedule. Tune using a learning-rate range test or validation; do not mistake a scheduler for a substitute for sound data.

Loss functions: MSE, cross-entropy, hinge & Huber

Definition. A loss scores prediction errors during training. MSE squares numeric errors; cross-entropy scores class probabilities; hinge loss encourages a classification margin; Huber behaves quadratically near zero and more linearly for large residuals.

Example. Predicting rupee sales may use MSE when big misses are costly; a noisy delivery-time dataset may benefit from Huber; a spam probability model usually uses cross-entropy.

Trade-off. The training loss should match the output and learning goal, but it need not be the same as the final business metric. Outliers, class weights, probability calibration, and label noise influence the choice.

Regularisation: L1, L2, dropout & early stopping

Definition. L1/L2 penalise large weights; dropout randomly disables units during training; early stopping halts when held-out validation performance stops improving.

Example. If a neural network memorises a small set of handwritten postal codes, regularisation discourages brittle, training-only solutions.

Trade-off. Too little can overfit; too much can underfit. Dropout is normally disabled at inference and its implementation rescales activations. Early stopping uses a validation set, so keep a separate test set for the final estimate.

Hyperparameter tuning: Grid, Random, Bayesian & Optuna

Definition. Hyperparameters are settings chosen outside ordinary parameter fitting, such as tree depth or learning rate. Grid search tries a fixed combination list; random search samples settings; Bayesian optimisation uses prior trials to suggest promising next trials. Optuna is a popular framework offering search, pruning, and study tracking.

Example. Search a boosted tree's depth, learning rate, and minimum leaf size using cross-validation, stopping clearly poor trials early.

Trade-off. Search only a sensible range, record seeds/data versions, and use the same validation protocol. Many trials overfit validation choices; nested CV or a final test helps estimate that selection effect. Bayesian search can struggle in noisy, changing objectives.

Convex vs non-convex optimization

Definition. A convex objective has no bad local minimum below a global minimum; non-convex objectives can have many valleys and saddle points.

Example. Ordinary least squares is convex in coefficients; a deep neural network's loss is generally non-convex in all its weights.

Trade-off. Convexity gives stronger guarantees about finding a global optimum, but does not guarantee data quality or useful predictions. Deep learning uses practical iterative methods and multiple design safeguards; convergence to a low training loss alone is not proof of good generalisation.

Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) vs MAP

Definition. MLE chooses parameters that make observed data most likely. Maximum a Posteriori (MAP) chooses parameters with the highest posterior probability after combining likelihood with a prior belief.

Example. Estimating a coin's bias from 8 heads in 10 tosses yields 0.8 by MLE; a prior favouring moderate biases can pull the MAP estimate away from an extreme based on a tiny sample.

Connection. Many regularisation penalties correspond to priors under specific assumptions. Priors express information, not objective truth; state the likelihood, prior, and data assumptions instead of treating MAP as automatically safer.

9Deep learning

Neural networks combine layers of simple mathematical units. More layers can learn useful representations, but require data, compute, careful training, and testing.

Perceptron & Multi-layer Perceptron (MLP)

Definition. A perceptron computes a weighted sum then applies an activation. An MLP stacks layers of such units; hidden layers let it represent nonlinear functions.

Example. An MLP can combine age, past payment delays, and loan amount into a default-risk score, learning interactions that a straight line may miss.

Trade-off. MLPs are flexible but need good data, scaling, tuning, and enough examples. A shallow network may be harder to explain than a simple linear model without earning better predictions.

Backpropagation & the chain rule

Definition. Backpropagation efficiently computes how each weight contributed to the final loss by applying the calculus chain rule from output layer back toward inputs.

Example. If a network predicts tomorrow's demand too high, gradients send each weight a signal about how changing it would reduce that error.

Trade-off. Backprop computes gradients; the optimizer uses them to update weights. It does not itself choose the learning rate or guarantee the model learned a meaningful pattern. Finite-difference checks can help verify a small custom implementation.

Activation functions: ReLU, Leaky ReLU, Sigmoid, Tanh, Softmax & GELU

Definition. Activations make networks nonlinear. ReLU keeps positive inputs; Leaky ReLU allows a small negative slope; sigmoid maps to 0–1; tanh to −1–1; softmax turns class logits into a probability distribution; GELU smoothly gates activations and is common in transformers.

Example. Use softmax for mutually exclusive categories like a message's main language, but a sigmoid per label for a message that can be both Hindi and English or both complaint and refund request.

Trade-off. Sigmoid/tanh can saturate; ReLU can have inactive units; softmax is not meaningful confidence unless calibrated. Match output activation and loss to whether labels are exclusive and whether the task is binary, multiclass, or multilabel.

Vanishing & exploding gradients

Definition. During backpropagation, repeated multiplications can make gradients shrink near zero (vanish) or grow extremely large (explode), especially in deep or recurrent networks.

Example. A sequence model may struggle to connect a clue at the start of a long customer message to its meaning at the end.

Response. Use suitable activations/initialisation, residual paths, normalisation, gated recurrent units, careful learning rates, and gradient clipping when appropriate. Diagnose with logged gradient norms; clipping limits explosions but cannot repair bad data or an unsuitable architecture.

Weight initialization: Xavier & He

Definition. Initialization sets starting weights so signal scale does not vanish or explode as it travels through layers. Xavier/Glorot is commonly paired with tanh-like activations; He initialization is designed for ReLU-like activations.

Example. If every neural connection starts with the same value, units learn identical behaviour; random, scale-aware starts help them specialise.

Trade-off. Initialisation supports stable optimisation but does not fix architecture or learning rate. Use the framework's recommended defaults unless there is a reason to customise; preserve and report seeds for reproducibility.

Batch Normalization vs Layer Normalization

Definition. BatchNorm normalises activations using statistics across a mini-batch (with running statistics for inference). LayerNorm normalises features within each example, independent of other examples in the batch.

Example. BatchNorm is common in convolutional image models; LayerNorm is common in transformers where batches or sequence lengths may vary.

Trade-off. BatchNorm can behave poorly with tiny or shifting batches and requires correct train/eval mode. LayerNorm avoids cross-example dependence but does not solve every optimisation issue. Neither is the same as scaling raw input features.

Dropout

Definition. Dropout randomly masks some activations while training, discouraging units from relying on one another too much. At inference, the full network is used with the framework's matching scaling convention.

Example. It is like practising a team presentation where any speaker may be absent: each member learns to carry more of the explanation.

Trade-off. Dropout can reduce overfitting, but too much slows learning or underfits. It is not automatically useful in every architecture; use the standard framework layer so training and inference scaling are consistent.

CNNs: convolution, pooling, stride & padding

Definition. A convolution applies a small shared filter across an image to detect local patterns. Stride controls movement between positions; padding adds border values to control output size; pooling downsamples nearby responses.

Example. A CNN may first detect edges in a photo of a damaged road, then combine them into cracks or potholes.

Trade-off. Local connections and shared filters are efficient for images and other grid-like signals, but can miss relationships far apart unless layers/receptive fields grow. Pooling reduces detail; padding/stride alter spatial dimensions and should be reported.

Famous CNN architectures: VGG, Inception, ResNet & EfficientNet

Definition. VGG used a simple stack of small convolutions; Inception processes multiple filter sizes in parallel; ResNet adds skip/residual connections to ease very deep training; EfficientNet scales depth, width, and image resolution in a coordinated way.

Example. Transfer a pretrained image network to classify plant leaf issues from locally collected photographs, then check it under real lighting and camera conditions.

Trade-off. Architecture names do not guarantee current best performance. Compare compute, latency, licence, data fit, and robustness; fine-tune only after establishing a simple baseline.

RNN, LSTM & GRU

Definition. Recurrent Neural Networks process sequence steps while carrying a hidden state. LSTM and GRU add gates to control what information to keep or forget, helping with longer dependencies.

Example. A text model reads a customer complaint word by word; an electricity model reads past hourly demand to forecast the next hour.

Trade-off. RNNs handle variable sequences but process steps sequentially, making them harder to parallelise and long memory difficult. Transformers handle many language tasks well, but may use much more memory and data; use a simpler sequence model when it meets requirements.

Transfer learning & fine-tuning

Definition. Transfer learning reuses representations learned on a source task. Fine-tuning continues training some or all pretrained weights on target data; feature extraction freezes the base and trains a new head.

Example. Start with a network trained on many images, then adapt it to classify locally photographed crop disease leaves with a smaller labelled set.

Trade-off. Pretraining can save data and compute, but source-target mismatch can hurt. Keep a clean target test set, compare frozen and fine-tuned versions, and check licensing, privacy, and demographic/environmental coverage.

Autoencoders & Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

Definition. An autoencoder compresses an input into a latent code and learns to reconstruct it. A VAE learns a probability distribution over latent codes with a reconstruction term and a regularising KL-divergence term, enabling sampling.

Example. Train on normal machine sensor patterns and investigate samples with large reconstruction error; a VAE can also generate plausible variations from its latent space.

Trade-off. Reconstruction error is not a reliable anomaly score for every anomaly, and a VAE's outputs can be blurrier than originals. Inspect reconstructions and latent assumptions; generated examples are not guaranteed to be safe or faithful.

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Definition. A GAN pits a generator, which creates samples, against a discriminator, which distinguishes generated samples from real training data. Both improve through an adversarial training game.

Example. A research team could create synthetic images of road damage to augment rare training examples, then verify that the artificial images really preserve labels and do not leak private originals.

Trade-off. GAN training can be unstable or collapse to a narrow set of outputs. Synthetic data can reproduce bias or private details; it is not a substitute for representative real-world testing.

10NLP & transformers

Language systems turn text into tokens and vectors, then use patterns and context to classify, compare, or generate sequences. Indian languages make tokenisation and evaluation especially important.

Tokenization: BPE & WordPiece

Definition. A tokenizer maps text into model vocabulary units. Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) repeatedly merges frequent symbol pairs; WordPiece chooses subword pieces according to a learned vocabulary objective. A word may become one token or several pieces.

Example. A Hindi-English Hinglish sentence, a Tamil suffix, or a newly written place name may split differently from English training text; token count affects context length and cost.

Trade-off. Subwords handle rare words better than a word-only dictionary but may fragment low-resource scripts. Normalisation, Unicode, punctuation, and transliteration matter. Never assume one token equals one word, especially across Indian languages.

TF-IDF & Bag of Words

Definition. Bag of Words counts tokens while ignoring order. TF-IDF downweights terms common in many documents and gives more weight to terms distinctive to a document.

Example. A Hindi-English complaint classifier can use TF-IDF features to learn that “refund,” “paisa,” or their character fragments are associated with a refund request.

Trade-off. Fast and interpretable, but word order and deep meaning are mostly lost. Word and character n-grams can help with spelling variation; TF-IDF is often a strong baseline before costly transformer fine-tuning.

Word embeddings: Word2Vec, GloVe & FastText

Definition. Embeddings represent tokens as vectors. Word2Vec learns from local context prediction; GloVe uses global co-occurrence statistics; FastText includes character n-grams, helping represent unseen or morphologically related words.

Example. FastText can share some subword information between related Hindi word forms even when a complete word is rare.

Trade-off. Traditional word embeddings give one vector per word, so “bank” may have one mixed meaning and bias can be encoded in geometry. Contextual transformer embeddings change with surrounding words but cost more to run.

Attention & self-attention

Definition. Attention computes how strongly one representation should use information from others. In self-attention, tokens in the same sequence form queries, keys, and values; similarity scores weight a mixture of values.

Example. In “The farmer stored the grain after it dried,” the model can use attention to connect “it” with the grain or crop context rather than treating each word alone.

Trade-off. Attention makes contextual relationships flexible and parallelisable, but standard full self-attention grows roughly quadratically with sequence length. Attention maps are not automatically faithful explanations of a model's reasoning.

Transformer architecture: encoder, decoder & positional encoding

Definition. A transformer uses attention and feed-forward blocks. Encoders can attend bidirectionally across input tokens. Autoregressive decoders predict next tokens using a causal mask. Positional encodings inject token order, since attention alone does not know which word came first.

Example. For translation, an encoder may read a full source sentence, while a decoder generates the target sentence a token at a time and can attend to the encoded source.

What if attention is removed? The core mechanism for content-dependent token interactions is gone. Feed-forward layers still process tokens, and alternatives can mix information, but the standard transformer loses its main way of connecting distant contextual clues. Positional information alone cannot replace token-to-token interaction.

BERT vs GPT: encoder vs decoder

Definition. BERT-style encoders use bidirectional context and are commonly pretrained by predicting masked tokens, then adapted for understanding tasks. GPT-style decoder-only models use causal attention and next-token prediction, making them natural autoregressive generators.

Example. BERT-like models can classify a full support message using words on both sides; GPT-like models can draft a reply one token at a time.

Trade-off. These are common architecture/training patterns, not absolute limits: encoder-decoder models also exist, and either family can be adapted. Choose by task, latency, data, context, and desired output; generation ability does not guarantee factual understanding.

Perplexity, BLEU & ROUGE

Definition. Perplexity measures how surprised a language model is by a sequence under its predicted probabilities; lower is usually better on comparable text/tokenisation. BLEU compares generated text with reference n-gram overlap, commonly for translation. ROUGE measures reference overlap/coverage, often for summarisation.

Example. Evaluate a Marathi translation model with automatic scores plus bilingual human checks for meaning, politeness, and script handling.

Trade-off. Perplexity is not a direct measure of helpfulness. BLEU/ROUGE can penalise valid paraphrases and miss factual errors. Report dataset, tokeniser, reference process, and human/task evaluation alongside them.

11Generative AI & large language models

An LLM predicts text from context. Useful systems surround it with reliable information, constrained tools, safety checks, and evaluation—not just a clever prompt.

Prompt engineering: zero-shot, few-shot & chain-of-thought

Definition. Prompting specifies the task, context, constraints, and output format. Zero-shot gives instructions without examples; few-shot includes demonstrations. Chain-of-thought prompting asks for intermediate reasoning, though exposed reasoning may be unreliable or inappropriate.

Example. Ask for a concise bilingual customer-service draft with a JSON schema, then provide two examples of the approved tone and an instruction to cite supplied policy text.

Trade-off. Better prompts can improve consistency but cannot create missing knowledge or guarantee truth. Prefer clear task boundaries and verifiable outputs; do not request or trust hidden reasoning as proof. Validate structured outputs and protect against prompt injection.

RAG: Retrieval-Augmented Generation & its pipeline

Definition. RAG retrieves relevant external passages and places them in the model's context before generation. Typical pipeline: ingest and clean trusted documents → chunk → embed/index → retrieve → optionally rerank → assemble grounded prompt → generate citations → evaluate and monitor.

Example. A scholarship assistant retrieves current official eligibility rules and deadlines before answering a student's question, with source links for checking.

Trade-off. RAG can keep knowledge fresher and make sources inspectable, but bad retrieval, stale files, missing pages, or prompt injection can still yield wrong answers. Test retrieval recall and answer faithfulness separately; adding documents alone does not ensure grounding.

Embeddings & vector databases: FAISS, Pinecone & Chroma

Definition. An embedding maps text or another item to a numeric vector where useful similarities may be nearby. A vector index searches for nearby vectors. FAISS is a similarity-search library; Pinecone is a managed vector database; Chroma is an open-source vector database/tooling option.

Example. Embed a question about a ration card and retrieve semantically similar paragraphs from verified government guidance, even if the wording differs.

Trade-off. Similarity is not the same as truth or relevance. Filter by metadata/permissions, keep embedding model and index versions aligned, test Hindi and regional-language retrieval, and protect personally identifiable information.

Chunking strategies

Definition. Chunking splits documents into retrievable pieces. Fixed-size chunks are simple; sentence/paragraph chunks respect boundaries; semantic chunks follow topic shifts; overlap preserves context across chunk edges; parent-child retrieval searches small passages then returns larger context.

Example. A government circular's eligibility condition and exception should stay together; splitting after “unless” can reverse the meaning of a retrieved fragment.

Trade-off. Large chunks dilute search; tiny chunks lose context and may omit the clause that qualifies an answer. Tune size/overlap against retrieval tests and citations, not a universal token-count rule.

Fine-tuning vs RAG vs prompting

Definition. Prompting changes task instructions and examples at inference. RAG supplies changing external knowledge at inference. Fine-tuning updates model behaviour/weights using examples.

Example. Use a prompt to format a reply, RAG to look up today's train policy, and fine-tuning to teach a consistent domain style or task pattern when enough governed examples exist.

Decision. Use the simplest tool that solves the measured gap. Fine-tuning is not a reliable substitute for a frequently changing facts database; RAG does not automatically teach a new response style; prompts can hit context limits. Combinations need separate evaluation and versioning.

LoRA / QLoRA / PEFT

Definition. PEFT (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) adapts a model while training only a small set of parameters. LoRA adds trainable low-rank update matrices to selected weights. QLoRA loads a quantised base model and trains LoRA adapters to reduce memory needs.

Example. Adapt an open model to label customer tickets in a consistent schema without storing a complete new copy of every model weight.

Trade-off. Less memory and storage can make adaptation practical, but does not remove the need for clean representative data, evaluation, or licence review. Quantised training and merging adapters have implementation-specific precision/compatibility issues.

RLHF & DPO

Definition. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) uses human preferences to train a reward model and then optimise a policy, often with reinforcement learning. DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) trains directly on preferred-versus-rejected response pairs using a reference policy objective.

Example. Human reviewers can compare two answers to a benefits question for helpfulness, accuracy, and respectful language, creating preference data for alignment work.

Trade-off. Preferences vary and reward models can be gamed; DPO simplifies a training path but still depends on high-quality preference pairs and a suitable reference. Neither method ensures truth, fairness, or policy compliance by itself.

Hallucinations & mitigation

Definition. A hallucination is a plausible-sounding output that is unsupported or false. Fluent language generation is not the same as checking facts against reality.

Example. A chatbot inventing a government helpline number could cause real harm. A safe system should link to verified current sources or say it cannot confirm the number.

Mitigate. Retrieve trusted material, require citations that are checked against passages, use constrained tools for facts, set abstention/escalation paths, and test adversarial and out-of-date questions. RAG can reduce some errors, not eliminate them.

Temperature, top-k & top-p sampling

Definition. Temperature changes how sharply token probabilities are used (lower is more concentrated). Top-k samples only among the k most likely next tokens. Top-p (nucleus sampling) samples from the smallest set whose cumulative probability reaches p.

Example. A factual extraction task often needs more repeatable output than a creative story-writing task; both should still be validated.

Trade-off. Lower temperature reduces variety, not factual error. Top-k and top-p alter diversity, not underlying knowledge. Exact controls differ by provider/model; record settings for reproducibility and do not set randomness high for a high-stakes answer.

Context window & KV cache

Definition. A context window is the maximum input-plus-output token span a model can process at once. A key-value cache stores attention keys and values from earlier tokens so autoregressive generation need not recompute them at every step.

Example. A long case file may exceed the window; summarising or retrieving relevant paragraphs can help. During token-by-token generation, cached previous states speed up later steps.

Trade-off. Bigger context does not guarantee every detail is used accurately and can increase cost/latency. KV cache consumes memory as sequence length and batch size grow; manage truncation, privacy, and retrieval explicitly.

AI agents & tool use

Definition. An agent uses a model to choose actions—such as calling a search, calculator, database, or booking API—then uses tool results to continue toward a goal.

Example. A travel helper may check official train availability and ask before booking; the model should not invent a successful booking confirmation.

Trade-off. Tools add current data and exact operations but expand security and failure risks. Restrict permissions, validate arguments, isolate untrusted inputs, require confirmation for consequential actions, set budgets/timeouts, and log auditable results. More autonomy is not automatically better.

LLM evaluation: benchmarks & LLM-as-judge

Definition. Evaluation combines task-specific test sets, human review, safety tests, operational metrics, and sometimes public benchmarks. LLM-as-judge uses another model to score or compare outputs against a rubric.

Example. Evaluate an Indian-language public-service assistant on factual correctness, citation support, language quality, accessibility, refusal cases, and answer time—not only an English benchmark.

Trade-off. Benchmarks can be contaminated or unlike local users. A judge model may prefer its own style, miss factual errors, or be sensitive to answer order. Calibrate against human raters, blind identities, use explicit rubrics, and report uncertainty and subgroup results.

Quantization & model distillation

Definition. Quantization stores or computes weights/activations at lower numerical precision to save memory and speed inference. Distillation trains a smaller student model to imitate a larger teacher's outputs or behaviour.

Example. A smaller model may run an offline FAQ assistant on a low-cost device; a distilled model can approximate the teacher for common questions.

Trade-off. Lower precision can hurt rare-language or safety performance. A smaller student may fail on difficult cases. Benchmark accuracy, calibration, latency, memory, subgroup quality, and abstention after compression—not just model size.

12Probability & statistics

Statistics helps us reason about uncertainty: how evidence changes belief, what patterns repeat across samples, and how noisy measurements affect conclusions.

Bayes' theorem

Definition. Bayes' theorem updates a prior belief using observed evidence: P(A|B)=P(B|A)P(A)/P(B). The base rate matters.

Example. Suppose a hypothetical screening test catches 90% of a condition and falsely flags 5% of people without it. If the condition affects 1 in 1000 people, most positive tests can still be false positives: among 1000, about 0.9 true positives and about 50 false positives are expected.

Lesson. “The test is 90% accurate” is not enough to answer “What is the chance I have it after a positive result?” Ask for prevalence, sensitivity, and specificity. The numbers above are teaching assumptions, not advice about a real medical test.

Central Limit Theorem (CLT)

Definition. Under suitable independence and finite-variance conditions, the distribution of a properly standardised sample mean approaches a normal shape as sample size grows—even if individual measurements are not normally distributed.

Example. Daily grocery bills can be skewed, but averages from many comparable random groups of shoppers can be more bell-shaped than individual bills.

Trade-off. CLT does not say all raw data become normal, and it can fail or converge slowly for dependent, heavy-tailed, or biased samples. “Large n” is not a magic fixed number; study sampling design and outliers.

Hypothesis testing, p-value & Type I / II errors

Definition. A test starts with a null hypothesis and asks whether data would be surprising under it. A p-value is the probability, assuming the null and model assumptions, of results at least as extreme as observed. Type I error is a false positive; Type II is a missed effect.

Example. Test whether a new study reminder changes course completion, predefining the outcome, analysis, and acceptable false-alarm rate before seeing results.

Trade-off. A p-value is not the probability the null is true, nor effect size or business value. Repeated peeking, many uncorrected tests, and selective reporting inflate false discoveries. Report confidence intervals and practical impact too.

Confidence intervals

Definition. A 95% confidence-interval procedure would cover the true fixed parameter in about 95% of repeated samples under its assumptions. A computed interval expresses uncertainty in the estimate.

Example. If an A/B experiment estimates a new checkout increases completion by 2 percentage points, the interval shows a range of effects consistent with the sampled data and method.

Trade-off. It does not mean there is a 95% probability the fixed parameter lies in this particular frequentist interval. Small or biased samples yield unreliable intervals; report units, method, and assumptions.

A/B testing

Definition. An A/B test randomly assigns eligible units to a control and a treatment, then compares a predefined outcome.

Example. Randomly show half of eligible app users a revised UPI reminder and compare completed payments, while monitoring failed transactions and complaints.

Trade-off. Define randomisation unit, primary metric, sample size, duration, exclusions, and guardrails before launch. Beware users influencing each other, seasonal effects, novelty, multiple metrics, and stopping early. Statistical significance does not guarantee worthwhile impact or equitable outcomes.

Common distributions: Normal, Binomial, Poisson & Exponential

Definition. Normal models symmetric continuous variation; Binomial counts successes in a fixed number of independent trials with the same success chance; Poisson models event counts in a fixed interval under a roughly stable rate; Exponential models waiting times between Poisson-like events.

Example. A normal model may describe small measurement noise; a Binomial can model correct answers in 20 questions; Poisson can approximate incoming calls per minute; Exponential can model time between calls.

Trade-off. These are assumptions, not labels every dataset must fit. Independence, constant rates, and tail shape matter; overdispersion or clustering can break simple models. Plot data and check domain assumptions.

Covariance vs correlation

Definition. Covariance indicates whether two quantities tend to move together, in units tied to their scales. Correlation standardises covariance to a range from −1 to 1.

Example. Umbrella sales and rainy days may be positively correlated across weeks; the association alone does not prove umbrellas caused rain or capture every seasonal factor.

Trade-off. Pearson correlation describes linear association and is sensitive to outliers; zero correlation does not rule out a nonlinear relation. Correlation does not establish causation, and spurious associations can result from season or a common cause.

Entropy, KL divergence & mutual information

Definition. Entropy measures uncertainty in a distribution. KL divergence measures how one probability distribution differs from a reference (it is asymmetric and not a true distance). Mutual information measures how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about another.

Example. A decision tree uses entropy reduction to prefer a split that makes labels less mixed; mutual information can score how informative a feature is about a target.

Trade-off. These depend on probability estimates and sample size. KL is not symmetric; small probabilities need numerical care. High mutual information can reflect leakage, confounding, or a feature unavailable at decision time.

13Linear algebra & math

Linear algebra is the language for representing many features, transformations, and relationships at once. You can understand the role of the math before deriving every formula.

Eigenvalues & eigenvectors

Definition. For a square matrix A, an eigenvector v keeps its direction under transformation: Av = λv. The eigenvalue λ is how much it is stretched or flipped.

Example. A covariance matrix's eigenvectors give principal directions in the data; eigenvalues say how much variance lies along each direction.

Trade-off. Eigenvectors help reveal important directions for suitable matrices, but sign and scaling conventions can vary. A large eigenvalue describes variance direction, not automatically predictive or causal importance.

Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Definition. SVD factors a matrix as UΣVᵀ: left directions, non-negative singular values, and right directions. Truncating small singular values gives a lower-rank approximation.

Example. A user–movie rating table with many blanks can be approximated by a smaller set of latent preference patterns.

Trade-off. SVD is a powerful building block for compression and factorisation, but full decompositions can be costly for huge matrices. Low-rank structure is an assumption; missing ratings are not automatically zero-valued ratings.

Matrix calculus, Jacobian & Hessian

Definition. The Jacobian collects first derivatives of a vector output with respect to vector inputs. The Hessian collects second derivatives of a scalar function and describes local curvature. Matrix calculus keeps many related gradients organised.

Example. Backpropagation applies chain rules through layers; a Jacobian describes how output probabilities change when inputs change.

Trade-off. These objects can be enormous, so software uses vector-Jacobian products instead of storing every element. Check dimensions and transposes carefully; notation conventions differ across textbooks.

Norms: L1 & L2

Definition. A norm measures vector size. L1 sums absolute values, Σ|xᵢ|; L2 is the square root of summed squares, √Σxᵢ².

Example. In regression, L1 regularisation can create exact zero coefficients, while L2 regularisation smoothly shrinks weights. Geometrically, distances can describe how different two feature vectors are.

Trade-off. Norm choice changes optimisation and what “small” means. Scale features when comparing distances; do not assume a sparse L1 solution is more truthful or interpretable without checking correlated inputs.

14Time series & recommenders

Time and preference data need special care: order matters, future information must stay in the future, and missing user-item pairs are not necessarily dislikes.

Stationarity & Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test

Definition. A stationary series has stable statistical properties such as mean and variance over time. The ADF test evaluates a unit-root null against stationarity under a chosen model specification.

Example. Daily vegetable prices may trend and change seasonally; a short-run deviation around a stable level is different from a changing long-term price process.

Trade-off. ADF failing to reject a unit root does not prove a series is non-stationary; results depend on lag and trend settings and test power. Plot the series, understand breaks and seasonality, and avoid treating one p-value as a complete diagnosis.

ARIMA / SARIMA

Definition. ARIMA models autoregression (past values), differencing (to reduce trend/non-stationarity), and moving-average error terms. SARIMA adds seasonal structure with period-specific terms.

Example. Forecast monthly electricity use with a yearly season for hot summers, using only data available before each forecast date.

Trade-off. Interpretable classical baselines can work on modest univariate data, but struggle with complex external drivers or abrupt regime changes. Select orders using rolling validation and compare with seasonal-naive forecasts; avoid random train/test shuffles.

Seasonality, trend & decomposition

Definition. A time series can be viewed as trend (long-run movement), seasonal pattern (regular repeated cycle), and residual remainder. Decomposition can be additive or multiplicative depending on whether seasonal size stays constant or scales with the series.

Example. Retail orders may trend upward, rise around Diwali each year, and still include unpredictable daily variation.

Trade-off. Decomposition is descriptive and can be misleading with too little history, changing seasons, missing dates, or structural breaks. Align calendar frequency, holidays, and local events; validate forecasts on future time windows.

Collaborative vs content-based filtering

Definition. Collaborative filtering recommends items using patterns across users' interactions. Content-based filtering uses item attributes and an individual user's history or stated interests.

Example. A learning app might recommend a course because similar learners completed it (collaborative) or because its language and subject match a learner's preferences (content-based).

Trade-off. Collaborative methods can discover unexpected choices but need interaction data and face cold starts. Content-based methods work for new items with metadata but can over-personalise into the same narrow category.

Matrix factorization

Definition. Matrix factorisation approximates a user-item interaction matrix as lower-dimensional user and item vectors whose dot product predicts preference or rating.

Example. A film recommender can learn latent taste dimensions from past ratings and rank movies for each user.

Trade-off. It handles sparse matrices efficiently and can discover latent structure, but needs interactions and is less directly interpretable. Implicit clicks are not explicit likes; account for exposure bias and evaluate with time-aware ranking metrics.

Cold-start problem

Definition. Cold start is difficulty recommending for new users, new items, or an entirely new system with little interaction history.

Example. A new student has not watched lessons yet, so a course app can ask subject/language preferences and show popular, diverse starter lessons.

Response. Use onboarding, item content, contextual/popularity baselines, and safe exploration. Popularity can reinforce majority exposure; monitor who receives visibility and provide ways to update preferences.

15MLOps & production

A model is only one component of a working product. Production means repeatable data, reliable serving, clear ownership, monitoring, and a safe response when conditions change.

Deployment: REST API, FastAPI & Docker

Definition. Deployment makes a model usable by another system. A REST API exposes request/response endpoints; FastAPI is a Python framework for building typed APIs; Docker packages an application and its dependencies into a container image.

Example. A delivery service sends parcel details to a prediction endpoint and receives an estimated delay plus model version.

Trade-off. Validate inputs, handle timeouts/errors explicitly, authenticate access, rate-limit, protect personal data, and version schemas. Containers improve repeatability but do not solve orchestration, secrets management, or monitoring alone. Test latency at realistic traffic.

Data drift & concept drift

Definition. Data drift means the input distribution changes. Concept drift means the relationship between inputs and target changes, so the old mapping becomes less reliable.

Example. A change in smartphone adoption may alter who uses a payment service (data drift); a new fraud tactic may change which transaction patterns signal fraud (concept drift).

Trade-off. A shift is a warning, not proof performance fell; performance labels may arrive late. Monitor input, prediction, and eventual outcomes, investigate causes, and use safe retraining tests rather than automatically retraining on unverified data.

Model monitoring

Definition. Monitoring tracks technical health and model/product behaviour: latency, errors, missingness, data distributions, score distributions, outcome metrics, and fairness or safety guardrails.

Example. A loan model dashboard could flag a spike in missing income values and a drop in approval rates for a monitored segment while labels mature.

Trade-off. Alerts need owners, thresholds, investigation playbooks, and rollback plans. Aggregate averages can hide subgroup failures; track label delay and false-alarm rates so responders are not overwhelmed.

Feature stores

Definition. A feature store manages reusable model inputs, often with offline historical values for training and online low-latency values for serving.

Example. A bank's “transactions in last 24 hours” feature should be computed consistently in historical training data and real-time scoring.

Trade-off. Stores can reduce training-serving skew and duplication, but add infrastructure and governance. Enforce point-in-time correctness, freshness, access controls, lineage, and clear feature definitions; a store cannot fix a leaked or poorly designed feature.

CI/CD for ML: MLflow & DVC

Definition. CI/CD automates tests and controlled delivery. MLflow tracks experiments, parameters, metrics, and models. DVC versions data/model pipeline artifacts alongside code workflows.

Example. A pipeline can verify a new model against quality thresholds, data checks, and latency constraints before registering a release candidate.

Trade-off. Reproducibility needs code, data, environment, feature, and model versioning—not only a metric screenshot. Validate licensing and access, secure artifacts, and preserve a rollback path.

Batch vs real-time inference

Definition. Batch inference scores many records on a schedule. Real-time inference scores a request on demand under a latency target.

Example. A nightly batch can recommend tomorrow's practice lessons; a payment authorisation service may need a fraud score in milliseconds.

Trade-off. Batch is efficient and easier to operate but may be stale; real-time is fresh but needs availability, low latency, and failure handling. Choose based on decision timing, cost, and safe fallback—not novelty.

Explainability: SHAP & LIME

Definition. SHAP assigns feature contributions to a prediction using Shapley-value ideas; LIME fits a simple local approximation around one example to describe behaviour near it.

Example. A loan officer can inspect which input factors raised a model score before reviewing the application under policy.

Trade-off. Explanations are approximations, depend on feature background and correlations, and may not reveal causation. Test stability and whether the explanation is actionable; never present a feature attribution as the reason a person deserved an outcome.

Fairness & bias in ML

Definition. Bias can enter through historical labels, sampling, measurement, features, objectives, or deployment. Fairness describes desired treatment or outcomes across groups, but different fairness definitions can conflict.

Example. A hiring screener trained on past hires may reproduce unequal opportunity. Audit who is represented, false rejection rates by relevant groups, accessibility, and whether each input is justified.

Trade-off. No single score proves a system fair. Work with affected communities and domain experts, respect Indian data-protection and sector rules, document intended use, provide appeals/human review, and monitor after launch. Removing a sensitive column alone does not remove proxy bias.

Frequently asked in Indian ML interviews

Practice each answer in your own words. Say the core contrast first, then add a practical consideration.

“Explain how Random Forest differs from Gradient Boosting.”

Random Forest uses bagging: many trees are trained mostly independently on bootstrap samples with random feature choices, then averaged. This mainly reduces variance and is robust. Gradient Boosting grows trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the current loss's errors. It can be very accurate but is more sensitive to tuning/noisy labels. Compare on the same leakage-safe validation split.

“How would you handle a dataset with 95% class imbalance?”

First learn the business cost and verify labels and split. Accuracy is not enough. Set a majority baseline; use stratified (or time/group-aware) validation, class weights or training-only resampling such as SMOTE, and tune the threshold to capacity/cost. Report precision, recall, PR-AUC and subgroup results; inspect false alarms and missed cases.

“Why does logistic regression use log loss rather than MSE?”

Logistic regression predicts probabilities through a sigmoid. Cross-entropy is the negative log-likelihood for a Bernoulli outcome: it matches the probability model and penalises confident wrong predictions strongly. With a sigmoid, MSE can give weak gradients when predictions saturate. MSE can be optimised, but is usually a less suitable objective for probability classification.

“Explain the bias–variance trade-off with an example.”

A straight line predicting monsoon crop yield from rainfall alone may be too simple: high bias. A huge tree memorising every farm and season may change sharply on new farms: high variance. Compare held-out scores across complexity; choose enough flexibility to learn the signal without memorising noise.

“What happens if you remove attention from a Transformer?”

Standard self-attention lets each token build a context-dependent mixture of other tokens. Without it, ordinary feed-forward blocks process token positions independently; token communication is lost unless another mixing mechanism replaces it. Positional encoding says where tokens are, but alone does not let them exchange content.

“How do you debug high training accuracy but poor test accuracy?”

Check the split and leakage first, then confirm test data reflects the real future population. Compare learning curves; audit duplicates, label quality, preprocessing, class balance, and drift. Reduce complexity or regularise, add representative data, use group/time-aware validation, and report relevant metrics. Do not tune repeatedly on the test set.

Walk through an ML project end to end

A strong project answer explains how you made decisions and measured impact—not just which algorithm you used.

  1. Frame the decision. Who will use the prediction, at what moment, and what action follows? Define success, cost of mistakes, latency, and constraints.
  2. Understand the data. Map sources, owners, time coverage, labels, missingness, class rates, permissions, and potential historical bias. Check whether features exist at prediction time.
  3. Choose a realistic evaluation design. Split by time, customer, household, or location when deployment demands it. Keep the test set untouched; establish simple baselines.
  4. Build a reproducible pipeline. Create train-only preprocessing, feature engineering, model fitting, and validation. Track versions and random seeds. Avoid leakage in cross-validation.
  5. Compare and select. Tune a small set of plausible models against the business metric and guardrails. Inspect errors, calibration, subgroup behaviour, and operational capacity.
  6. Deploy safely. Package model and preprocessing together; version inputs/outputs; test APIs, latency, access control, fallbacks, and rollback. Start with a controlled release if possible.
  7. Monitor and learn. Track technical health, data and concept drift, delayed outcome quality, complaints, fairness and cost. Assign alert owners and a retraining/retirement process.
Interview finish: state the measured outcome and limitations honestly. If you do not have a deployment result, distinguish offline validation from real-world impact.

Study tip: learn the idea, not just the name

For each concept, practise four short parts: one-line definition, real example, trade-off, and when you would not use it. Then teach it aloud to someone who has not studied machine learning.

Build intuition in this order

Start with data splits, leakage, metrics, and bias/variance. Then learn a simple baseline for regression and classification, tree methods, and ensembles. Move to clustering and dimensionality reduction, then neural networks, transformers, and LLM applications. Statistics and linear algebra should support the questions you encounter.

Code small cores from scratch

Implement linear regression, K-Means, KNN, logistic regression, and a decision-tree split in NumPy. Compare each with a trusted library, test edge cases, and write down what assumptions your short implementation leaves out.

Remember: an algorithm is a tool, not a result. A strong ML practitioner can explain what the data says, what it cannot say, which mistake matters, and how the system will be checked after release.

AAcronyms & technical-name glossary

Use this as a lookup section for the shortened names in the guide. Each entry gives the expanded words where there is a standard expansion, then explains in plain language what the term means and where it appears. Some names are product or method names rather than true acronyms; those are identified too.

Names can have more than one meaning. In this guide, MAP can mean Mean Average Precision (ranking) or Maximum a Posteriori (statistics), and LDA can mean Linear Discriminant Analysis or Latent Dirichlet Allocation. Use the surrounding topic to tell which is meant. CI can mean confidence interval or continuous integration.

A/B — control and treatment comparison

A/B testing compares two versions, usually by randomly assigning eligible people or items to a control (A) or treatment (B). The slash is not an expansion: A and B are simply the names of the two groups.

ADF — Augmented Dickey–Fuller

A statistical test used in time-series analysis to look for evidence against a unit-root process. In simple terms, it helps assess whether a series behaves as if shocks persist indefinitely or tends to return toward a stable pattern; its result depends on modelling choices and is not a complete stationarity diagnosis.

AI — Artificial Intelligence

The broad field of making computer systems perform tasks associated with intelligent behaviour, such as recognising images, planning, or understanding language. Machine learning is one approach within AI: systems learn patterns from data rather than relying only on hand-written rules.

AdaBoost — Adaptive Boosting

A boosting algorithm that builds learners in sequence and gives more emphasis to examples earlier learners got wrong. “Adaptive” describes this shifting emphasis; noisy or incorrectly labelled examples can therefore become a problem.

Adam — Adaptive Moment Estimation

A neural-network optimizer that tracks averages of gradients and squared gradients to adapt update sizes. The “moments” are running statistics, not a guarantee of better results; learning rate and regularisation still matter.

AdamW — Adam with decoupled weight decay

A version of Adam that applies weight decay separately from Adam's gradient-based update. This distinction makes the intended parameter shrinkage behave more reliably than simply adding an L2 penalty in many common Adam setups.

API — Application Programming Interface

A defined way for software systems to request and exchange information or actions. A model API might accept a JSON request with transaction details and return a risk score; the interface should validate inputs, handle errors, and protect access.

ARIMA — Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

A family of time-series forecasting models. “Autoregressive” uses earlier values, “integrated” refers to differencing the series, and “moving average” uses past forecast errors; SARIMA adds seasonal terms.

ATM — Automated Teller Machine

A cash machine used for withdrawals and other banking tasks. In the anomaly-detection example, unusual ATM activity is flagged for review; unusual does not automatically mean fraudulent.

AUC — Area Under the Curve

A general phrase for the area under a plotted curve. In classification, it usually refers to ROC-AUC: the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve, a summary of how well a model ranks positive cases above negative ones across thresholds.

BatchNorm — Batch Normalization

A neural-network layer that normalizes activations using statistics from a mini-batch during training, then uses stored running statistics at inference. Unlike LayerNorm, its behaviour depends on the batch, so small or changing batches need care.

BERT — Bidirectional Encoder Representations from Transformers

An encoder-style language model whose representations use context from both directions around a token. It is commonly pretrained by predicting masked tokens and then adapted for understanding tasks such as classification; the name describes the model family, not a guarantee of factual correctness.

BLEU — Bilingual Evaluation Understudy

An automatic language-generation score, first popularized for machine translation, that compares word or token n-gram overlap with reference translations. It is useful for consistent comparisons, but a good paraphrase can score poorly and a high score does not prove meaning is correct.

BPE — Byte Pair Encoding

A subword tokenization method that repeatedly merges frequent adjacent symbol pairs to build a vocabulary. It lets a language model represent unfamiliar words as smaller pieces instead of needing a separate token for every possible word.

CatBoost — Categorical Boosting

The name of a gradient-boosted decision-tree library, formed from “categorical” and “boosting.” It includes methods designed to work conveniently with categorical columns; users still need leakage-safe validation and suitable tuning.

Chart.js — JavaScript chart library

A library for making interactive charts in web pages. “JS” is short for JavaScript, the programming language used for the guide's charts; the chart explanations remain in the page if the library cannot load.

CD — Continuous Delivery / Continuous Deployment

In CI/CD, CD means automating the path from tested changes toward release. Continuous delivery keeps a verified release ready for approval; continuous deployment automatically releases changes that pass the pipeline. Teams use the term differently, so clarify the intended level of automation.

CI — Confidence Interval

In statistics, an interval produced by a procedure that would cover a fixed true value at a stated rate over repeated samples (for example, 95%). It describes uncertainty in an estimate under assumptions; it is not a promise that this particular frequentist interval has a 95% probability of containing the fixed value.

CI — Continuous Integration

In CI/CD, automatically building and testing changes as they are added to a shared project. It can catch broken code, data checks, and model-quality regressions before a model is released. Context distinguishes this meaning from confidence interval.

CLT — Central Limit Theorem

A result explaining why averages from many suitable independent samples tend toward a normal-shaped sampling distribution as sample size grows. It concerns the distribution of sample averages, not a claim that the original measurements become normally distributed.

CNN — Convolutional Neural Network

A neural network that uses shared filters to detect local patterns, especially in images. Early filters may respond to edges; later layers can combine them into parts and objects. CNNs are useful for grid-like data but do not automatically understand what an image depicts.

CV — Cross-Validation

A method for estimating how a model may perform on unseen data by repeatedly fitting on some folds and checking on a held-out fold. The split must match the task: random folds can leak future information in a time-series prediction problem.

DBSCAN — Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise

A clustering algorithm that groups points in dense neighbourhoods and marks sparse points as noise. Despite “spatial” in its original name, it can be used with other features too; feature scaling and the choice of neighbourhood settings strongly affect results.

DPO — Direct Preference Optimization

A method for adapting a language model from pairs of preferred and less-preferred answers, without the separate reward-model-plus-reinforcement-learning loop used in many RLHF pipelines. It still depends on representative, carefully judged preference examples.

DVC — Data Version Control

A tool and workflow for tracking data, models, and reproducible machine-learning pipelines alongside code. DVC helps teams identify which inputs and steps produced an artifact; access control and sensitive-data handling remain the team's responsibility.

EM — Expectation-Maximization

An iterative method used when a model includes hidden or incomplete information. It alternates an expectation step, estimating hidden memberships using current parameters, with a maximization step, updating parameters from those estimates; it can settle at a local solution.

E-step / M-step — Expectation step / Maximization step

The two alternating parts of EM. The E-step estimates how likely each hidden explanation is for the data; the M-step updates model settings to better fit those estimated explanations.

EfficientNet — Efficient convolutional network family

A family of image-recognition neural networks known for scaling depth, width, and input resolution in a coordinated way. “EfficientNet” is a model-family name rather than a letter-by-letter acronym; efficiency depends on the hardware and task.

FAISS — Facebook AI Similarity Search

A library for searching large collections of vectors by similarity, including approximate nearest-neighbour search. It is useful for finding text embeddings near a query embedding; vector similarity alone does not establish that a result is correct or relevant.

FastAPI — Python API framework name

The name of a Python framework for building web APIs with type hints and automatic interface documentation. “API” expands to Application Programming Interface; “FastAPI” itself is a product/framework name, not a standard initialism.

FastText — subword-aware text method

A Facebook-developed text representation and classification toolkit that represents words using character n-grams as well as word information. Its name is stylized, not an acronym; subword features can help with rare or morphologically varied words.

FAQ — Frequently Asked Questions

A collection of common questions and their answers. An AI FAQ assistant might retrieve an approved answer from such a collection, but should still confirm the retrieved guidance is current and applies to the question.

F1 / F-beta — F-scores

F1 is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, giving them equal weight. F-beta changes the balance: beta above 1 gives recall more weight, while beta below 1 gives precision more weight. These scores do not include true negatives and may not match the actual cost of errors.

FN — False Negative

A real positive case that the model incorrectly predicts as negative—for example, a fraudulent payment the detector misses. In a confusion matrix it is one of the four basic outcome counts.

FP — False Positive

A negative case incorrectly predicted as positive—for example, a genuine payment incorrectly flagged as possible fraud. False positives can waste investigation time or inconvenience customers.

GAN — Generative Adversarial Network

A generative model with two competing parts: a generator makes samples and a discriminator tries to distinguish generated from real examples. The training competition can be unstable, and synthetic data can copy bias or sensitive details.

GD — Gradient Descent

An optimization method that updates model parameters in the direction that reduces the loss, using its gradient. Batch, stochastic, and mini-batch describe how many examples are used to estimate each update.

GELU — Gaussian Error Linear Unit

A smooth neural-network activation that scales an input according to a Gaussian cumulative distribution function. It is commonly used in transformer models; the name's “Gaussian” refers to the mathematical function, not an assumption that the training data are normally distributed.

GloVe — Global Vectors for Word Representation

A method for learning word vectors from global word co-occurrence counts in a text collection. Its vectors capture some relationships between words, but traditional GloVe gives a word one fixed representation regardless of its sentence.

GMM — Gaussian Mixture Model

A probability model that represents data as a weighted mixture of Gaussian-shaped groups. Unlike K-Means, it can give each example partial membership probabilities; it assumes the data can be described reasonably by the chosen Gaussian components.

GPT — Generative Pre-trained Transformer

A family of decoder-style transformer language models commonly trained to predict the next token. “Generative” describes producing sequences and “pre-trained” describes broad initial training; neither guarantees that an answer is accurate.

GRU — Gated Recurrent Unit

A recurrent neural-network layer with gates that control how information is updated and retained across a sequence. It is a gated alternative to a basic RNN and is often simpler than an LSTM, though the best choice depends on the task.

HTML / CSS / JS — page technologies

HTML means HyperText Markup Language and describes page content; CSS means Cascading Style Sheets and controls presentation; JS means JavaScript and adds behaviour. The guide's interactive charts use Chart.js, a JavaScript chart library.

IQR — Interquartile Range

The distance from the first quartile (Q1) to the third quartile (Q3), covering the middle half of sorted observations. Because it focuses on the middle of the data, it is less affected by extreme values than the full range.

JSON — JavaScript Object Notation

A text format for structured data using objects and lists, widely used in web APIs. For example, a service can send a named field such as "risk": 0.2; systems should validate that incoming JSON matches the expected schema.

KL — Kullback–Leibler divergence

A measure of how one probability distribution differs from a reference distribution. It is used in areas such as VAEs, but it is asymmetric—swapping the two distributions generally changes the value—so it is not an ordinary distance.

KNN — K-Nearest Neighbors

A method that predicts from the labels or values of the K closest examples in the training data. Here K means the chosen number of neighbours, not a fixed constant; scale features because distance depends on their units.

KV — Key-Value cache

In a transformer, keys and values are intermediate attention results saved for previous tokens while generating text. Reusing the cache avoids recalculating all earlier tokens each time, at the cost of memory that grows with context and batch size.

L1 / L2 — first and second norms

L1 size sums absolute values; L2 size is the square root of the sum of squares. L1 penalties can encourage exactly zero model weights, while L2 penalties usually shrink weights smoothly; both are also used to define distances.

LayerNorm — Layer Normalization

A neural-network operation that normalizes features within each individual example, rather than calculating statistics across a batch. This makes it useful in transformer models whose batch sizes or sequence lengths can vary.

LDA — Linear Discriminant Analysis

A supervised method that seeks linear directions that separate labelled classes, using distribution assumptions such as shared within-class covariance in its standard form. This is the meaning used for classification in the guide.

LDA — Latent Dirichlet Allocation

A separate probabilistic topic-modelling method that represents documents as mixtures of topics and topics as distributions over words. The same initials are used for this and Linear Discriminant Analysis, so context matters.

Leaky ReLU — Leaky Rectified Linear Unit

An activation similar to ReLU that keeps a small non-zero slope for negative inputs instead of setting them all to zero. This can help avoid permanently inactive units; the slope is a design setting.

LIME — Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations

A method that perturbs one example and fits a simple interpretable model nearby to approximate the original model's behaviour locally. The explanation is approximate and depends on which perturbations and features are chosen.

LightGBM — Light Gradient Boosting Machine

A library for fast gradient-boosted decision trees, using histogram-based techniques and a leaf-wise tree-growing strategy. That strategy can fit well but may overfit smaller datasets unless depth, leaves, and other settings are controlled.

LLM — Large Language Model

A language model with many learned parameters, commonly trained on large text collections to predict or generate tokens. “Large” is relative and does not mean a model knows every fact or reasons correctly.

LOO — Leave-One-Out cross-validation

A cross-validation method that trains on all but one observation and repeats until each observation has been held out once. It uses most examples for each fit but can require many expensive fits and can give a noisy performance estimate.

LoRA — Low-Rank Adaptation

A parameter-efficient fine-tuning method that learns small low-rank update matrices alongside selected model weights. It can reduce training memory and adapter size, but still needs representative examples, careful evaluation, and a compatible model licence.

LSTM — Long Short-Term Memory

A recurrent neural-network architecture with gates and a cell state designed to preserve or forget information over sequence steps. It can learn longer dependencies than a basic RNN, though processing remains sequential and can be slower than attention-based models.

MAE — Mean Absolute Error

The average absolute difference between predictions and observed numeric values. It is measured in the target's units and is less dominated by a few large misses than squared-error metrics.

MAP — Mean Average Precision

A ranking measure that computes average precision for each query or user and then averages those values. It rewards relevant results appearing near the top; always state the ranking cutoff and how relevance is labelled.

MAP — Maximum a Posteriori

An estimation rule that chooses the parameter value with the greatest posterior probability given the data and a prior. It can be viewed as balancing observed evidence with an explicit prior assumption; this is a different meaning from Mean Average Precision.

MAPE — Mean Absolute Percentage Error

The average absolute prediction error expressed as a percentage of the observed value. It is difficult or impossible to interpret when actual values are zero or near zero, and percentage error can treat over- and under-prediction unevenly.

MCC — Matthews Correlation Coefficient

A correlation-like classification score that combines true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. It can summarise binary predictions under class imbalance, but should be shown with the confusion matrix and a clear threshold.

MICE — Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations

A method for handling missing data that repeatedly predicts each incomplete column from other columns, often producing several completed datasets. It represents uncertainty better than one simple fill, but relies on assumptions about how values are missing and should be fit using training data only.

ML — Machine Learning

A branch of AI in which a system learns patterns from examples to make predictions or decisions. Learning from data does not automatically remove human choices: people choose labels, inputs, objectives, and how outputs are used.

MLE — Maximum Likelihood Estimation

A way to choose model parameters that make the observed data most likely under the model. It uses the likelihood of the data but does not include a prior belief unless one is added, as in MAP estimation.

MLflow — machine-learning lifecycle tool name

A platform name used for tracking experiments, parameters, metrics, and models. “ML” means machine learning; MLflow itself is a product name rather than a standard acronym. Logging makes experiments easier to compare and reproduce.

MLP — Multi-Layer Perceptron

A feed-forward neural network with an input layer, one or more hidden layers, and an output layer. Hidden layers with nonlinear activations let it represent patterns more complex than a single straight-line model.

MLOps — Machine Learning Operations

Practices for reliably building, deploying, monitoring, and updating machine-learning systems. It brings together software operations, data pipelines, model versioning, governance, and people responsible for responding to failures.

MSE — Mean Squared Error

The average squared difference between numeric predictions and observations. Squaring makes large errors count more and leaves the metric in squared target units; RMSE takes the square root to return to the original units.

NDCG — Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain

A ranking measure that gives more credit to relevant items near the top and can handle graded relevance. It normalizes against an ideal ordering; report the cutoff (such as NDCG@10) and relevance labels used.

NLP — Natural Language Processing

The field of processing and generating human language with computers, including classification, translation, search, and summarisation. Indian-language NLP must consider scripts, morphology, transliteration, and mixed-language usage.

NumPy — Numerical Python

A Python library for numerical arrays and mathematical operations, often used for implementing small machine-learning algorithms. Its name combines “Numerical” and “Python”; array shape and broadcasting rules are important to understand.

OLS — Ordinary Least Squares

A method for fitting linear-regression coefficients by minimizing the sum of squared residuals. It can be calculated directly for suitable datasets or solved iteratively; the resulting fit does not prove that the model assumptions are valid.

OOB — Out-of-Bag

In bagging and Random Forests, training examples left out of a tree's bootstrap sample are that tree's out-of-bag examples. Their predictions can provide an internal error estimate, but they do not replace a deployment-realistic final test set.

OvO / OvR — One-vs-One / One-vs-Rest

Strategies for turning a binary classifier into a multiclass classifier. OvO trains a model for each pair of classes; OvR trains one class-versus-all-others model per class. They combine the separate predictions differently and can require different amounts of computation.

PCA — Principal Component Analysis

A method that transforms numeric features into perpendicular directions ordered by how much data variance they explain. PCA is useful for compression and visualisation, but it does not use a target label to find what best predicts the outcome.

PEFT — Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning

A family of methods that adapts a pretrained model by training a small portion of parameters or compact added modules. LoRA is a PEFT method; fewer trainable weights reduce resource needs but do not remove the need for quality data or evaluation.

pH — measure of acidity / alkalinity

A logarithmic measure related to hydrogen-ion activity in a solution, commonly used to describe acidity and alkalinity. It is a scientific symbol rather than an acronym; in a soil feature it can help describe growing conditions, but the useful range depends on the crop.

PR — Precision–Recall

A view of classification performance that plots precision against recall over possible thresholds. It is often helpful when the positive class is rare; the curve depends on how common positives are in the evaluated population.

PR-AUC — Area Under the Precision–Recall Curve

A summary of a precision–recall curve across thresholds. It can highlight performance on a rare positive class more clearly than ROC-AUC, but depends on prevalence and should be reported alongside operating-point precision, recall, and workload.

Q1 / Q3 — first / third quartile

Q1 is the value at the 25th percentile and Q3 the value at the 75th percentile, under a stated quartile convention. Their difference is the interquartile range; software packages can use slightly different conventions for small samples.

QLoRA — Quantized Low-Rank Adaptation

A fine-tuning approach that loads a base model in a lower-precision quantized form and trains LoRA adapters. It can reduce memory requirements, but quantization, hardware, and implementation choices may affect speed and quality.

R² — coefficient of determination

A regression score comparing squared prediction error with a mean-prediction baseline. On test data it can be negative when predictions are worse than that baseline; it is not “percent accuracy” and does not say whether errors are acceptable in real units.

RAG — Retrieval-Augmented Generation

A system design that retrieves relevant external information and gives it to a generative model as context before the model answers. Retrieval can ground an answer in sources, but bad or missing retrieval can still lead to a wrong response.

ReLU — Rectified Linear Unit

An activation that outputs zero for negative inputs and the input itself for positive inputs. It is simple and common in neural networks, although units can become inactive if their inputs remain negative.

ResNet — Residual Network

A family of neural networks that uses skip connections to let information and gradients pass around layers. These shortcuts help train deep networks; they do not guarantee the network generalizes well to new data.

REST — Representational State Transfer

An architectural style for web services that commonly uses HTTP methods and resources to exchange representations. A REST API is one way to serve a model; authentication, input checks, and reliable error responses still need to be designed.

RLHF — Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback

A model-alignment approach that uses human preferences, often to train a reward model, then optimizes model responses using reinforcement learning. Human feedback represents particular judgments and can be inconsistent, incomplete, or biased.

RMSE — Root Mean Squared Error

The square root of mean squared error, expressed in the same units as the target. It penalizes large misses more than MAE, so it is helpful when unusually large errors are especially costly.

RMSProp — Root Mean Square Propagation

An optimizer that adjusts parameter step sizes using a moving average of recent squared gradients. It can handle parameters with different gradient scales, but learning-rate choices and validation still matter.

RNN — Recurrent Neural Network

A network that processes a sequence one step at a time while carrying a hidden state forward. Basic RNNs can struggle to preserve information over long sequences because gradients may vanish or explode.

ROC — Receiver Operating Characteristic

A curve plotting the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate as a classifier threshold changes. It describes ranking trade-offs, but with very rare positives it can hide how many alerts will be false alarms.

ROUGE — Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation

A family of automatic text-generation scores based on overlap or coverage between a generated summary and reference summaries. The acronym is often expanded this way; overlap is only an imperfect stand-in for factual, useful summarisation.

SARIMA — Seasonal Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average

An ARIMA model with additional terms for repeating seasonal patterns. A seasonal period might represent 12 months in monthly data; seasonality settings should match the actual calendar and be tested on future time periods.

SGD — Stochastic Gradient Descent

Gradient descent that estimates an update from one training example at a time. Its noisy steps can make progress quickly on large datasets, though in deep-learning discussions “SGD” may also refer loosely to mini-batch stochastic gradient training.

SHAP — SHapley Additive exPlanations

A feature-attribution approach based on Shapley values from cooperative game theory. It allocates a prediction among features under a defined background/reference setup; attribution is not causation and may be tricky with correlated inputs.

SMOTE — Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique

A resampling method that creates synthetic minority-class examples between existing minority neighbours. It can help a learner see more minority examples, but synthetic points may be unrealistic and must only be generated within training folds.

SVD — Singular Value Decomposition

A matrix factorization into directions and non-negative singular values. Keeping only the largest singular values gives a compact, approximate representation useful in dimensionality reduction and recommendation; it does not mean missing ratings should be treated as zero.

SVM — Support Vector Machine

A model family that can find a boundary with a wide margin between classes; kernels can represent nonlinear boundaries through similarity calculations. SVMs often need scaled features, and kernel versions may be costly on large datasets.

t-SNE — t-distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding

A nonlinear method for making a low-dimensional visual map that tries to keep nearby high-dimensional examples near each other. It is useful for exploration, but the map's axes, island sizes, and global distances should not be read as a definitive clustering result.

TF-IDF — Term Frequency–Inverse Document Frequency

A text weighting that combines how often a term appears in a document with how uncommon that term is across documents. It highlights locally important words, but does not understand word order or full meaning.

TN — True Negative

A negative case correctly predicted as negative—for example, a genuine transaction correctly allowed by a fraud detector. Together with TP, FP, and FN, it forms the four counts in a binary confusion matrix.

TP — True Positive

A positive case correctly predicted as positive—for example, a fraudulent payment correctly flagged. The definition depends on which class the task declares positive.

Type I / Type II error

A Type I error is a false alarm: rejecting a true null hypothesis. A Type II error is a missed effect: failing to reject a false null hypothesis. In a screening example they resemble false positives and false negatives, respectively, but the exact mapping depends on how the hypotheses and positive class are defined.

UMAP — Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection

A method for projecting high-dimensional data to a smaller space while trying to preserve useful neighbourhood structure. Like t-SNE, settings and data preparation affect the picture; a visual separation alone does not prove meaningful clusters.

UPI — Unified Payments Interface

An Indian real-time payments system that allows participating apps and banks to send and receive payments. It appears in the guide as a familiar setting for fraud detection and customer-impact examples.

VAE — Variational Autoencoder

A generative autoencoder that learns a probability distribution over compact latent representations, using reconstruction and KL-divergence terms in its objective. Sampling can create new examples, but plausible-looking output is not necessarily accurate or safe.

VGG — Visual Geometry Group

The name of a research group at the University of Oxford and of CNN architecture families developed by that group. VGG-style networks use a straightforward stack of small convolution filters; their simplicity can come with substantial computation.

VIF — Variance Inflation Factor

A statistic that describes how much the variance of an estimated regression coefficient is inflated because a predictor is related to other predictors. A high VIF warns that individual coefficients may be unstable; it is not an automatic rule that a feature must be removed.

Word2Vec — Word to Vector

A family of methods that learns a vector for each word by predicting neighbouring words or the word from its context. The learned vectors capture some word relationships, but a word generally has one vector rather than a different context-sensitive vector for each meaning.

WordPiece — subword tokenization method

A tokenization method that builds a vocabulary of word pieces and represents an unfamiliar word as a sequence of known pieces. It is a method name rather than an acronym, and the way it divides text affects model input length and coverage of Indian-language words.

XGBoost — eXtreme Gradient Boosting

A software library for optimized gradient-boosted decision trees. The stylized name expands to eXtreme Gradient Boosting; regularisation and efficient training are useful features, but the library still needs leakage-safe evaluation and careful tuning.
