1 Algorithm Description

Real-life analogy: Imagine a doctor estimating the risk of a heart attack. She combines several risk factors — age, blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking — into a single risk score. But she doesn't want a raw score; she wants a probability: "This patient has a 73% chance of a heart attack in the next 10 years." Logistic Regression does exactly this — it takes a weighted sum of features and squashes it through an S-shaped curve (the sigmoid) so the output is always a valid probability between 0 and 1.

What is Logistic Regression?

Logistic Regression is a supervised classification algorithm that predicts the probability of a binary outcome (yes/no, spam/not-spam, churn/not-churn). Despite the name, it is a classification model, not a regression model — the word "regression" is a historical artefact.

It works in three steps:

  • Step 1 — Linear score: Compute a weighted sum of the input features: z = β₀ + β₁x₁ + β₂x₂ + … + βₚxₚ.
  • Step 2 — Squash with sigmoid: Pass z through the sigmoid function σ(z) = 1/(1 + e⁻ᶻ), producing a value between 0 and 1 that we interpret as P(y = 1 | x).
  • Step 3 — Threshold: Convert the probability into a class label by comparing it to a threshold (default 0.5).

Why "Logistic"?

The "logistic" comes from the logistic function (also called the sigmoid). It is the inverse of the logit function, which converts a probability into log-odds. The model is linear in log-odds space, which is why coefficients have a clean interpretation: eᵝ is the odds ratio.

How the Algorithm Learns (Step by Step)

Step 1 — State the model

P(y = 1 | x) = σ(β0 + β1x1 + … + βpxp) = 1 / (1 + e−(β0 + Σβjxj))

Step 2 — Define the likelihood

We want the coefficients that make the observed labels most likely. The likelihood is:

L(β) = Πi=1N piyi · (1 − pi)1 − yi

Step 3 — Maximise the log-likelihood

Taking the log converts the product to a sum, which is easier to optimise:

ℓ(β) = Σi=1N [ yi · log(pi) + (1 − yi) · log(1 − pi) ]

Equivalently, we minimise the negative log-likelihood, which is the log-loss (binary cross-entropy).

Step 4 — Solve with gradient descent or Newton's method

Unlike linear regression, logistic regression has no closed-form solution. Coefficients are found iteratively. The gradient of the log-likelihood has a beautifully simple form:

∂ℓ/∂βj = Σi=1N ( yi − pi ) · xij

Step 5 — Predict and threshold

Compute p̂ = σ(Xβ̂) for new samples, then assign class 1 if p̂ ≥ τ where τ is the threshold (default 0.5).

Step 6 — Evaluate and calibrate

Check that predicted probabilities are well-calibrated (e.g. samples predicted at 0.7 should actually be positive ~70% of the time). Recalibrate (Platt scaling, isotonic regression) if needed.

2 Applications

Logistic Regression is one of the most widely used classifiers in the world. It's the default model for binary classification in medicine, finance, marketing, and any domain where interpretability and calibrated probabilities matter.

🏥 Healthcare & Medicine

  • Disease risk prediction — 10-year heart attack risk (Framingham Risk Score).
  • Readmission prediction — will a patient return within 30 days?
  • Clinical trial analysis — treatment effect with covariates.
  • Diagnostic support — probability of disease given symptoms and labs.
  • Epidemiology — odds ratios for risk factors.

🏦 Banking & Finance

  • Credit scoring — probability of default on a loan.
  • Fraud detection — probability that a transaction is fraudulent.
  • Churn prediction — probability a customer will leave.
  • Basel-compliant PD models — probability of default for regulatory capital.
  • Insurance claims — probability of a claim.

📧 Technology & Web

  • Spam filtering — the classic logistic regression use case.
  • Click-through-rate prediction — probability a user clicks an ad.
  • Abandoned cart prediction — will the user complete checkout?
  • Malware detection — probability a file is malicious.
  • Subscription conversion — probability a free user upgrades.

🛒 Marketing & Retail

  • Lead scoring — probability a lead will convert.
  • Campaign response — probability a customer responds to an offer.
  • Customer segmentation — probability of belonging to a segment.
  • Upsell / cross-sell — probability of buying a related product.

🎓 Social Sciences

  • Voter turnout — probability a citizen votes.
  • College admission — probability of acceptance.
  • Criminal recidivism — probability of reoffending (COMPAS).
  • Survey analysis — modelling binary survey responses.
  • Loan approval — probability of approval.

🏭 Operations & Manufacturing

  • Predictive maintenance — probability a machine fails soon.
  • Quality control — probability a unit is defective.
  • Supply chain risk — probability a shipment is late.
  • Employee attrition — probability an employee leaves.
Why is it everywhere? Because it delivers calibrated probabilities and interpretable coefficients in one package. Regulated industries (banking, insurance, healthcare) demand both — you need to know not just what the model predicts, but why, and with what confidence. Logistic regression does both natively, which is why it's still the gold standard for credit scoring and clinical risk models in 2025.

3 Advantages

  • Highly interpretable — each coefficient tells you how the log-odds change per unit change in that feature. eᵝ is the odds ratio — a concept physicians, regulators, and executives understand.
  • Produces calibrated probabilities — not just class labels, but the actual probability of the positive class. Perfect for risk scoring, triage, and threshold tuning.
  • Fast to train and predict — training is O(N·p) per gradient step; prediction is a single dot product plus a sigmoid.
  • Low memory footprint — you store only the coefficients and intercept. A model with 100 features is ~400 bytes.
  • Works with few samples — unlike deep learning, it doesn't need millions of rows. 100–200 samples per feature can be enough.
  • No feature scaling required (for L-BFGS / Newton) — solvers like lbfgs are scale-invariant. (Gradient descent benefits from scaling.)
  • Statistically well-founded — maximum likelihood estimation gives coefficients with standard errors, p-values, confidence intervals, and Wald tests.
  • Handles multicollinearity with Ridge/Lasso — regularisation stabilises coefficients when features are correlated.
  • Performs feature selection with L1 — Lasso logistic regression zeroes out irrelevant features, yielding a sparse, explainable model.
  • Extends naturally to multi-class — softmax regression handles any number of mutually exclusive classes.
  • Robust to class imbalance with adjustments — class weights, oversampling, or a threshold tuned on a validation set.
  • Foundation for more advanced models — the last layer of almost every neural network classifier is a softmax/logistic layer.
  • Excellent baseline — before trying XGBoost or a neural net, logistic regression tells you if the problem is linearly separable and how much lift the complex model actually provides.

4 Disadvantages

  • Assumes a linear decision boundary — if the true boundary is curved, logistic regression will underfit. You must add polynomial or interaction features manually, or use a nonlinear model.
  • Sensitive to outliers — extreme feature values can dominate the loss and skew coefficients.
  • Struggles with multicollinearity — highly correlated features cause unstable, hard-to-interpret coefficients. Use Ridge/Lasso or remove correlated features.
  • Requires sufficient events per variable (EPV) — a rule of thumb is 10–20 positive cases per predictor. Fewer leads to overfitting and unreliable estimates.
  • Sensitive to class imbalance — with 1% positives, the model may predict everything as negative. Use class weights, resampling, or focal loss.
  • Cannot capture complex interactions automatically — you must manually create interaction terms (X₁ × X₂) if you suspect they matter.
  • Assumes independence of observations — repeated measures or clustered data violate this. Use mixed-effects or GEE models.
  • Assumes log-odds linearity — the relationship between each feature and the log-odds must be linear. Check with splines or polynomial terms.
  • Perfect separation breaks the model — if a feature perfectly separates the classes, coefficients diverge to infinity. Use regularisation or Firth's penalised likelihood.
  • Predicts poorly on very high-dimensional sparse data — without regularisation, text (TF-IDF) and image data overwhelm it.
  • No closed-form solution — must be solved iteratively (Newton, IRLS, L-BFGS), which is slower than OLS on large data.
  • Cannot model nonlinear probability curves — the sigmoid is monotonic and symmetric; more flexible shapes require splines or GAMs.
  • Miscalibration with regularisation — L1 and L2 shrink coefficients, which biases probabilities. Recalibrate on a validation set if probabilities matter.
The linearity trap: Imagine you want to classify points arranged in a ring (class 1 in the middle, class 0 around it). No straight line can separate them. Logistic regression will always fail here — you need a kernel method (SVM), a tree ensemble, or a neural network. Always plot your data first; if the boundary looks curved, logistic regression alone won't be enough.

5.2 Classification KPIs — Detailed

Accuracy Classification

What it measures: The overall fraction of predictions that were correct — both positives and negatives.

Formula Accuracy = (TP + TN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)
Worked example: TP = 480, TN = 850, FP = 50, FN = 120
Accuracy = (480 + 850) / (480 + 850 + 50 + 120) = 1330 / 1500 = 0.887 → 88.7%
Interpretation: 88.7% of all predictions were correct. Range: 0 to 1 (higher is better).
✔ Use when Classes are balanced and every correct prediction has equal business value.
✘ Avoid when Classes are imbalanced. A model that always says "legitimate" scores 99% accuracy but catches zero fraud.

Precision (Positive Predictive Value) Classification

What it measures: Of everything the model flagged as positive, how many actually were positive?

Formula Precision = TP / (TP + FP)
Worked example: TP = 480, FP = 50
Precision = 480 / (480 + 50) = 480 / 530 = 0.906 → 90.6%
Interpretation: When the model says "positive", it's right 90.6% of the time. High precision = few false alarms.
✔ Use when False positives are expensive — spam filters, product recommendations, targeted ads.
✘ Avoid when Missing a real positive is far worse than a false alarm (e.g. cancer screening).

Recall / Sensitivity / True Positive Rate Classification

What it measures: Of all the actual positives, how many did the model correctly catch?

Formula Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
Worked example: TP = 480, FN = 120
Recall = 480 / (480 + 120) = 480 / 600 = 0.800 → 80.0%
Interpretation: The model catches 80% of all real positives. It misses 20%. High recall = few missed cases.
✔ Use when Missing a positive is costly — fraud detection, disease screening, safety systems.
✘ Avoid when False alarms cause serious harm (e.g. flagging innocent customers).

Specificity / True Negative Rate Classification

What it measures: Of all the actual negatives, how many did the model correctly identify?

Formula Specificity = TN / (TN + FP)
Worked example: TN = 850, FP = 50
Specificity = 850 / (850 + 50) = 850 / 900 = 0.944 → 94.4%
Interpretation: The model correctly clears 94.4% of the actual negatives. It wrongly flags 5.6% as positive.

Relationship: Specificity + False Positive Rate (FPR) = 1. So FPR = 1 − Specificity = FP / (FP + TN).

F1-Score Classification

What it measures: A single number that balances Precision and Recall. It's the harmonic mean — so it punishes extreme imbalance between them.

Formula F1 = 2 × (Precision × Recall) / (Precision + Recall)

Equivalent form F1 = 2·TP / (2·TP + FP + FN)
Worked example: Precision = 0.906, Recall = 0.800
F1 = 2 × (0.906 × 0.800) / (0.906 + 0.800) = 1.4496 / 1.706 = 0.850
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1. A high F1 means the model is good at both finding positives and not crying wolf.
✔ Use when Classes are imbalanced and you care about both precision and recall roughly equally.
✘ Avoid when Precision and recall have very different business costs — use a weighted F-beta score.

F-beta generalisation: Fβ = (1+β²)·(P·R) / (β²·P + R). β > 1 weights recall higher; β < 1 weights precision higher.

ROC-AUC (Area Under the ROC Curve) Classification

What it measures: The probability that the model ranks a random positive example higher than a random negative example. It evaluates the model across all possible thresholds.

ROC Curve x-axis: FPR = FP / (FP + TN) = 1 − Specificity
y-axis: TPR = TP / (TP + FN) = Recall

AUC (via trapezoidal rule) AUC = Σi ½ · (xi+1 − xi) · (yi+1 + yi)

Probabilistic interpretation (Mann–Whitney U) AUC = P( score(positive) > score(negative) )
Interpretation scale:
• 0.50 → no better than random guessing
• 0.70–0.80 → acceptable
• 0.80–0.90 → excellent
• 0.90–1.00 → outstanding (be suspicious of data leakage)
• Below 0.50 → the model is systematically wrong (flip its predictions!)
✔ Use when You want a threshold-independent measure and classes are reasonably balanced.
✘ Avoid when The positive class is extremely rare — use PR-AUC instead.

PR-AUC (Precision–Recall Area Under the Curve) Classification

What it measures: Area under the Precision–Recall curve. It focuses only on the positive class, making it far more informative when positives are rare.

PR Curve x-axis: Recall = TP / (TP + FN)
y-axis: Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

PR-AUC PR-AUC = ∫ Precision d(Recall)  (approximated by trapezoids)

Baseline Baseline PR-AUC = prevalence = (TP + FN) / (TP + TN + FP + FN)
Example: In a fraud dataset with 1% fraud, a random classifier gets PR-AUC ≈ 0.01 (not 0.5). So a PR-AUC of 0.45 is actually very strong!
✔ Use when The positive class is rare — fraud, disease, defects, intrusions.
✘ Avoid when Both classes matter equally; ROC-AUC is more general.

Log Loss (Binary Cross-Entropy) Classification

What it measures: How well the model's predicted probabilities match reality. It punishes confident wrong predictions very heavily. This is also the loss function logistic regression minimises during training.

Formula (binary) LogLoss = −(1/N) · Σi=1N [ yi·log(pi) + (1−yi)·log(1−pi) ]

yi ∈ {0,1} = actual label  |  pi = predicted probability of class 1

Multi-class extension LogLoss = −(1/N) · Σi Σk yi,k · log(pi,k)
Worked example (N=2):
• Sample 1: actual = 1, predicted prob = 0.9 → loss = −log(0.9) = 0.105
• Sample 2: actual = 0, predicted prob = 0.8 → loss = −log(0.2) = 1.609
LogLoss = (0.105 + 1.609) / 2 = 0.857
Interpretation: Range 0 to ∞ (lower is better). Perfect predictions → 0. A model that always predicts 0.5 gets LogLoss ≈ 0.693 (binary).
✔ Use when You need well-calibrated probabilities — risk scoring, insurance pricing, medical estimates.
✘ Avoid when You only care about the hard class label, not the probability.

Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) Classification

What it measures: A balanced metric that uses all four confusion-matrix cells. It behaves like a correlation coefficient between predictions and truth.

Formula MCC = (TP·TN − FP·FN) / √[ (TP+FP)(TP+FN)(TN+FP)(TN+FN) ]
Worked example: TP=480, TN=850, FP=50, FN=120
Numerator = (480×850) − (50×120) = 408,000 − 6,000 = 402,000
Denominator = √(530 × 600 × 900 × 1000) ≈ 535,000
MCC = 402,000 / 535,000 = 0.751
Interpretation: Range −1 to +1. +1 = perfect, 0 = random, −1 = total disagreement.
✔ Use when Classes are very imbalanced and you want a single trustworthy number.
✘ Avoid when Explaining to a non-technical audience — harder to interpret.

Cohen's Kappa (κ) Classification

What it measures: Agreement between predictions and truth, corrected for agreement that would happen by chance.

Formula κ = (po − pe) / (1 − pe)

po = observed accuracy = (TP + TN) / N
pe = expected chance accuracy = [ (TP+FP)(TP+FN) + (TN+FP)(TN+FN) ] / N²
Landis & Koch scale:
• < 0.00 → poor · 0.00–0.20 → slight · 0.21–0.40 → fair
• 0.41–0.60 → moderate · 0.61–0.80 → substantial · 0.81–1.00 → almost perfect

Balanced Accuracy Classification

What it measures: The average of Recall (on positives) and Specificity (on negatives) — each class contributes equally regardless of size.

Formula Balanced Accuracy = (Recall + Specificity) / 2

= ½ · [ TP/(TP+FN) + TN/(TN+FP) ]
Worked example: Recall = 0.800, Specificity = 0.944
Balanced Accuracy = (0.800 + 0.944) / 2 = 0.872

Brier Score Calibration

What it measures: The mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes. Lower is better; it measures calibration.

Formula Brier = (1/N) · Σi=1N ( pi − yi )²

Decomposition Brier = Reliability − Resolution + Uncertainty
Interpretation: Range 0 (perfect) to 1. A model that always predicts the base rate gets Brier = p(1−p).

Calibration Curve (Reliability Diagram) Calibration

What it measures: Whether the predicted probabilities match the observed frequencies. A perfectly calibrated model lies on the diagonal.

Expected Calibration Error (ECE) ECE = Σb=1B ( nb / N ) · | acc(b) − conf(b) |

B = number of probability bins
nb = samples in bin b
acc(b) = actual positive rate in bin b
conf(b) = average predicted probability in bin b
Interpretation: ECE < 0.05 = well-calibrated. Larger ECE suggests recalibration (Platt scaling, isotonic regression).

5.3 Model Quality & Robustness KPIs

Cross-Validation Score Robustness

K-Fold CV Score CV = (1/K) · Σk=1K Metrick

Standard deviation (stability) σCV = √[ (1/K) · Σk ( Metrick − CV )² ]

Train–Test Gap (Overfitting Indicator) Robustness

Formula Gap = Metrictrain − Metrictest

For error metrics: Gap = Metrictest − Metrictrain
Rule of thumb:
• Gap < 5% → healthy
• Gap 5–10% → mild overfitting, increase regularisation
• Gap > 10% → serious overfitting, reduce features or add L1/L2

AIC & BIC (Model Comparison) Robustness

What it measures: Penalised likelihood scores for comparing logistic regression models with different numbers of features.

AIC AIC = 2k − 2·ℓ̂

BIC BIC = k·ln(N) − 2·ℓ̂

k = number of parameters
ℓ̂ = maximised log-likelihood
N = number of samples
Interpretation: Lower AIC/BIC is better. BIC penalises complexity more heavily than AIC.

Wald Test & Likelihood Ratio Test Statistical

What it measures: Statistical significance of individual coefficients (Wald) or the whole model (LRT).

Wald statistic Wj = (β̂j / SE(β̂j))²   ~ χ²(1)

Likelihood Ratio Test LRT = 2 · ( ℓ(full) − ℓ(reduced) )   ~ χ²(Δk)
Interpretation: p < 0.05 → significant. Wald tests each coefficient; LRT compares nested models.

6 Post-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Once the model is live, you're no longer asking "is it accurate?" — you're asking "is it still accurate today, and is anything silently breaking?"

6.1 Data & Model Health KPIs

PSI (Population Stability Index) Data Drift

What it measures: How much the distribution of a single feature (or the model's scores) has shifted between training and production.

Formula PSI = Σi=1B ( Actual%i − Expected%i ) · ln( Actual%i / Expected%i )

B = number of bins (usually 10 deciles)
Expected%i = % of training samples in bin i
Actual%i = % of production samples in bin i
Interpretation thresholds:
• PSI < 0.10 → no significant change, model is stable ✅
• 0.10 ≤ PSI < 0.25 → moderate shift, monitor closely ⚠️
• PSI ≥ 0.25 → major shift, retrain the model 🚨

KS Statistic (Kolmogorov–Smirnov) Data Drift

What it measures: The maximum vertical distance between two cumulative distribution functions (training vs. production).

Formula KS = maxx | Ftrain(x) − Fprod(x) |

F(x) = empirical cumulative distribution function
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1. Higher = more drift. A p-value < 0.05 from the KS test indicates statistically significant drift.

KL Divergence (Kullback–Leibler) Data Drift

Formula DKL(P ‖ Q) = Σi P(i) · log( P(i) / Q(i) )

P = production distribution  |  Q = training distribution
Interpretation: 0 = identical distributions. Higher = more drift. Not symmetric.

Concept Drift (Performance Decay) Model Health

Formula Drift Magnitude = Metricvalidation − Metriclive

Relative decay Decay % = ( Metricvalidation − Metriclive ) / Metricvalidation × 100
Interpretation: Decay > 10% typically triggers a retraining investigation.

Prediction Distribution Shift Model Health

Formula PSIpred = Σ ( ProdPred%i − TrainPred%i ) · ln( ProdPred%i / TrainPred%i )
Interpretation: A sudden jump in predicted probabilities signals a pipeline problem or concept drift.

Coefficient Stability Model Health

What it measures: Whether the learned coefficients are drifting over time. If they change rapidly, the underlying relationship is unstable.

Stability Index Stability = 1 − ( || βtrain − βprod || / || βtrain || )
Interpretation: Stability close to 1 = stable. Values below 0.8 suggest the relationship has shifted.

Missing Value Rate Model Health

Formula Missing % = ( Missing values / Total values ) × 100
Interpretation: Logistic regression cannot handle missing values natively, so a spike in missingness will directly degrade predictions.

6.2 Live Performance KPIs

Live Accuracy / Precision / Recall / AUC Performance

What it measures: The same classification metrics, recomputed on labelled production data as ground truth arrives.

Same formulas as pre-deployment Live Precision = TPlive / (TPlive + FPlive)
Live Recall = TPlive / (TPlive + FNlive)
Live AUC = computed on labelled production data
Interpretation: Ground truth often arrives with a delay. Set up automated pipelines to compute these as soon as labels arrive.

Actual vs. Predicted Monitoring Performance

Formula Deltat = Metricpredicted,t − Metricactual,t

CUSUM (cumulative sum control chart) CUSUMt = max(0, CUSUMt−1 + Deltat − k)
Interpretation: A steadily rising CUSUM indicates systematic drift. Alert when CUSUM crosses a threshold h.

Live Calibration (Reliability Drift) Performance

What it measures: Whether the model's probabilities remain well-calibrated over time.

Live ECE ECElive = Σb ( nb,live / Nlive ) · | acclive(b) − conflive(b) |
Interpretation: ECE rising above 0.05 in production suggests probabilities are drifting — recalibrate or retrain.

Model Age / Time Since Retrain Performance

Formula Model Age = Current Date − Last Retrain Date
Interpretation: Many teams set a hard limit (e.g. 90 days) as a retraining trigger, regardless of measured drift.

Latency Percentiles (p50, p95, p99) Operational

Definition p95 = the latency value below which 95% of requests fall

Why not the average? Average latency hides tail problems. A few slow requests (p99) can ruin user experience even if the average looks fine.
Target example: Logistic regression is extremely fast. p50 < 1 ms, p95 < 5 ms, p99 < 20 ms is typical for a well-optimised service.

Example formulas Cost Saved = ( Errorswithout − Errorswith ) × Cost per error
Revenue Uplift = Revenuewith model − Revenuewithout model
Churn Reduction % = ( Churnbaseline − Churnmodel ) / Churnbaseline × 100

ROI (Return on Investment) Business

Formula ROI % = ( Net Gain / Total Cost ) × 100%

Net Gain = Business Value Generated − Total Cost
Total Cost = Development + Infrastructure + Maintenance + Monitoring
Worked example: Fraud prevented = ₹5,00,000. Total cost = ₹1,00,000.
Net Gain = ₹4,00,000. ROI = (4,00,000 / 1,00,000) × 100 = 400%

Human Override Rate Business

Formula Override Rate % = ( Overrides / Total decisions ) × 100
Interpretation: A high override rate (e.g. > 30%) means users don't trust the model.

Alert / Incident Count Governance

Formula Alert Rate = Alerts triggered / Time period
Golden rule: A logistic regression model is not "done" when it's deployed. It's done when it's retired. Post-deployment monitoring is what keeps it from silently degrading.

7 Mathematical Formulas — How Logistic Regression Actually Works

The core idea: model the log-odds as a linear function of features, then squashing through the sigmoid to get probabilities. Here are the formulas.

7.1 The Sigmoid (Logistic) Function

Maps any real number into (0, 1). This is the "squashing" that turns a linear score into a probability.

Sigmoid function σ(z) = 1 / (1 + e−z)

Properties σ(0) = 0.5  |  σ(+∞) → 1  |  σ(−∞) → 0
σ(−z) = 1 − σ(z)
σ'(z) = σ(z) · (1 − σ(z))  (convenient for gradients!)

7.2 The Logit (Log-Odds) Function

The inverse of the sigmoid. Converts a probability into a real-valued linear score.

Logit function logit(p) = log( p / (1 − p) ) = log(odds)

Odds odds = p / (1 − p)   (e.g. p=0.75 → odds = 3, i.e. 3:1)

Inverse relationship p = σ(logit(p)) = 1 / (1 + e−logit(p))

7.3 The Model Equation

Log-odds form (what the model learns) log( p / (1 − p) ) = β0 + β1x1 + … + βpxp

Probability form p = σ(β0 + Σj βjxj) = 1 / (1 + e−(β0 + Σβjxj))

7.4 Odds Ratio Interpretation

Coefficients are easiest to interpret in odds-ratio form.

Odds ratio for feature j ORj = eβj

Interpretation A one-unit increase in xj multiplies the odds of y = 1 by eβj.

Example If βage = 0.05, then ORage = e0.05 ≈ 1.051.
Each additional year of age increases the odds of the event by ~5.1%.

Percentage change in odds % change = ( eβj − 1 ) × 100

7.5 Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

Coefficients are found by maximising the likelihood of the observed labels.

Likelihood L(β) = Πi=1N piyi · (1 − pi)1 − yi

Log-likelihood ℓ(β) = Σi=1N [ yi · log(pi) + (1 − yi) · log(1 − pi) ]

MLE β̂ = argmaxβ ℓ(β)

7.6 Log-Loss (Binary Cross-Entropy)

The negative log-likelihood — the loss function logistic regression minimises.

Log-loss J(β) = −(1/N) · Σi=1N [ yi · log(pi) + (1 − yi) · log(1 − pi) ]

Gradient ∂J/∂βj = (1/N) · Σi=1N ( pi − yi ) · xij

Hessian (Newton's method) Hjk = (1/N) · Σi=1N pi(1 − pi) · xij · xik

7.7 Gradient Descent Update

Update rule βj ← βj − α · ∂J/∂βj

α = learning rate (step size)
Repeat until convergence.

Newton's method (faster) β ← β − H−1 · ∇J
Converges in ~5–10 iterations, but requires inverting the Hessian.

7.8 Regularised Logistic Regression

L1 and L2 penalties prevent overfitting and enable feature selection.

L2 (Ridge) — shrinks coefficients JL2(β) = J(β) + (λ/2) · Σj=1p βj²

L1 (Lasso) — performs feature selection JL1(β) = J(β) + λ · Σj=1p |βj|

Elastic Net — combines both JEN(β) = J(β) + λ₁ · Σ|βj| + (λ₂/2) · Σβj²

7.9 Multinomial (Softmax) Logistic Regression

For K > 2 mutually exclusive classes, use the softmax function instead of the sigmoid.

Softmax P(y = k | x) = ezk / Σj=1K ezj

where zk = β0,k + β1,kx1 + … + βp,kxp

Loss (categorical cross-entropy) J = −(1/N) · Σi Σk yi,k · log(pi,k)

7.10 Standard Errors & Wald Tests

Fisher Information Matrix I(β) = Xᵀ W X

W = diag( pi(1 − pi) )

Covariance of coefficients Var(β̂) = I(β̂)−1

Standard error of coefficient j SE(β̂j) = √[ Var(β̂)jj ]

Wald statistic (per coefficient) Wj = (β̂j / SE(β̂j))²   ~ χ²(1)

Wald 95% CI for odds ratio CI = exp( β̂j ± 1.96 · SE(β̂j) )

7.11 Assumptions of Logistic Regression

Weaker than linear regression but still important for valid inference.

B
Binary outcome
Target is 0/1
I
Independence
Observations are independent
L
Linearity in logit
Log-odds linear in features
N
No multicollinearity
Features not too correlated
E
Enough events
10+ positives per predictor

8 Glossary of Acronyms & Terms

Every abbreviation used in this study guide, explained in plain English.

Algorithm & Model Concepts

ML
Machine Learning — a field of AI where systems learn patterns from data instead of being explicitly programmed.

Sigmoid
The S-shaped function σ(z) = 1/(1+e⁻ᶻ) that maps any real number to (0,1).
Logit
The inverse of the sigmoid: logit(p) = log(p/(1−p)).
Odds
p / (1 − p). The ratio of the probability of the event to the probability of no event.
Odds Ratio
eᵝ — the multiplicative change in odds per unit increase in a feature.
MLE
Maximum Likelihood Estimation — the method for finding coefficients by maximising the likelihood of the observed data.
IRLS
Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares — a Newton-like algorithm for fitting logistic regression.
L-BFGS
Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno — a quasi-Newton optimiser, the default in scikit-learn.

Ordinal
Logistic regression with 3+ ordered classes (e.g. Low/Med/High).

Fitting K binary logistic regressions for a K-class problem.

Confusion Matrix & Metrics

TP
True Positive — model predicted positive, actual was positive. ✅
TN
True Negative — model predicted negative, actual was negative. ✅
FP
False Positive — model predicted positive, actual was negative. Type I error.
FN
False Negative — model predicted negative, actual was positive. Type II error.
Accuracy
(TP + TN) / N — overall correctness.
Precision
TP / (TP + FP) — of the positives predicted, how many were right.
Recall
TP / (TP + FN) — of the actual positives, how many were found. Also called Sensitivity or TPR.
Specificity
TN / (TN + FP) — of the actual negatives, how many were correctly identified. Also called TNR.
F1
Harmonic mean of Precision and Recall: 2·(P·R)/(P+R).
Fβ
Weighted F-score: (1+β²)·P·R / (β²·P + R).
ROC
Receiver Operating Characteristic — a curve plotting TPR vs. FPR across thresholds.
AUC
Area Under the Curve — area under the ROC curve. 0.5 = random, 1.0 = perfect.
PR-AUC
Precision–Recall Area Under the Curve — better for imbalanced datasets.
FPR
False Positive Rate — FP / (FP + TN). The x-axis of the ROC curve.
TPR
True Positive Rate — same as Recall. The y-axis of the ROC curve.
Log Loss
Logarithmic Loss / Cross-Entropy — the loss function that penalises confident wrong probability predictions.
MCC
Matthews Correlation Coefficient — balanced metric from −1 to +1.
Brier Score
Mean squared difference between predicted probabilities and actual outcomes.
ECE
Expected Calibration Error — measures how well predicted probabilities match observed frequencies.
Balanced Accuracy
(Recall + Specificity) / 2 — class-balanced accuracy.
Cohen's κ
Agreement between predictions and truth, corrected for chance.

Statistical Concepts

SE
Standard Error — the standard deviation of a coefficient's sampling distribution.
Wald Test
Tests whether a coefficient is significantly different from 0. W = (β̂/SE)².
LRT
Likelihood Ratio Test — compares two nested models.
CI
Confidence Interval — e.g. 95% CI = β̂ ± 1.96·SE.
OR
Odds Ratio — eᵝ. The multiplicative change in odds.
MLE
Maximum Likelihood Estimation — the estimation method.
AIC
Akaike Information Criterion — model comparison metric. Lower is better.
BIC
Bayesian Information Criterion — like AIC but penalises complexity more heavily.
VIF
Variance Inflation Factor — measures multicollinearity.
EPV
Events Per Variable — number of positive cases divided by number of predictors. Aim for ≥10.
Perfect Separation
When a feature perfectly separates the classes; coefficients diverge. Use regularisation.
Firth
Firth's penalised likelihood — a solution to perfect separation.

Rescaling features to mean 0 and SD 1. Recommended for gradient-based solvers.

Weighting the loss to compensate for class imbalance.

Running a new model alongside the old one without acting on its output.
