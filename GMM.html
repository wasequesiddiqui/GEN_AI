1 Algorithm Description

Real-life analogy: Imagine you're handed a large bag of coloured marbles and asked to sort them. You can't see the colours directly — you only feel their size and weight. You're told there are three kinds of marbles, each with its own characteristic size and weight distribution, but the bags got mixed. You don't know which marble belongs to which kind. That's exactly the problem GMM solves — you have mixed data, and you want to infer the underlying types and assign each observation probabilistically to its type. Unlike K-Means (which forces hard assignments), GMM says "this marble is 70% Type A and 30% Type B."

What is a Gaussian Mixture Model?

A Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM) is a probabilistic generative model that assumes the data was generated by a mixture of K Gaussian distributions, each with its own mean, covariance, and mixing weight. GMM is the natural probabilistic generalisation of K-Means: it produces soft cluster assignments (probabilities) rather than hard ones, and it can model elliptical clusters of different sizes and orientations.

Key properties:

  • Generative — it models p(x), the probability density of the data. You can sample new points from it.
  • Soft assignment — every point gets a probability of belonging to each component (called a "responsibility").
  • Elliptical clusters — each component's covariance matrix can capture different shapes, sizes, and orientations.
  • Trained with EM — the Expectation-Maximisation algorithm iteratively refines the parameters.
  • K is a hyperparameter — chosen by BIC, AIC, silhouette, or domain knowledge.

How the Algorithm Works (Step by Step)

Step 1
Choose K and initialise
Pick K Gaussian components. Initialise each with random means, covariances, and equal weights (or use K-Means to seed them).
Step 2
E-step: compute responsibilities
For each point xi and component k, compute γik = how likely component k generated xi. These are the "soft assignments."
Step 3
M-step: update parameters
Using the responsibilities as weights, recompute each component's mean, covariance, and mixing weight.
Step 4
Compute log-likelihood
Compute the total log-likelihood of the data under the current parameters. It should increase every iteration.
Step 5
Repeat or stop
Repeat Steps 2–4 until the log-likelihood stops improving (below a tolerance like 1e-6).
Step 6
Assign or sample
Use responsibilities for soft clustering, or take argmax for hard clustering. Sample new points via ancestral sampling.

2 Applications

GMM is used everywhere you need a probabilistic, generative view of clustered data — from speaker recognition to financial regime detection to image segmentation.

🎙️ Speech & Audio

  • Speaker recognition — the classic use case. Each speaker is modelled by a GMM over MFCC features.
  • Speech emotion recognition — cluster emotional states.
  • Music genre classification — GMM of spectral features.
  • Voice activity detection — GMM vs. background model.
  • Language identification — one GMM per language.

🖼️ Image Processing

  • Background subtraction — model each pixel's background as a GMM over colours.
  • Image segmentation — soft tissue/object boundaries.
  • Colour quantisation — GMM instead of K-Means for smoother palettes.
  • Skin detection — GMM of skin colour distributions.

💰 Finance & Economics

  • Regime detection — bull vs. bear vs. sideways markets.
  • Volatility clustering — GMM of return distributions.
  • Fraud detection — anomalies have low GMM likelihood.
  • Credit risk — mixture of default and non-default populations.
  • Portfolio construction — regime-conditional allocation.

🧬 Biology & Healthcare

  • Gene expression — mixture of expression regimes.
  • Cell population clustering — flow cytometry / CyTOF.
  • Medical imaging — tissue segmentation.
  • EEG/ECG analysis — state detection.
  • Single-cell RNA-seq — soft cell type assignment.

📊 Data Science & ML

  • Anomaly detection — points with low likelihood are anomalies.
  • Density estimation — approximate any smooth density.
  • Feature engineering — GMM responsibilities as features.
  • Data generation — sample synthetic points from GMM.
  • Missing data imputation — use conditional expectations.

🌐 Other Domains

  • Astronomy — galaxy morphology, star populations.
  • Geology — mineral composition clustering.
  • Cybersecurity — traffic pattern modelling.
  • Robotics — sensor fusion, environment mapping.
  • Seismology — event classification.
Why is GMM so widely used? Because it's the smallest model that captures three things K-Means can't: (1) soft probabilistic assignments, (2) elliptical cluster shapes, and (3) a proper density function you can sample from. When any of those three matters — and in real data, they almost always do — GMM is the natural upgrade from K-Means.

3 Advantages

  • Soft, probabilistic assignments — every point gets a probability of belonging to each component. Ideal when the boundaries are fuzzy.
  • Elliptical clusters — the covariance matrix per component captures shape, size, and orientation. K-Means cannot do this.
  • Different-sized clusters — mixing weights πk automatically balance components with different populations.
  • Generative model — you can sample new points from the fitted GMM, useful for data augmentation and simulation.
  • Density estimation — GMM is a universal density approximator: with enough components, it can fit any smooth distribution.
  • Principled model selection — BIC and AIC provide a formal criterion for choosing K, unlike K-Means's ad-hoc elbow method.
  • Handles overlapping clusters — because it models probabilities, GMM naturally handles points on the boundary between two clusters.
  • Missing data handling — GMM can be extended to handle missing values through EM with conditional expectations.
  • Anomaly detection built-in — points with very low likelihood under the fitted density are natural outliers.
  • Extensions for structured data — Bayesian GMM, Dirichlet Process GMM (infinite mixtures), variational GMM, hidden Markov models built on top.
  • Well-studied theory — convergence of EM, identifiability of mixtures, asymptotic properties are all well understood.
  • Feature of many pipelines — speaker recognition, HMM-GMM speech models, image background models all rely on it.
  • Probabilistic distance — Mahalanobis distance to each component gives a well-defined similarity measure that accounts for covariance.
  • Generalises K-Means — K-Means is a limiting case of GMM with equal spherical covariances and hard assignments.

4 Disadvantages

  • You must choose K in advance — same as K-Means. BIC and AIC help, but they don't always agree and can overestimate K if the data isn't truly Gaussian.
  • Assumes Gaussian components — if the true clusters are non-Gaussian (e.g. heavy-tailed, skewed, multi-modal), GMM will fit poorly unless you use many components.
  • Sensitive to initialisation — different random inits can lead to different local optima. Always use multiple restarts, K-Means initialisation, or K-Means++ seeding.
  • EM converges to local optima only — no guarantee of finding the global maximum log-likelihood.
  • Singular covariance problem — if a component collapses onto a single point, its covariance becomes singular and the likelihood explodes to infinity. Common fix: add a small regularisation (reg_covar).
  • Slow for large N — O(N·K·d²) per iteration. Slower than K-Means, especially with full covariance in high dimensions.
  • Many parameters with full covariance — K · (d + d(d+1)/2) + K − 1 parameters. For high d, this overfits badly. Use diagonal or tied covariance to reduce.
  • Curse of dimensionality — in high dimensions, Gaussian assumptions become increasingly unrealistic and covariance estimation becomes unstable.
  • Requires feature scaling — covariance estimation is sensitive to feature scales.
  • Not ideal for non-convex clusters — a single Gaussian is elliptical. Non-convex clusters need multiple overlapping components.
  • Difficult to interpret high-dimensional components — you can't easily visualise a 50-D Gaussian.
  • Numerical instability — computing Gaussian PDFs involves exponentials and determinants. In high dimensions, underflow is a real problem (typically fixed with log-sum-exp tricks).
  • Doesn't scale to millions of points — for very large datasets, use Mini-Batch K-Means or variational Bayesian GMM with stochastic optimisation.
  • Model selection isn't perfect — BIC/AIC can favour more components than the "true" K if the data has slight non-Gaussianity.
The singularity trap: Imagine a GMM with K=5 on a dataset that only truly has 3 clusters. Two of the extra components might collapse onto individual data points — their covariance matrices shrink to near-zero and their likelihood shoots to infinity. Always use reg_covar (a small positive number on the diagonal) to prevent this.

5 Pre-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Because GMM is a probabilistic model, its KPIs are divided into three groups: likelihood-based (model fit), internal (cluster quality on hard labels), and external (when ground-truth labels exist).

5.1 Likelihood-Based KPIs — Unique to Probabilistic Models

Log-Likelihood (ℓ) Fit

What it measures: The total log-probability of the observed data under the fitted GMM. Higher is better. This is the quantity EM maximises.

Mixture density p( xi ) = Σk=1K πk · N( xi | μk, Σk )

Log-likelihood ℓ( θ ) = Σi=1N log [ Σk=1K πk · N( xi | μk, Σk ) ]
Worked example: For a single 1-D point x = 3.2 with two components:
π₁ = 0.6, μ₁ = 3.0, σ₁ = 0.5 → N(3.2|3.0,0.5) = 0.78
π₂ = 0.4, μ₂ = 6.0, σ₂ = 1.0 → N(3.2|6.0,1.0) = 0.008
p(x) = 0.6·0.78 + 0.4·0.008 = 0.471 → log p = −0.753
Interpretation: Absolute value depends on the dataset. Compare across models on the same data — higher ℓ is better. But never compare across datasets (different scales).
✔ Use when Comparing GMM fits with the same K on the same data. Watching EM convergence in training.
✘ Avoid when Comparing different K — more components always increase ℓ. Use BIC/AIC instead.

BIC (Bayesian Information Criterion) Model Selection

What it measures: Log-likelihood penalised by the number of parameters. Balances fit and complexity. Lower is better.

BIC BIC = −2 · ℓ( θ̂ ) + k · log( N )

Number of parameters (full covariance, d dimensions, K components) k = K · d + K · d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)

Where:
• K·d = K mean vectors
• K·d(d+1)/2 = K full covariance matrices
• K−1 = K−1 mixing weights (last is determined by sum-to-1)
Worked example: N = 1000, K = 3, d = 2, ℓ = −2400.
k = 3·2 + 3·3 + 2 = 17
BIC = −2·(−2400) + 17·log(1000) = 4800 + 117.5 = 4917.5
Interpretation: Lower BIC is better. The optimal K minimises BIC.
BIC tends to favour fewer components than AIC (stricter penalty).
✔ Use when Choosing between GMMs with different K, different covariance types, or different feature sets.
✘ Avoid when N is very small (the log N penalty dominates) or the model space is huge.

AIC (Akaike Information Criterion) Model Selection

What it measures: Log-likelihood penalised by complexity, but with a lighter penalty than BIC. Lower is better.

AIC AIC = −2 · ℓ( θ̂ ) + 2 · k

Where k = number of parameters (same formula as BIC above).
Worked example: Same as BIC example: ℓ = −2400, k = 17.
AIC = −2·(−2400) + 2·17 = 4800 + 34 = 4834
Interpretation: Lower AIC is better. AIC tends to favour more components than BIC — useful when you suspect more components exist but the data is limited.
✔ Use when You suspect BIC is underfitting and want a less strict penalty.
✘ Avoid when N is very large — AIC's penalty becomes trivial and it overfits.

Average Log-Likelihood per Point Fit

What it measures: The log-likelihood normalised by N — useful for comparing models on datasets of different sizes.

Formula ℓ̄ = ℓ( θ ) / N

Also called average log-probability or mean log-likelihood.
Interpretation: Higher is better. Cross-entropy loss in neural networks is exactly the negative of this quantity.

Perplexity Fit

What it measures: The exponentiated negative average log-likelihood — an information-theoretic measure of how "surprised" the model is on average.

Formula Perplexity = exp( − ℓ( θ ) / N ) = exp( − ℓ̄ )
Interpretation: Lower is better. Perplexity = 1 means perfect prediction (impossible in practice). Widely used in language modelling.

Effective Number of Parameters & DOF Complexity

What it measures: The total count of free parameters, depending on K, d, and the covariance type.

Spherical covariance k = K · d + K + (K − 1)

Diagonal covariance k = K · d + K · d + (K − 1)

Tied full covariance k = K · d + d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)

Full covariance (each component separate) k = K · d + K · d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)
Interpretation: Feed this into BIC/AIC. Fewer parameters = less overfitting risk but potentially worse fit.

5.2 Internal KPIs — Quality of the Hard Labels

Silhouette Score (on Hard Assignments) Internal

What it measures: Standard silhouette coefficient, computed on the hard labels (argmax of responsibilities). Measures how well-separated the resulting clusters are.

Per-point silhouette s(i) = ( b(i) − a(i) ) / max( a(i), b(i) )

a(i) = mean distance from i to other points in its cluster
b(i) = min mean distance from i to points in any other cluster

Overall silhouette S = (1/N) · Σ s(i)    Range: −1 to +1
Interpretation scale:
• S > 0.5 → strong, well-separated clusters ✅
• S 0.25–0.5 → weak but usable
• S < 0.25 → no real structure
✔ Use when You want a metric that doesn't depend on the Gaussian assumption — compares the resulting clusters geometrically.
✘ Avoid when Clusters are highly non-spherical — silhouette favours compact clusters.

Davies-Bouldin Index (DBI) Internal

What it measures: Average ratio of within-cluster scatter to between-cluster separation. Lower is better.

Formula DBI = (1/K) · Σk=1K maxj ≠ k ( Sj + Sk ) / Mjk

Sk = mean distance from cluster k points to its centroid
Mjk = distance between centroids j and k
Interpretation: Lower is better. DBI < 1 is excellent; DBI > 2 is poor.

Calinski-Harabasz Index (CH) Internal

What it measures: Ratio of between-cluster dispersion to within-cluster dispersion. Higher is better.

Formula CH = ( SSB / (K − 1) ) / ( SSW / (N − K) )

SSB = Σk |Ck| · || μk − μ ||²
SSW = Σk Σxi ∈ Ck || xi − μk ||²
Interpretation: Higher is better. Peaks at the "best" K on hard labels.

Dunn Index Internal

Formula Dunn = minj ≠ k d( Cj, Ck ) / maxk diam( Ck )
Interpretation: Higher is better. Dunn > 1 means well-separated clusters.

5.3 GMM-Specific KPIs — Unique to Mixture Models

Average Maximum Responsibility (Confidence) GMM-Specific

What it measures: For each point, take the highest responsibility across components, then average. Measures how "confident" the model is on average.

Formula Confidence = (1/N) · Σi=1N maxk γik

γik = responsibility of component k for point i
Interpretation scale:
• > 0.95 → very confident (clusters are well-separated) ✅
• 0.75–0.95 → normal
• < 0.75 → lots of boundary points, clusters overlap ⚠️
✔ Use when You want to know how "sure" the GMM is about its clusters.

Assignment Entropy GMM-Specific

What it measures: How "mixed" the responsibilities are. High entropy = soft/borderline points; low entropy = confident assignments.

Per-point entropy Hi = − Σk γik · log γik

Average entropy H = (1/N) · Σi Hi

Normalised entropy H̄ = H / log( K )    Range 0 to 1
Interpretation: H̄ close to 0 = crisp assignments. H̄ close to 1 = every point is split equally across components — the GMM hasn't learned anything.

Effective Number of Components GMM-Specific

What it measures: The number of components with non-negligible mixing weights. Useful for detecting "dead" components.

Effective K Keff = # { k : πk > ε }

ε = threshold, typically 0.01 or 0.05

Alternative (entropy-based) Keff = exp( − Σk πk · log πk )
Interpretation: If Keff < K, some components died during training. Reduce K or use a stronger initialisation.

Component Condition Numbers GMM-Specific

What it measures: The condition number of each component's covariance matrix. Very high values indicate near-singular covariance (a collapsing component).

Formula κ( Σk ) = λmax( Σk ) / λmin( Σk )

λmax, λmin = largest and smallest eigenvalues
Interpretation: κ < 10⁴ = healthy. κ > 10⁸ = near-singular, increase reg_covar to prevent collapse.

5.4 External KPIs — With Ground-Truth Labels

Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) External

What it measures: Agreement between the predicted hard labels and the true labels, corrected for chance. Range −1 to +1.

Rand Index RI = ( a + b ) / C(N, 2)

Adjusted Rand Index ARI = ( RI − E[RI] ) / ( max(RI) − E[RI] )

a = pairs together in both clusterings
b = pairs apart in both clusterings
Interpretation: ARI = 1 → perfect; ARI = 0 → random; ARI < 0 → worse than random.

Normalised Mutual Information (NMI) External

NMI NMI = MI( U, V ) / √( H(U) · H(V) )

MI = mutual information between predicted clusters U and true labels V
H = Shannon entropy
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher is better). NMI = 1 means perfect correspondence.

Fowlkes-Mallows Index (FMI) External

Formula FMI = √( precision · recall )

precision = TP / (TP + FP), recall = TP / (TP + FN)
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher is better).

5.5 📊 Interactive: BIC/AIC + Silhouette Across K

The three most common heuristics for choosing K in GMM. BIC and AIC use likelihood; silhouette uses geometry. When they agree, you can be confident in K.

6 Post-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Once a GMM is deployed (e.g., speaker identification, background subtraction, market regimes), you must monitor whether the components still describe the current data.

6.1 Likelihood Drift

Average Log-Likelihood Drift Model Health

What it measures: Whether new production data is less likely under the fitted GMM than training data.

Formula Δℓ̄ = ℓ̄train − ℓ̄prod

ℓ̄ = (1/N) · Σi log p( xi | θ )

Relative decay Decay % = ( ℓ̄train − ℓ̄prod ) / | ℓ̄train | × 100
Interpretation: Decay > 10% typically triggers a retraining investigation. A large negative drift means production data looks very different from training.

Perplexity Drift Model Health

What it measures: The same idea in perplexity terms — a scale-free measure of surprise.

Formula Perplexityt = exp( − ℓ̄t )

Drift ratio Ratio = Perplexityprod / Perplexitytrain
Interpretation: Ratio < 1.1 = stable. Ratio > 1.3 = significant drift. Ratio > 2 = severe drift, retrain immediately.

Outlier Rate (Low-Likelihood Points) Model Health

What it measures: Fraction of production points whose likelihood under the GMM falls below a threshold (e.g., 1st percentile of training likelihoods).

Formula OutlierRate = # { i : log p( xi | θ ) < τ } / N

τ = threshold (e.g., 1st percentile of training log-likelihoods)
Interpretation: A spike in outlier rate is one of the earliest signals of concept drift. Also useful directly for anomaly detection.

6.2 Parameter Drift

Component Mean Shift (Mahalanobis) Parameter Drift

What it measures: How much each component's mean has moved between training and production, measured in units of the training-time covariance.

Mahalanobis shift Dk = √[ ( μkprod − μktrain )ᵀ Σktrain,−1 ( μkprod − μktrain ) ]
Interpretation: Dk > 1 means the mean moved by more than 1 standard deviation on average. Dk > 2 = significant shift, examine that component.

Covariance Matrix Drift (Frobenius) Parameter Drift

What it measures: How much each component's covariance matrix has changed.

Relative Frobenius norm Driftk = || Σkprod − Σktrain ||F / || Σktrain ||F

|| A ||F = √( Σij Aij² )
Interpretation: Drift < 0.2 = stable. Drift > 0.5 = component's shape has changed significantly.

Mixing Weight Drift Parameter Drift

What it measures: Change in the relative size of each component (its prior probability).

Total Variation Distance TVD = ½ · Σk=1K | πkprod − πktrain |
Interpretation: TVD < 0.05 = stable. TVD > 0.15 = significant population shift. A component shrinking to near-zero means that class has disappeared.

Parameter Stability Index Parameter Drift

What it measures: Aggregate stability across all parameters (means, covariances, weights) in a single number.

Formula Stability = 1 − ( || θtrain − θprod || / || θtrain || )

θ = concatenation of all GMM parameters
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1. > 0.85 = stable. < 0.65 = significant shift, retrain.

6.3 Assignment Quality Drift

Responsibility Stability Assignment

What it measures: How consistent the soft assignments are across time. Uses KL divergence between responsibility distributions.

KL divergence per point KLi = Σk γikprod · log( γikprod / γiktrain )

Average drift Drift = (1/N) · Σi KLi
Interpretation: Range 0 to log(K). Lower is more stable. Drift > 0.5 means assignments have shifted significantly.

Assignment Confidence Drift Assignment

What it measures: Change in the average maximum responsibility over time. A drop means the model is more uncertain now.

Formula ΔConf = Confidencetrain − Confidenceprod

Confidence = (1/N) · Σi maxk γik
Interpretation: A drop of > 0.15 means the model is losing confidence — production data is crossing boundaries it didn't see during training.

Anomaly Score Distribution Assignment

What it measures: Distribution of negative log-likelihoods across production points. Rising tail = more anomalies.

Anomaly score s( xi ) = − log p( xi | θ )

Track the 95th and 99th percentiles of s over time.
Interpretation: A rising 99th percentile means the model is increasingly surprised by production data.

6.4 Business & Operational KPIs

Segment Actionability Business

What it measures: Whether business KPIs differ across components — proving the segmentation is useful.

Between-component variance of business KPI Varbetween = Σk πk · ( KPIk − KPIoverall )²

F-ratio F = Varbetween / Varwithin
Interpretation: Higher F = components differ meaningfully on business metrics.

Sampling Validity Business

What it measures: If you generate synthetic data from the GMM, does it look like real data? Useful for data augmentation.

Two-sample test Use a Kernel Two-Sample Test (MMD) or Adversarial Validation AUC:
• MMD close to 0 → synthetic looks real
• AUC close to 0.5 → classifier can't distinguish real from synthetic

Assignment Latency Operational

What it measures: Time to compute responsibilities for a new point.

Formula AssignTime = O( K · d² )

K Gaussian PDF evaluations per point, each requiring a d×d matrix inverse.
Target example: For K = 10, d = 20: p50 < 0.5 ms, p95 < 5 ms. Fast enough for real-time scoring.
Golden rule: A GMM is a snapshot of the data distribution at one point in time. As data drifts, the components no longer represent reality. Monitor log-likelihood drift, mean shifts, and weight changes — and retrain when any crosses a threshold.

7 Mathematical Formulas — How GMM Actually Works

The core idea: the data is a mixture of K Gaussian distributions, and we want to infer their parameters from the data using maximum likelihood and the EM algorithm.

7.1 The Multivariate Gaussian PDF

Formula N( x | μ, Σ ) = ( 2π )−d/2 · | Σ |−1/2 · exp( −½ ( x − μ )ᵀ Σ−1 ( x − μ ) )

x ∈ ℝd = data point
μ ∈ ℝd = mean vector
Σ ∈ ℝd×d = covariance matrix (symmetric positive-definite)
| Σ | = determinant of Σ
( x − μ )ᵀ Σ−1 ( x − μ ) = Mahalanobis distance squared

7.2 The Gaussian Mixture Density

Mixture density p( x | θ ) = Σk=1K πk · N( x | μk, Σk )

Constraints Σk πk = 1    (weights sum to 1)
πk ≥ 0    (weights non-negative)

Parameters θ = { π1:K, μ1:K, Σ1:K }

7.3 The Log-Likelihood Objective

Log-likelihood ℓ( θ ) = Σi=1N log [ Σk=1K πk · N( xi | μk, Σk ) ]

Maximum likelihood estimate θ̂ = argmaxθ ℓ( θ )

Why EM? There is no closed-form solution for θ. The log of a sum makes the
gradient intractable. EM handles this by introducing latent variables.

7.4 Latent Variables & Responsibilities

Introduce a latent variable zi ∈ {1,…,K} indicating which component generated point xi. We don't observe zi, but we can compute its posterior — the responsibility.

Joint distribution p( xi, zi = k | θ ) = πk · N( xi | μk, Σk )

Posterior (responsibility) — the E-step γik = p( zi = k | xi, θ ) = ( πk · N( xi | μk, Σk ) ) / ( Σj=1K πj · N( xi | μj, Σj ) )

Properties Σk=1K γik = 1    (each point's responsibilities sum to 1)
0 ≤ γik ≤ 1

7.5 The EM Algorithm — Full Derivation

E-step (Expectation) For each i, k:
γik = ( πk · N( xi | μk, Σk ) ) / ( Σj πj · N( xi | μj, Σj ) )

M-step (Maximisation)
Nk = Σi=1N γik   (effective number of points in component k)

μknew = ( 1 / Nk ) · Σi=1N γik · xi

Σknew = ( 1 / Nk ) · Σi=1N γik · ( xi − μknew )( xi − μknew )ᵀ

πknew = Nk / N

7.6 The EM Guarantee — Monotone Likelihood Increase

Monotonicity ℓ( θ( t+1 ) ) ≥ ℓ( θ( t ) )

This is guaranteed by the EM algorithm. It converges to a local maximum of the log-likelihood.

Convergence criterion Stop when | ℓ( θ( t+1 ) ) − ℓ( θ( t ) ) | < ε
Typical ε = 1e-6 or 1e-4

7.7 Log-Sum-Exp Trick (Numerical Stability)

In high dimensions, the Gaussian PDF underflows. To compute the log-likelihood stably, use:

Log-sum-exp log Σk exp( ak ) = amax + log Σk exp( ak − amax )

where amax = maxk ak

Log of Gaussian PDF log N( x | μ, Σ ) = −½ [ d · log( 2π ) + log | Σ | + ( x − μ )ᵀ Σ−1 ( x − μ ) ]

This avoids computing p( x ) directly — everything stays in log-space.

7.8 BIC and AIC — Full Formulas

Number of free parameters (full covariance) k = K · d + K · d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)

BIC BIC = −2 · ℓ( θ̂ ) + k · log( N )

AIC AIC = −2 · ℓ( θ̂ ) + 2 · k

Interpretation Lower is better for both. BIC's log(N) penalty grows with sample size;
AIC's penalty is fixed at 2 per parameter.

7.9 Regularised Covariance (Preventing Singularities)

Regularisation Σkreg = Σk + ε · I

ε = reg_covar (typically 1e-6)
I = identity matrix

Why it helps Ensures the covariance matrix is always invertible.
Prevents the log-likelihood from blowing up to +∞ when a component collapses onto a point.

7.10 Covariance Types & Parameter Counts

Spherical Σk = σk² · I   (1 parameter per component)
k = K · d + K + (K − 1)

Diagonal Σk = diag( σk1², …, σkd² )   (d parameters per component)
k = K · d + K · d + (K − 1)

Tied full Σk = Σ   (same for all k; d(d+1)/2 parameters total)
k = K · d + d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)

Full Σk arbitrary symmetric positive-definite
k = K · d + K · d(d+1)/2 + (K − 1)

7.11 Sampling from a Fitted GMM (Ancestral Sampling)

Because GMM is generative, you can generate new data points that look like the training data:

Ancestral sampling algorithm 1. Sample a component index k ~ Categorical( π1, …, πK )
2. Sample a point x ~ N( μk, Σk )
3. Repeat for as many samples as needed

Cholesky trick for step 2 Compute L such that Σk = L · Lᵀ
Sample z ~ N( 0, I )   (standard normal)
Set x = μk + L · z

8 Glossary of Acronyms & Terms

Every abbreviation used in this study guide, explained in plain English.

Algorithm & Model Concepts

GMM
Gaussian Mixture Model — a probabilistic generative model that represents data as a weighted sum of Gaussian distributions.
EM
Expectation-Maximisation — the iterative algorithm used to fit GMMs (and other latent-variable models).
Mixture Model
A probability distribution that is a weighted sum of simpler distributions.
Component
One of the K Gaussian distributions making up the mixture.
Mixing Weight
πk — the prior probability that a random point comes from component k.
Soft Assignment
Probabilistic cluster membership — every point has a probability of belonging to each cluster.
Hard Assignment
Definitive cluster membership (argmax over responsibilities).
Generative Model
A model of the full data distribution p(x), allowing sampling of new data.
Discriminative Model
A model of p(y|x) — predicts labels given features. K-Means is closer to this.
K-Means
A hard-assignment, spherical-cluster algorithm. GMM generalises it.
Bayesian GMM
A GMM with priors on parameters; often uses a Dirichlet process to allow infinite components.
DPMM
Dirichlet Process Mixture Model — infinite-mixture extension of GMM.
Variational GMM
A scalable approximation to Bayesian GMM using variational inference.
HMM-GMM
A hidden Markov model whose emissions are GMMs. Classic speech recognition architecture.

Probability & Statistics

Gaussian
Another name for the normal distribution.
PDF
Probability Density Function — describes the relative likelihood of continuous values.
μ (mu)
Mean of a Gaussian component (vector for multivariate).
Σ (Sigma)
Covariance matrix of a Gaussian component.
π (pi)
Mixing weight of a component.
Covariance
Matrix describing the variance and correlation of features within a component.
Mahalanobis
A distance measure that accounts for covariance — the "shape-aware" distance to a mean.
Likelihood
The probability of the observed data given parameters.
Log-Likelihood
The log of the likelihood. Sum rather than product, numerically stable.
Posterior
P(z|x,θ) — the probability of a latent variable given data and parameters.
Latent Variable
An unobserved variable (here, the component assignment zi).
Responsibility
γik — the posterior probability that component k generated point i.
Log-Sum-Exp
A numerical trick to compute log of a sum of exponentials without underflow.
Cholesky
A matrix decomposition used to sample from a multivariate Gaussian.
Regularisation
Adding a small value to the covariance diagonal to prevent singularity.
Identifiability
Property that the true parameters can be recovered from infinite data. GMMs are identifiable up to label permutation.
Label Switching
Ambiguity in mixture models — components can be relabelled without changing the likelihood.

Model Selection Metrics

BIC
Bayesian Information Criterion — −2ℓ + k·log(N). Lower is better.
AIC
Akaike Information Criterion — −2ℓ + 2k. Lower is better.
ℓ
Log-likelihood of the data under the model.
k
Number of free parameters in the model.
Perplexity
exp(−ℓ/N). Information-theoretic surprise. Lower is better.
ICL
Integrated Completed Likelihood — BIC with an extra entropy term favouring crisp clusters.
Silhouette
Per-point cohesion-vs-separation score, −1 to +1.
DBI
Davies-Bouldin Index — lower is better.
CH
Calinski-Harabasz Index — higher is better.
Dunn Index
Inter-cluster / intra-cluster distance ratio. Higher is better.
ARI
Adjusted Rand Index — agreement with true labels, adjusted for chance.
NMI
Normalised Mutual Information — shared information between two labellings.
FMI
Fowlkes-Mallows Index — geometric mean of pairwise precision and recall.

Data & Training Concepts

Feature
An input variable (dimension).
Dimensionality (d)
Number of features per point.
Feature Scaling
Standardising or normalising features before GMM.
Standardisation
Subtract mean, divide by standard deviation.
Initialisation
Starting values for EM — usually K-Means centroids or random draws.
Restarts
Running EM multiple times with different inits, keeping the best log-likelihood.
Convergence
When the log-likelihood change per iteration falls below tolerance.
Local Optimum
A fixed point of EM that may not be the global maximum.
Singularity
When a component's covariance becomes non-invertible (collapsed onto a point).
Overfitting
Fitting noise — happens with too many components or full covariance on small data.
Regularisation
Small diagonal addition to covariances — prevents singularity and reduces overfitting.
Cross-Validation
Held-out evaluation for hyperparameter tuning (K, covariance type).
Hyperparameter
A setting chosen before training (K, covariance type, reg_covar).

Monitoring & Deployment

KPI
Key Performance Indicator — a measurable value tracking model or business performance.
Drift
Change in data distribution or model behaviour over time.
Log-Likelihood Drift
Drop in average log-likelihood on production data.
Parameter Drift
Change in the fitted parameters between training and production refits.
Mahalanobis Shift
Distance a mean moved, scaled by the covariance.
Frobenius Norm
Matrix norm — √(Σ Aij²). Used to measure covariance drift.
TVD
Total Variation Distance — half the L1 distance between two probability vectors.
KL Divergence
Asymmetric measure of how one distribution differs from another.
PSI
Population Stability Index — a symmetric, threshold-based drift metric.
KS Test
Kolmogorov-Smirnov Test — tests whether two distributions differ.
Outlier Rate
Fraction of production points below a likelihood threshold.
CUSUM
Cumulative Sum Control Chart — sequential change detection.
MLOps
Machine Learning Operations — deploying and monitoring ML in production.
Shadow Deployment
Running a new model alongside the old one without acting on its output.
Champion / Challenger
The current production model vs. a candidate model.
MMD
Maximum Mean Discrepancy — a kernel-based two-sample test.

Final tip: GMM is the natural probabilistic upgrade from K-Means — it adds soft assignments, elliptical clusters, and a full density model. Always (1) standardise features, (2) use multiple restarts or K-Means++ initialisation, (3) set reg_covar to avoid singularities, (4) pick K with BIC/AIC plus silhouette, and (5) monitor log-likelihood drift in production. GMM is a workhorse — from speaker recognition to anomaly detection to regime modelling.
