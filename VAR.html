1 Algorithm Description

Real-life analogy: Imagine you're a portfolio manager with ₹100 crore under management. Your boss asks: "How much could we lose in a really bad day?" You don't say "it depends." You say: "On 99% of days, our losses won't exceed ₹4 crore. Only 1 day in 100 will be worse." That single number — ₹4 crore — is the 99% 1-day VaR. It answers the boss's question with a specific, defensible number, backed by a statistical model of returns.

What is Value at Risk?

Value at Risk (VaR) is a statistical measure of the maximum loss a portfolio is expected to suffer over a given time horizon, at a given confidence level, under normal market conditions. It summarises an entire loss distribution into a single number that's easy to communicate to management, regulators, and traders.

VaR is not a prediction — it's a quantile of the loss distribution. It says: "With probability α, the loss will not exceed VaRα."

The Three Main VaR Methodologies

MethodHow it WorksAssumptionSpeedBest For
Historical Simulation Revalue the current portfolio under the last N historical return scenarios; take the α-quantile. Past is a good guide to the future Fast Portfolios with non-normal, fat-tailed returns
Parametric (Variance-Covariance) Assume returns are Gaussian; compute VaR from portfolio mean and standard deviation. Returns are normally distributed Very fast Large portfolios, linear instruments, quick estimates
Monte Carlo Simulation Simulate many paths of risk factors; reprice the portfolio; take the α-quantile. Correct factor model + correct distribution Slow Complex, non-linear, path-dependent portfolios
Filtered Historical GARCH the returns, then apply historical simulation to standardised residuals. Volatility is time-varying Moderate Regime-switching markets
Extreme Value Theory (EVT) Model the tail of the loss distribution explicitly (GPD). Tail follows a generalised Pareto distribution Moderate Extreme quantiles (99.9% VaR, ES)

How VaR is Computed (Step by Step)

Step 1
Choose parameters
Pick a confidence level α (typically 95% or 99%) and a horizon T (1 day, 10 days). Regulators often mandate 99% / 10 days.
Step 2
Collect return scenarios
Historical: use the last N daily returns. Parametric: estimate μ and σ. Monte Carlo: simulate factor returns.
Step 3
Revalue the portfolio
For each scenario, compute the portfolio's P&L. For non-linear portfolios (options), reprice with a full model.
Step 4
Build the loss distribution
Sort the N simulated/historical P&L values. This is the empirical loss distribution.
Step 5
Extract the α-quantile
VaR is the (1 − α)-quantile of the loss distribution. For α = 99%, it's the worst 1% cutoff.
Step 6
Compute ES alongside
Expected Shortfall = average of all losses beyond VaR. It's a coherent risk measure and complements VaR's blind spot.

2 Applications

VaR is the lingua franca of financial risk management. Since the Basel Committee endorsed it in 1996, it has become mandatory for banks worldwide — and it's used far beyond regulatory compliance.

🏦 Banking & Regulation

  • Market risk capital — Basel II/III require 99% / 10-day VaR for trading books.
  • FRTB — Basel IV's Fundamental Review of the Trading Book uses Expected Shortfall (ES) at 97.5% instead of VaR.
  • Internal models approach — banks use their own VaR models, subject to regulatory backtests.
  • Regulatory reporting — daily VaR disclosure to supervisors.
  • Limit setting — trading desks get VaR limits; breaches trigger investigation.

📈 Portfolio Management

  • Risk budgeting — allocate VaR contribution across strategies.
  • Portfolio optimisation — mean-VaR efficient frontier (alternative to mean-variance).
  • Hedge fund monitoring — daily risk reporting to investors.
  • Pension funds — funding-ratio VaR.
  • Sovereign wealth funds — multi-asset VaR across portfolios.

🏢 Corporate Treasury

  • FX exposure — VaR on foreign currency positions.
  • Interest rate risk — VaR on debt portfolios.
  • Commodity risk — airlines hedging jet fuel, miners hedging metals.
  • Cash management — VaR on short-term investments.
  • Earnings-at-Risk (EaR) — VaR applied to income statement.

💼 Asset Managers & Funds

  • Mutual fund risk reporting — daily VaR disclosure.
  • Benchmark-relative VaR — active risk vs. index.
  • Drawdown forecasting — VaR over multiple horizons.
  • Liquidity-adjusted VaR — accounts for wide bid-ask spreads.
  • Stress-VaR — VaR under hypothetical crisis scenarios.

🏭 Non-Financial Applications

  • Energy trading — power, gas, oil price risk.
  • Supply chain — VaR on procurement costs.
  • Insurance — VaR on reserves, underwriting risk.
  • Real estate — property portfolio VaR.
  • Crypto funds — 24/7 VaR with heavy tails.

📊 Data Science & ML

  • Quantile regression — VaR as a special case of quantile prediction.
  • ML-based VaR — neural nets for non-linear portfolios.
  • Reinforcement learning — VaR-constrained trading agents.
  • Anomaly detection — VaR breaches signal regime change.
  • LLM evaluation — VaR-like tail metrics for model risk.
Why is VaR so dominant? Because it answers a specific question — "How much can we lose?" — with a single number. Regulators, CEOs, and boards love single numbers. The downside is that VaR hides what happens in the tail beyond that number, which is where catastrophic losses live. That's why ES (Expected Shortfall) is now becoming the regulatory standard.

3 Advantages

  • Single-number summary — VaR condenses an entire loss distribution into one number that executives, boards, and regulators can understand immediately.
  • Regulatory acceptance — Basel Committee endorsed VaR in 1996; it's the standard for market risk capital worldwide.
  • Comparable across portfolios — VaR lets you compare the risk of a bond portfolio to an equity portfolio to a currency book on the same scale (rupees).
  • Aggregates risks coherently — correlation-aware; recognises diversification benefits unlike naive sum-of-notionals.
  • Flexible confidence levels — α can be tuned from 90% to 99.9% depending on regulatory or management needs.
  • Multiple time horizons — 1-day VaR for trading, 10-day for regulatory, 1-year for pension risk.
  • Works for any asset class — equities, fixed income, FX, commodities, credit, crypto, real estate.
  • Simple for linear portfolios — under Gaussian assumptions, parametric VaR reduces to a matrix multiplication.
  • Extensible to other risk measures — Marginal VaR, Component VaR, Incremental VaR, ES all derived from the same framework.
  • Natural limit framework — "Desk A has a ₹5 Cr VaR limit" is a straightforward, enforceable policy.
  • Foundation for risk budgeting — VaR contributions let you allocate capital to strategies by risk, not just by notional.
  • Extensive tooling available — every major risk system (Murex, Calypso, Bloomberg, RiskMetrics) supports VaR natively.

4 Disadvantages

  • Ignores the tail beyond VaR — VaR tells you the cutoff, not what happens past it. Two portfolios with identical VaR can have wildly different tail risk. ES fixes this.
  • Not sub-additive (not coherent) — a classic counterexample: two risky bonds each with VaR = 0 can have combined VaR > 0. This violates the sub-additivity axiom of coherent risk measures.
  • Assumes normal returns (parametric) — financial returns have fat tails and skew. Gaussian VaR underestimates tail risk by 30–50% in crisis periods.
  • Historical method fails under regime change — if the last N days were calm, VaR looks small right before a crash.
  • Monte Carlo is computationally expensive — pricing exotic derivatives across 100,000 paths takes hours; not feasible for intraday risk.
  • Sensitive to correlation assumptions — correlations rise sharply in crises ("correlation breakdown"); models calibrated to calm periods understate joint losses.
  • Time-varying volatility ignored (basic versions) — GARCH effects mean volatility clusters; a static VaR model is often wrong.
  • Illiquid positions are hard to model — no continuous price data means historical VaR can't be computed reliably. Liquidity-adjusted VaR needed.
  • Model risk is significant — the 2008 crisis revealed that VaR models badly underestimated subprime mortgage risk.
  • Backtesting is noisy — at 99%, you expect ~2.5 breaches per year of daily data; distinguishing bad luck from bad model is statistically hard.
  • Procyclical — VaR falls in calm markets (encouraging leverage) and spikes in crises (forcing deleveraging); it amplifies cycles.
  • Confidence level ambiguity — 95%, 99%, 99.9%? Different choices give wildly different numbers; cross-firm comparison is difficult.
  • Horizon scaling is crude — the √T rule assumes i.i.d. returns; autocorrelation and mean reversion break this assumption.
  • No information on the shape of losses — VaR can't tell you whether the worst 1% is 2× VaR or 20× VaR.
  • Gaming risk — traders can choose positions that minimise VaR while increasing tail risk (short volatility, sell tail insurance).
The tail trap: Imagine two portfolios with identical 99% VaR = ₹10 Cr. Portfolio A: worst 1% is uniformly distributed between ₹10–₹12 Cr (ES ≈ ₹11 Cr). Portfolio B: worst 1% is ₹10 Cr 99% of the time but ₹500 Cr once a decade (ES ≈ ₹20 Cr). VaR says they're the same risk. ES, stress tests, and scenario analysis reveal they're worlds apart. This is why Basel IV moved to ES.

5 Pre-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Before deploying a VaR model, you must validate its accuracy, calibration, and robustness. These KPIs are computed on historical or simulated data where the model can be tested against reality.

5.1 Accuracy KPIs

Violation Ratio (VR) Accuracy

What it measures: The ratio of observed VaR breaches to expected breaches. The single most important VaR accuracy metric.

Formula VR = Observed breaches / Expected breaches

Expected breaches = ( 1 − α ) × T

T = number of backtesting days
α = confidence level (e.g. 0.99)
Worked example: 99% VaR, T = 500 days. Expected breaches = 0.01 × 500 = 5.
If actual breaches = 4 → VR = 4 / 5 = 0.80
Interpretation scale:
• VR ∈ [0.8, 1.2] → well-calibrated ✅
• VR < 0.8 → VaR too conservative (overestimates risk)
• VR > 1.2 → VaR too aggressive (underestimates risk) ⚠️
• VR > 2.0 → model is failing
✔ Use when You need a quick calibration check. This is the first metric regulators look at.
✘ Avoid when T is small (< 250 days) — sampling noise dominates.

Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) Accuracy

What it measures: Average difference between VaR predictions and realised losses on breach days — measures the magnitude error, not just frequency.

Formula MAPE = (1/T) · Σt | VaRt − Losst | / Losst   (over breach days)
Interpretation: Lower is better. < 10% = excellent. > 30% = the VaR magnitude is systematically off.

Bias Statistic Accuracy

What it measures: Whether VaR is systematically too high or too low.

Formula Bias = (1/T) · Σt ( Losst − VaRt )

Bias > 0 → losses systematically exceed VaR (underestimate)
Bias < 0 → VaR is systematically too conservative

5.2 Backtesting KPIs — Statistical Tests

Kupiec POF Test Backtesting

What it measures: Whether the observed breach frequency is statistically consistent with the expected frequency. The regulatory standard.

Null hypothesis H₀: actual breach rate = expected breach rate

Likelihood ratio statistic LRPOF = −2 · ln[ (1−p)T−x · px ] + 2 · ln[ (1−x/T)T−x · (x/T)x ]

p = expected breach probability = 1 − α
x = observed number of breaches
T = number of observations

LRPOF ~ χ²(1) under H₀
Worked example: p = 0.01, T = 500, x = 12 observed breaches.
Expected = 5. Observed = 12 (2.4×).
LRPOF ≈ 6.6, p-value ≈ 0.010 → reject H₀ at 1% level. Model underestimates risk.
Interpretation:
• p-value > 0.05 → cannot reject H₀ (model OK) ✅
• p-value < 0.05 → model is mis-calibrated ⚠️
• p-value < 0.01 → model is seriously broken 🚨
✔ Use when You need a formal statistical test for regulatory reporting.
✘ Avoid when T is very small or when breaches cluster (see Christoffersen test).

Christoffersen Independence Test Backtesting

What it measures: Whether breaches are independent over time. Cluster of breaches = model misses regime changes.

Transition counts nij = number of transitions from state i to state j
(0 = no breach, 1 = breach)

LR statistic LRIND = −2 · ln[ (1−π)n₀₀+n₁₀ · πn₀₁+n₁₁ ] + 2 · ln[ (1−π₀)n₀₀ · π₀n₀₁ · (1−π₁)n₁₀ · π₁n₁₁ ]

π = (n₀₁ + n₁₁) / T
π₀ = n₀₁ / (n₀₀ + n₀₁)
π₁ = n₁₁ / (n₁₀ + n₁₁)

LRIND ~ χ²(1)
Interpretation: p-value < 0.05 → breaches cluster; the model misses volatility regimes.

Christoffersen Conditional Coverage (CC) Test Backtesting

What it measures: Joint test of unconditional coverage (frequency) and independence. The most complete backtest.

Formula LRCC = LRPOF + LRIND   ~ χ²(2)
Interpretation: Rejects only if the model fails on either frequency OR independence (or both).

TRA (Traffic Light) Test Backtesting

What it measures: Basel's regulatory backtest — classifies models into green/yellow/red zones based on the number of breaches in 250 days.

Basel zones (99% VaR, 250 days, expected = 2.5) Green: 0–4 breaches (model accepted)
Yellow: 5–9 breaches (capital multiplier increased)
Red: 10+ breaches (model rejected, regulator intervention)

Capital multiplier mc = 3 + (yellow zone penalty from 0.00 to 0.85)
Interpretation: Green zone = trust the model. Yellow = penalty. Red = replace the model.

5.3 Model Quality KPIs

Expected Shortfall (ES) Backtest Model Quality

What it measures: Whether the average loss on breach days matches the predicted ES.

Formula ESactual = ( 1 / #breaches ) · Σbreach days Losst

Ratio ES ratio = ESactual / ESpredicted
Interpretation: Ratio ∈ [0.8, 1.2] = well-calibrated. Ratio > 1.2 = tail underestimated. Ratio < 0.8 = tail overestimated.

Tail Ratio (ES / VaR) Model Quality

What it measures: How severe the tail is beyond VaR. High ratio = fat tails.

Formula Tail Ratio = ESα / VaRα
Interpretation:
• ~1.15–1.25 → Normal-ish tails
• ~1.25–1.5 → Fat tails
• > 1.5 → Very heavy tails; VaR alone is dangerously misleading

Stress Test Coverage Model Quality

What it measures: Whether the model's VaR is exceeded in historical crisis scenarios (2008, COVID-19 crash, etc.).

Formula ST coverage = # crisis scenarios where VaR is breached / total scenarios
Interpretation: VaR is a normal-times measure; a good model should still breach under severe stress. If it doesn't, it's too conservative.

6 Post-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Once a VaR model is live and used for capital, limits, or reporting, you must continuously monitor whether it still reflects current market conditions. Regulators require daily backtesting, and the model must be re-validated regularly.

6.1 Continuous Backtesting KPIs

Rolling 250-day Breach Count Regulatory

What it measures: The Basel standard — number of breaches in the trailing 250 trading days.

Formula Rolling breachest = Σs=t−249t 1{ Losss > VaRs }

Expected under correct model = 0.01 × 250 = 2.5
Basel traffic light zones:
• 0–4 → Green (model OK)
• 5–9 → Yellow (capital multiplier increased)
• 10+ → Red (model rejected)

Rolling Violation Ratio Continuous

What it measures: A moving version of the VR metric to detect calibration drift.

Formula VRt = ( # breaches in window ) / ( (1 − α) × window length )
Interpretation: Watch the trend. A steadily rising VR is the earliest warning that the model is falling behind.

Breach Magnitude Trend Continuous

What it measures: Whether breaches are getting larger (severity) even if frequency is stable.

Formula Severityt = mean( Lossbreach − VaRbreach )

Track over a rolling window of 60 days.
Interpretation: Rising severity with stable frequency means the tail is getting fatter — a leading indicator of crisis.

CUSUM on Losses Continuous

What it measures: Cumulative sum of standardised losses — detects systematic bias quickly.

Formula CUSUMt = max( 0, CUSUMt−1 + ( Losst / VaRt ) − k )

k = 1 (neutral threshold)

Alert when CUSUM crosses h (typically 5–10)

6.2 Model Health KPIs

Volatility Forecast Accuracy Model Health

What it measures: Whether the volatility input to the parametric VaR model is accurate.

RMSE on volatility RMSEσ = √[ (1/T) · Σt ( σpred,t − σrealised,t )² ]

QLIKE loss (robust to noise) QLIKE = (1/T) · Σt [ σ²realised,t / σ²pred,t − log( σ²realised,t / σ²pred,t ) − 1 ]
Interpretation: Lower is better. Use QLIKE as it's robust to the noise in realised volatility estimates.

Correlation Stability Model Health

What it measures: Whether the correlation matrix used in the VaR model still matches reality. Correlations rise in crises.

Correlation drift Driftij = | ρtrainij − ρcurrentij |

Aggregate drift Frobenius norm = || Σtrain − Σcurrent ||F / || Σtrain ||F
Interpretation: Drift > 0.3 suggests the correlation structure has shifted — recalibrate.

Distribution Drift (PSI) Model Health

What it measures: Whether the distribution of returns has shifted from the calibration window.

Population Stability Index PSI = Σi ( A%i − E%i ) · ln( A%i / E%i )

Kolmogorov-Smirnov KS = maxx | Ftrain(x) − Fcurrent(x) |
Interpretation:
• PSI < 0.10 → distribution stable ✅
• 0.10 ≤ PSI < 0.25 → moderate shift, monitor ⚠️
• PSI ≥ 0.25 → significant drift, recalibrate 🚨

Model Version Stability Model Health

What it measures: Whether a model upgrade changes VaR significantly.

Formula ΔVaR = | VaRnew − VaRold | / VaRold
Interpretation: ΔVaR < 10% = minor upgrade. ΔVaR > 30% = significant change; requires formal re-validation.

6.3 Operational KPIs

Daily Calculation Latency Operational

What it measures: Time from market close to production VaR availability.

Formula Latency = VaRavailable time − Marketclose time
Target: Parametric: < 5 minutes. Historical: < 30 minutes. Monte Carlo: < 2 hours (must complete overnight).

Calculation Failure Rate Operational

Formula Failure rate = failed VaR runs / total runs
Interpretation: Should be < 0.5%. Common failures: missing market data, stale positions, covariance matrix not positive definite.

Data Quality Score Operational

What it measures: Completeness and freshness of input data.

Formula DQ score = 1 − ( missing prices + stale prices ) / total inputs
Interpretation: DQ > 0.99 required for reliable VaR.

6.4 Business & Regulatory KPIs

Regulatory Capital Multiplier Business

What it measures: The Basel multiplier applied to VaR to determine capital. Driven by backtest zone.

Formula Capital = max( VaRt−1, mc × avg( VaR60 days ) )

mc = 3 + yellow zone penalty (0.00 to 0.85)
Interpretation: Green zone → mc = 3. Yellow zone → up to 3.85. Each yellow-zone breach costs real capital.

Limit Utilisation Business

What it measures: How close each desk is to its VaR limit.

Formula Utilisation = VaRcurrent / VaRlimit
Interpretation: Utilisation > 90% for sustained periods means either limits are too tight or the desk is taking too much risk. Target: 60–80%.

Cost of VaR Capital Business

Formula Cost = Regulatory Capital × Cost of Equity (ROE)

Lower VaR → lower capital → lower cost.
Interpretation: Model improvements that reduce VaR directly reduce the cost of capital — a hard, measurable ROI.

Stress Loss Coverage Business

What it measures: Whether the trading book can survive a hypothetical 2008-style crisis.

Formula Coverage = Capital / Stress Loss
Interpretation: Coverage > 1.5 = comfortable headroom. Coverage < 1.0 = a severe stress would breach capital.
Golden rule: VaR is a snapshot of current risk under normal conditions. It must be paired with (1) daily backtests — the rolling breach count — (2) stress tests for extreme events, and (3) ES or tail metrics to capture what's beyond VaR. A VaR model without monitoring is a fire alarm without batteries.

7 Mathematical Formulas — How VaR Actually Works

The core idea: VaR is a quantile of the loss distribution. Different methods differ in how they estimate that distribution.

7.1 Definition of VaR

General definition VaRα( L ) = inf { x ∈ ℝ : P( L > x ) ≤ 1 − α }

Equivalently: VaRα = the α-quantile of the loss distribution L

In terms of P&L (profit/loss) If P&L is normally distributed with mean μP and standard deviation σP:

VaRα = − μP + zα · σP

where zα = Φ−1( α ) is the α-quantile of the standard normal.

7.2 Parametric (Variance-Covariance) VaR

Single-asset, 1-day VaR VaRα = zα · σ · V

σ = daily return volatility
V = portfolio value

Portfolio VaR (matrix form) VaRα = zα · √( wᵀ Σ w ) · V

w = vector of portfolio weights
Σ = covariance matrix of asset returns

Multi-horizon scaling (√T rule) VaRα( T days ) = VaRα( 1 day ) · √T

This assumes returns are i.i.d. — often violated in practice.

Common zα values α = 90% → z = 1.282
α = 95% → z = 1.645
α = 99% → z = 2.326
α = 99.9% → z = 3.090

7.3 Historical Simulation VaR

Algorithm Given historical returns r1, r2, …, rN (last N days):

1. Revalue the current portfolio under each historical scenario:
P&Li = Portfolio( St · (1 + ri) ) − Portfolio( St )

2. Sort P&L1:N ascending: P&L(1) ≤ P&L(2) ≤ … ≤ P&L(N)

3. VaRα = − P&L(⌈N · (1−α)⌉)

For α = 99% and N = 500: VaR = −P&L(5) (5th worst loss)

7.4 Monte Carlo VaR

Algorithm 1. Specify a factor model (e.g. GBM, jump-diffusion)
2. Simulate M scenarios of factor returns
3. Revalue the portfolio in each scenario
4. VaRα = empirical α-quantile of simulated P&L

GBM path simulation St+Δt = St · exp[ ( μ − σ²/2 ) · Δt + σ · √Δt · Z ]
Z ~ N( 0, 1 )

Required M for target error M ≈ ( zα / ε )² · σ²P&L

Monte Carlo error ~ σP&L / √M

7.5 Expected Shortfall (ES / CVaR)

Definition ESα = E[ L | L > VaRα ]

= ( 1 / (1−α) ) · ∫α1 VaRu du

Parametric ES (normal) ESα = σ · φ( zα ) / ( 1 − α ) − μ

φ = standard normal PDF

Key property: ES is coherent Unlike VaR, ES is sub-additive: ES(A+B) ≤ ES(A) + ES(B)
This makes ES well-behaved for portfolio risk aggregation.

7.6 Incremental, Marginal, and Component VaR

Incremental VaR (IVaR) IVaRi = VaR( w + Δwi ) − VaR( w )
Risk added or removed by changing position i

Marginal VaR (MVaR) MVaRi = ∂VaR / ∂wi
Under Gaussian assumptions:
MVaRi = zα · ( Σw )i / √( wᵀΣw )

Component VaR (CVaR) CVaRi = wi · MVaRi

Euler's theorem (VaR is homogeneous of degree 1) VaR = Σi CVaRi
This lets you attribute portfolio VaR to each position cleanly.

7.7 Backtesting Statistics

Kupiec POF test LRPOF = −2 · ln[ (1−p)T−x · px ] + 2 · ln[ (1−x/T)T−x · (x/T)x ]
~ χ²(1)

Christoffersen independence LRIND = −2 · ln[ (1−π)n₀₀+n₁₀ · πn₀₁+n₁₁ ] + 2 · ln[ (1−π₀)n₀₀ · π₀n₀₁ · (1−π₁)n₁₀ · π₁n₁₁ ]
~ χ²(1)

Conditional coverage (joint test) LRCC = LRPOF + LRIND   ~ χ²(2)

Standard error of breach rate SE( p̂ ) = √[ p(1−p) / T ]
Under H₀, breach count ~ Binomial( T, p )

7.8 GARCH-Based VaR (Dynamic Volatility)

GARCH(1,1) σ²t = ω + α · ε²t−1 + β · σ²t−1

εt = σt · zt,   zt ~ i.i.d. N(0,1)

Constraints ω > 0, α ≥ 0, β ≥ 0, α + β < 1 (for stationarity)

Unconditional variance σ²∞ = ω / (1 − α − β)

Dynamic 1-day VaR VaRt = zα · σ̂t · V

Filtered Historical Simulation 1. Fit GARCH to returns
2. Extract standardised residuals zt = εt / σt
3. Resample zt and reapply current σt
4. VaRt = zαempirical · σ̂t · V

7.9 Extreme Value Theory (EVT) for Tail VaR

Pickands-Balkema-de Haan theorem For large threshold u, excess losses follow a generalised Pareto distribution (GPD):

P( L − u > y | L > u ) → Gξ,β( y )

GPD CDF Gξ,β( y ) = 1 − ( 1 + ξ y / β )−1/ξ   if ξ ≠ 0
Gξ,β( y ) = 1 − exp( −y / β )   if ξ = 0

Tail VaR estimator VaRα = u + ( β / ξ ) · [ ( ( N / Nu ) · ( 1 − α ) )−ξ − 1 ]

N = total observations
Nu = number of losses exceeding u
ξ = tail index (ξ > 0 = fat tail)

7.10 Coherent Risk Measures

A risk measure is coherent if it satisfies four axioms. VaR fails one of them; ES satisfies all four.

Four coherence axioms 1. Monotonicity: If X ≤ Y, then ρ(X) ≥ ρ(Y)
2. Translation invariance: ρ(X + c) = ρ(X) − c
3. Positive homogeneity: ρ(λX) = λ ρ(X) for λ > 0
4. Sub-additivity: ρ(X + Y) ≤ ρ(X) + ρ(Y)   ← VaR FAILS this

VaR counterexample (sub-additivity) Two independent digital bonds:
- Each pays ₹100 unless a specific default occurs (prob 0.9%)
- 99% VaR of each alone = ₹0 (default probability < 1%)
- Jointly, the probability that at least one defaults is ~1.8% > 1%
- Combined 99% VaR = ₹100 > 0 + 0
→ VaR( A + B ) = 100 > VaR(A) + VaR(B) = 0

8 Glossary of Acronyms & Terms

Every abbreviation used in this study guide, explained in plain English.

Core VaR Concepts

VaR
Value at Risk — the maximum loss expected over a given horizon at a given confidence level.
ES
Expected Shortfall — the average loss beyond VaR. Also called Conditional VaR (CVaR) or Average VaR.
CVaR
Conditional Value at Risk — same as ES. Not to be confused with Component VaR.
Confidence Level (α)
The probability that losses will not exceed VaR. Common: 95%, 99%, 99.9%.
Horizon (T)
The time period over which VaR is measured. Common: 1 day, 10 days, 1 year.
Loss Distribution
The probability distribution of portfolio losses over the horizon.
Quantile
The value below which a given fraction of the distribution lies.
Tail Risk
Risk of extreme losses beyond VaR.
Fat Tails
Distributions with more extreme events than a normal distribution predicts. Also called leptokurtic.

VaR Methodologies

Historical Simulation
VaR computed from empirical historical return scenarios.
Parametric VaR
VaR under an assumed distribution (usually normal). Also called variance-covariance.
Monte Carlo VaR
VaR from simulated factor paths and full revaluation.
FHS
Filtered Historical Simulation — GARCH + resampled standardised residuals.
EVT
Extreme Value Theory — tail-focused statistical framework.
GPD
Generalised Pareto Distribution — model for excesses over a threshold.
GARCH
Generalised AutoRegressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity — time-varying volatility model.
EWMA
Exponentially Weighted Moving Average — volatility estimator giving more weight to recent data.
CAViaR
Conditional Autoregressive VaR — direct quantile regression for VaR.

VaR Attribution

Marginal VaR
The change in VaR per unit change in position size.
Component VaR
Marginal VaR × position size; sums to total VaR (Euler's theorem).
Incremental VaR
Change in VaR from adding or removing a position.
Diversification Benefit
VaR(portfolio) − Σ VaR(components); usually negative.
Undiversified VaR
Sum of individual VaRs — an upper bound on portfolio VaR.

Backtesting

Breach
A day when the actual loss exceeded VaR.
Violation
Synonym for breach.
Violation Ratio (VR)
Observed breaches / expected breaches.
Kupiec POF
Proportion of Failures test — statistical test for breach frequency.
Christoffersen Independence
Test for independence of breaches.
Christoffersen CC
Conditional Coverage test — joint frequency + independence.
Traffic Light
Basel regulatory backtest — green/yellow/red zones.
Basel Multiplier
Capital multiplier (3.0 to 3.85) determined by backtest zone.
CUSUM
Cumulative Sum — sequential change detection.
Binomial Test
The distribution of breach counts under a correct model.

Statistical Concepts

i.i.d.
Independent and Identically Distributed — returns are uncorrelated and drawn from the same distribution.
Normal Distribution
The Gaussian — mean μ, standard deviation σ.
Student-t
A fat-tailed symmetric distribution with degrees of freedom ν.
Skewness
Asymmetry of a distribution. Negative skew = left tail is fatter.
Kurtosis
Tail-heaviness. Excess kurtosis > 0 = fat tails.
Volatility Clustering
The tendency for large moves to be followed by large moves.
Autocorrelation
Correlation of a series with its own lagged values.
Mean Reversion
The tendency of a series to return to its long-run mean.
Coherent Risk Measure
A risk measure satisfying monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity, and sub-additivity.
Sub-additivity
ρ(A+B) ≤ ρ(A) + ρ(B) — VaR fails this; ES satisfies it.

Monitoring & Operations

KPI
Key Performance Indicator — a measurable value tracking model or business performance.
PSI
Population Stability Index — measures distribution shift.
KS Test
Kolmogorov-Smirnov test — tests whether two distributions differ.
KL Divergence
Kullback-Leibler divergence — asymmetric distribution difference.
QLIKE
A robust loss function for comparing volatility forecasts.
RMSE
Root Mean Squared Error — standard forecast accuracy metric.
Latency
Time to compute the VaR after market close.
Throughput
Number of VaR calculations per unit time (across books, scenarios).
Data Quality Score
Fraction of complete, fresh market data inputs.

Regulatory & Business

Basel
The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision — sets global bank capital standards.

Introduced VaR-based market risk capital (1996 amendment, refined 2004).

Post-2008 reforms — higher capital, liquidity ratios.

Fundamental Review of the Trading Book — replaces VaR with ES at 97.5%.
FRTB
Fundamental Review of the Trading Book — Basel IV's market risk framework.

Internal Models Approach — using the bank's own VaR/ES models for regulatory capital.

Standardised Approach — regulatory-prescribed risk calculation without internal models.
Limit Utilisation
Current VaR / VaR limit for a trading desk.
Capital Multiplier
Basel factor (3.0–3.85) multiplied by average VaR to derive capital.
Stress Test
Simulating portfolio loss under hypothetical extreme scenarios.
Reverse Stress Test
Finding the scenario that would wipe out the firm.
Scenario Analysis
Evaluating impact of specific named scenarios (2008, COVID-19, etc.).

Financial Instruments

Linear Position
A position whose value changes linearly with the underlying (stocks, bonds).
Non-linear Position
Options, structured products — value changes non-linearly.
Greek
Sensitivity of an option's price to a parameter (Delta, Gamma, Vega, Theta, Rho).
Delta
Sensitivity to underlying price. Delta-normal VaR uses this.
Gamma
Second-order sensitivity. Ignored by basic Delta-Normal; causes underestimation.
Vega
Sensitivity to implied volatility.
Path-Dependent
Payoff depends on the entire price trajectory (Asian, lookback, barrier options).
Full Revaluation
Repricing the full instrument under each scenario (accurate).
Delta Approximation
Approximating scenario P&L from sensitivities (fast but less accurate).

Final tip: VaR is the most widely used risk measure in the world, but it is not the whole story. Always (1) pair VaR with ES to capture the tail, (2) backtest daily — Kupiec, Christoffersen, and Basel traffic light, (3) stress test with historical crisis scenarios, (4) monitor volatility, correlation, and distribution drift continuously, and (5) treat VaR as one input to a broader risk framework, not a substitute for judgement. The 2008 crisis taught us that a VaR number alone — however precise — can be dangerously misleading.
