01Foundations & Descriptive Statistics

Summarising data with center, spread, shape, and the right transformations.

1.1 Population vs Sample

  • Population — the complete set of units you want a statement about (every adult in the country, every transaction this year). Its numeric properties are parameters: fixed but unknown. Examples: μ (mean), σ (standard deviation), p (proportion).
  • Sample — the subset you actually measure. Its summaries are statistics and are random variables: x̄ (sample mean), s (sample SD), p̂ (sample proportion). Draw a different sample and every one of them changes.
  • Inference — the reasoning step that goes from a statistic back to a parameter. It always carries uncertainty, because a sample is only a partial look at the population.
  • Sampling frame — the practical list you draw from (a phone registry, a customer table). If the frame fails to cover the population you get coverage error, which no amount of modelling can repair.
  • Precision scales as √n — the standard error of x̄ is σ/√n, so quadrupling the sample only halves the error. Sampling effort has sharply diminishing returns.
  • Pitfall — a huge biased sample is worse than a small honest one: it yields a tight interval around the wrong answer and manufactures false confidence.
Python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
population = rng.normal(loc=50, scale=10, size=1_000_000)
sample = rng.choice(population, size=100, replace=False)
print(f"Population mu={population.mean():.2f}, sigma={population.std():.2f}")
print(f"Sample xbar={sample.mean():.2f}, s={sample.std(ddof=1):.2f}")

1.2 Types of Data (Stevens' Scales)

ScalePropertyExampleValid summaries
NominalCategories, no orderCity, colourMode, counts
OrdinalOrdered, unequal gapsRating 1–5Median, percentiles
IntervalEqual gaps, no true zeroTemperature °CMean, SD
RatioEqual gaps, true zeroHeight, incomeAll summaries, ratios
Why 20°C ≠ twice 10°CCelsius is interval, not ratio. Zero is arbitrary.
  • Why the scale matters — arithmetic is only meaningful where the scale permits it. Averages of nominal labels (“mean city”) are nonsense; ratios of interval values (“twice as hot”) are equally meaningless.
  • Choose the summary the scale allows — nominal data supports mode and counts only; ordinal adds median and percentiles; interval adds mean and SD; ratio adds geometric means and coefficients of variation.
  • Encoding drives analysis — one-hot encode nominal variables, keep ordinal ones as ordered integers (or use ordinal regression), and never feed a nominal code like ZIP-as-number into a linear model as if it were continuous.
  • Discretising continuous data loses information — binning a ratio variable into “low/medium/high” throws away rank detail and can hide monotone effects or manufacture spurious ones.
  • Measurement determines validity — a thermometer with an arbitrary zero cannot support claims about proportional change, so the same statistical test can be legitimate on a ratio scale and invalid on an interval one.

1.3 Center

x̄ = (1/n) · Σi=1..n xᵢ
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
x̄Sample meanThe arithmetic average of the observations. It is the balancing point of the data: the sum of deviations (xᵢ − x̄) is always exactly zero. It is a statistic, so it changes from sample to sample and has its own sampling distribution (whose spread is the standard error).
nSample sizeThe number of observations actually included after handling missing values. Dividing by n (rather than n−1) is what makes x̄ an unbiased estimator of μ.
ΣSummationShorthand for “add up the following quantity over every observation”, here i = 1 to n.
xᵢi-th observationThe value of the variable for unit i. Assumed to be measured on an interval or ratio scale for the mean to be meaningful.
iIndexPosition of an observation in the dataset; it carries no meaning beyond identifying the unit.
  • Mean — the sum of values divided by how many there are. It uses every observation, which makes it efficient but also fragile: a single extreme value drags it away from the bulk of the data.
  • Median — the middle value once the data is sorted (the average of the two middle values when n is even). It is a robust statistic: it only cares about rank, so it is unaffected by how extreme an outlier is.
  • Mode — the most frequently occurring value. It is the only measure of center that is valid for nominal data, and a dataset can be bimodal or multimodal, which usually signals two mixed sub-populations.
  • Skew rule — for right-skewed data mean > median > mode; for left-skewed data the order reverses. The gap between mean and median is a quick, interpretable measure of skewness.
  • Trimmed mean — drop the top and bottom k% of values, then average. It buys robustness while still using most of the data, and is the standard center for athletic judging and robust location estimation.
  • Weighted mean — x̄w = Σwᵢxᵢ / Σwᵢ, where wᵢ encodes importance or inverse-probability weights. Use it for survey designs and for aggregating group means that rest on different sample sizes.
  • Choosing between them — report the mean for roughly symmetric data with no extreme values, the median when the distribution is skewed or contaminated, and both plus a boxplot whenever you are unsure.
Python
import pandas as pd
x = pd.Series([2, 3, 3, 4, 100])
print("mean:", x.mean(), "median:", x.median(), "mode:", x.mode()[0])

1.4 Spread

s² = Σ(xᵢ − x̄)² / (n − 1)  ·  s = √s²  ·  IQR = Q₃ − Q₁
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
s²Sample varianceThe average squared distance of the observations from the mean. Squaring removes sign and makes large deviations count disproportionately, but it also means the units are squared (e.g. €²), which is why we usually report s instead.
ΣSummationAdd the quantity in parentheses for every observation i = 1 … n.
xᵢi-th observationThe individual data value being compared with the mean.
x̄Sample meanThe reference point of the spread. Note it is estimated from the very same data, which is what forces the n−1 correction.
(xᵢ − x̄)DeviationSigned distance from the mean. These deviations always sum to zero, so their raw average would be 0 and carries no information — hence the squaring.
nSample sizeNumber of observations used in the sum.
(n − 1)Degrees of freedomBessel's correction. Because x̄ was estimated from the same data, the deviations are systematically slightly too small; dividing by n−1 instead of n removes that downward bias so that E[s²] = σ².
sSample standard deviationThe square root of the variance, returning the spread to the original units. Roughly, a typical observation sits about s away from the mean.
Q₁, Q₃First / third quartileThe 25th and 75th percentiles — the values that cut off the lowest and highest quarter of the sorted data.
IQRInterquartile rangeQ₃ − Q₁, the width of the middle 50% of the data. Because it ignores the tails entirely it is highly resistant to outliers, unlike s.
  • Variance vs SD — variance is the mathematically convenient form (variances of independent variables add), while the standard deviation is the interpretable form because it lives in the original units.
  • Bessel's correction (n−1) — corrects the downward bias created by measuring deviations from an estimated mean rather than the true one; without it, s² systematically underestimates σ².
  • IQR and quantile spread — IQR is robust but ignores the tails, which is exactly where risk often lives; in finance and hydrology the tails are the story, so SD and quantile-based risk measures are used together.
  • MAD — median of |xᵢ − median(x)|. It is the most outlier-resistant common spread measure and pairs naturally with the median, just as SD pairs with the mean.
  • Range — max − min uses only two observations, so it is extremely unstable; it should never be the sole measure of spread.
  • Units and comparability — SD carries the unit of the variable (so you cannot compare a SD in kilograms with one in euros); divide by the mean to get the dimensionless coefficient of variation when you need to compare relative variability.
Python
import scipy.stats as st
x = np.array([1,2,3,4,5,100])
print("sd:", x.std(ddof=1), "IQR:", np.percentile(x,75)-np.percentile(x,25),
      "MAD:", st.median_abs_deviation(x))

1.5 Percentiles, Quantiles, Boxplots

  • p-th percentile — the value below which p% of the observations fall. Q₁ = 25th, Q₂ = median = 50th, Q₃ = 75th. Percentiles describe the shape of a distribution without assuming any particular form.
  • Quantile function — the inverse of the CDF: given a probability p it returns the value x such that F(x) = p. This is the same object as the percentile, just indexed by probability instead of percentage.
  • Boxplot anatomy — box = Q₁ to Q₃, thick line = median, whiskers extend to the most extreme points still within 1.5 × IQR, and anything beyond is drawn as a flagged outlier point.
  • Reading a boxplot — box length shows spread of the middle half, median position inside the box shows skew, and whisker asymmetry shows which tail is heavier. Side-by-side boxplots compare groups faster than any table.
  • Percentiles beat means for skewed data — income, latency and house prices are all better summarised by medians and 90th percentiles than by averages, because the average is dominated by a small tail.
  • Quartile computation varies — there are at least nine defensible definitions (R-1 … R-9) that disagree slightly on small samples, so always state which convention your software used before comparing numbers.

1.6 Shape

skewness g₁ = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)³ / s³  ·  excess kurtosis g₂ = (1/n) Σ(xᵢ − x̄)⁴ / s⁴ − 3
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
g₁Sample skewnessStandardised third moment. Positive means a long right tail (mean > median); negative means a long left tail; zero suggests symmetry. Values beyond roughly ±1 are usually described as strongly skewed.
g₂Excess kurtosisStandardised fourth moment with 3 subtracted so that the Normal distribution scores 0. Positive values mean heavier tails and a sharper peak (leptokurtic); negative means lighter tails (platykurtic).
xᵢi-th observationThe individual data value.
x̄Sample meanThe center used to form the deviations that get cubed or raised to the fourth power.
sSample standard deviationDividing by s³ (for skewness) and s⁴ (for kurtosis) makes the measures unit-free, so they can be compared across variables measured on different scales.
nSample sizeNumber of observations averaged over. Both measures are noisy in small samples — skewness and kurtosis need hundreds of observations before they stabilise.
3Kurtosis of a NormalThe raw (non-excess) fourth moment of a standard Normal is 3, which is why subtracting 3 re-centres the scale at zero.
  • Skewness — 0 = symmetric; positive = long right tail (income, latency, claim sizes); negative = long left tail (age at death, exam scores with a hard ceiling). It tells you which direction the mean is pulled.
  • Kurtosis — a Normal has kurtosis 3 and excess kurtosis 0. High excess kurtosis means heavy tails, so extreme events are far more likely than a Normal model predicts.
  • Skew versus outliers are different — a smooth long tail is genuine structure; a single rogue value is a data-quality question. Always plot before deciding which you are looking at.
  • Tail weight drives model choice — heavy-tailed data argues against assuming Normality; use Student-t likelihoods, robust losses (Huber), or quantile regression instead.
  • Distributional shape drives method choice — symmetric and light-tailed supports mean-based methods; skewed supports medians, log transforms, or GLMs; bimodal means you probably have two populations and should split the analysis.
  • Measures are fragile in small samples — skewness and especially kurtosis are themselves dominated by rare observations, so they need large n and should always be read alongside a histogram.

1.7 Covariance and Correlation

cov(X,Y) = E[(X − μₓ)(Y − μᵧ)]  ·  r = cov(X,Y) / (σₓ σᵧ)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
cov(X,Y)CovarianceThe average product of the deviations of X and Y from their own means. It is positive when the two variables tend to be above/below their means together, negative when one is high while the other is low. Its size depends on the units of both variables, so it is not comparable across datasets.
E[·]ExpectationThe long-run average value of the quantity in brackets over the population. In practice it is estimated by the sample average of the products of deviations.
X, YRandom variablesThe two quantities whose joint movement you are measuring. They may be measured on completely different scales.
μₓ, μᵧPopulation meansThe reference centers of X and Y. Subtracting them turns each observation into a signed deviation, which is what makes the product interpretable as “moving together or apart”.
rPearson correlationCovariance rescaled by the product of the standard deviations, which removes the units of both variables and constrains the result to [−1, 1]. It measures the strength of the linear relationship only.
σₓ, σᵧPopulation standard deviationsThe spread of X and of Y. Dividing by them is exactly the same as standardising both variables first and then computing the covariance.
ρ (Spearman)Rank correlationPearson correlation computed on ranks rather than raw values, so it measures any monotone relationship and resists outliers.
τ (Kendall)Concordance correlationBased on the proportion of pairs in concordant order minus discordant order. More robust and better behaved than Spearman on small samples.
  • Pearson r — measures linear association only; it answers “how well does a straight line describe this cloud?” and is insensitive to everything a straight line cannot capture.
  • Spearman ρ — Pearson computed on ranks, so it captures any monotone relationship, tolerates outliers, and works on ordinal data. Report it whenever the relationship might be curved but still increasing.
  • Kendall τ — based on concordant versus discordant pairs, giving a probability-like interpretation (“a random pair agrees in order”). Preferred for small samples and heavily tied data.
  • Correlation is not causation — a strong r tells you two variables move together, not why. Confounding, reverse causation and selection effects all produce correlations with no causal content.
  • Restriction of range — computing r on a narrow slice of the data (only rich customers, only fast servers) artificially deflates it, which is why correlations from truncated samples mislead.
  • Warning — r = 0 does not mean independence. Y = X² gives r ≈ 0 despite a perfectly deterministic relationship, because the dependence is symmetric and nonlinear.

1.8 Visualising Distributions

  • Histogram — counts of observations per bin. Its shape depends on the bin width you choose, so always try several widths before drawing conclusions; too-wide bins hide structure, too-narrow bins show noise.
  • KDE — a smooth density estimate built by placing a small kernel (usually Gaussian) on each observation and summing. It avoids arbitrary bin edges but introduces its own bandwidth choice.
  • ECDF — F(x) = the fraction of data ≤ x, drawn as a step function. It needs no tuning parameters at all, which makes it the most honest single-plot summary of a distribution.
  • Q-Q plot — plots sample quantiles against theoretical ones; points on a straight line mean the assumed distribution fits. It is the standard diagnostic for normality and is far more informative than a histogram for tail behaviour.
  • Violin / strip plots — combine density shape with the individual observations, which matters because two groups with identical boxplots can hide very different multimodal structures.
  • Plot before you compute — Anscombe's quartet and the Datasaurus show four datasets with identical means, variances and correlations but completely different shapes. A summary statistic is not a substitute for a picture.

1.9 Outliers and Robust Statistics

  • What an outlier is — a point that sits far from the bulk of the data. It is defined relative to a model of “typical”, so the same value can be an outlier in one analysis and perfectly ordinary in another.
  • Detection rules — the 1.5 × IQR rule (outside the boxplot whiskers) and |z| > 3 for roughly symmetric data. Both are conventions, not laws; the IQR rule is far safer on skewed data because it does not rely on a mean and SD that the outliers themselves have corrupted.
  • Never delete automatically — investigate first: is it a data-entry error, a unit mix-up, a genuine rare event, or a member of a different population? Each answer implies a different action (fix, keep, model separately, or use a heavier-tailed model).
  • Robust tools — median, IQR, MAD, trimmed mean, winsorisation (cap rather than drop) and Huber loss (quadratic near zero, linear in the tails) all limit the influence of extreme values.
  • Outliers can be the signal — in fraud, fault detection and medical monitoring the extremes are precisely what you are paid to find. Treating them as noise destroys the product.
  • State your rule and report sensitivity — any exclusion changes the answer, so document the criterion and show the result both with and without the excluded points.

1.10 Standardisation and Normalisation

z = (x − μ) / σ  ·  x' = (x − min) / (max − min)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
zStandard scoreThe number of standard deviations the observation lies above (positive) or below (negative) the mean. It is unit-free, so z-scores from different variables become directly comparable.
xRaw valueThe original observation, in its own units.
μMean of the training dataThe center subtracted off. In production this must be the value computed on the training set only; recomputing it on new data leaks information and makes results unreproducible.
σStandard deviation of the training dataThe scale divided out. After division the variable has SD exactly 1, so no feature can dominate a distance or gradient calculation purely because of its units.
x'Min-max scaled valueRescaled linearly so that the smallest training value maps to 0 and the largest maps to 1. It preserves the shape of the distribution but not the relative spacing of the mean.
min, maxTraining extremesThe bounds used for the rescaling. They are extremely sensitive to outliers — one extreme value compresses every other observation into a narrow band — and values outside the training range fall outside [0, 1] at prediction time.
  • Why scale at all — distance-based and gradient-based methods (k-NN, SVM, k-means, PCA, neural networks, ridge/lasso) treat a one-unit change in any feature as equally important, so a feature measured in thousands silently overwhelms one measured in fractions.
  • Standardisation (z-score) — subtract the mean, divide by the SD. Output has mean 0 and SD 1 but is unbounded and keeps the shape of the original distribution, including its skew.
  • Normalisation (min-max) — maps to a fixed range, usually [0, 1]. Choose it when you need bounded inputs (image pixels, certain neural-network activations) and when the data has no extreme outliers.
  • Robust alternatives — scaling by the median and IQR, or applying a quantile/rank transform, avoids letting outliers define the scale. Prefer these for skewed, heavy-tailed features.
  • Tree models do not need it — decision trees, random forests and gradient boosting split on order only, so any monotone rescaling leaves them unchanged. This is a genuine practical advantage on mixed-unit tabular data.
  • Always refit inside the CV loop — scaling parameters must be learned from the training fold only; computing them once on the full dataset before splitting leaks test information into training and inflates your reported score.
PitfallFit the scaler on training data only, then apply to test. Otherwise leakage.

Real-life Applications

  • EDA for house prices — the mean sale price sits far above the median because a handful of mansions dominate it. Reporting the median and the 90th percentile communicates the market far more honestly.
  • Fraud and anomaly detection — IQR and z-score rules flag unusual transactions and sensor readings; in both domains the anomaly is the finding, so suppression must never be automatic.
  • Feature scaling in ML pipelines — standardise before k-NN, SVM, PCA, ridge/lasso and neural networks, but refit the scaler inside each cross-validation fold to avoid leakage.
  • Clinical reference ranges — population percentiles (5th–95th) define “normal” for biomarkers such as cholesterol; a patient is judged against the distribution, not against a single mean.
  • Service-level objectives — API latency is heavily right-skewed, so teams track p50 (typical user) and p99 (worst experience) rather than the average, which no real user ever experiences.
  • Salary benchmarking — HR analysts use medians and quartiles by job family precisely because a single executive salary would distort any mean-based comparison.

02Probability

Quantify uncertainty and update beliefs with evidence.

2.1 Axioms

  • Sample space Ω — the set of every possible outcome of the experiment. For a die roll Ω = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}; for a customer's lifetime value Ω = [0, ∞).
  • Event A — any subset of Ω you care about, written A ⊆ Ω. “Rolled an even number” is the event {2, 4, 6}.
  • Axiom 1 — non-negativity: P(A) ≥ 0. Probabilities are never negative, which rules out nonsense answers such as “−0.3 chance of rain”.
  • Axiom 2 — normalisation: P(Ω) = 1. Something must happen, so the probabilities over the whole sample space sum to exactly one.
  • Axiom 3 — countable additivity: for pairwise disjoint events P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B), extending to any countable collection. This single axiom is what lets you add probabilities at all.
  • Derived rules worth memorising — complement P(Aᶜ) = 1 − P(A); the general addition rule P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), where the subtraction removes the double-counted overlap; and monotonicity, A ⊆ B ⟹ P(A) ≤ P(B).

2.2 Conditional, Independence, Mutual Exclusivity

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B)  ·  Independent: P(A ∩ B) = P(A) · P(B)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
P(A | B)Conditional probabilityThe probability of A given that B is known to have happened. The conditioning shrinks the universe from Ω down to B, so every probability is renormalised within that smaller world.
A, BEventsTwo subsets of the sample space. The order matters: P(A | B) and P(B | A) are generally different numbers, which is the single most common source of probabilistic confusion.
A ∩ BIntersection“Both A and B occur”. It is the only part of A that survives once we know B happened.
P(B)Marginal probability of BThe denominator. It must be strictly positive, otherwise the conditional probability is undefined.
P(A) · P(B)Independence testTwo events are independent exactly when the product rule holds, equivalently P(A | B) = P(A) — learning B tells you nothing about A.
Mutually exclusiveA ∩ B = ∅Both cannot happen together, so P(A ∩ B) = 0. With positive probabilities this forces dependence: knowing B occurred proves A did not.
Classic confusionMutually exclusive events with positive probability are dependent, not independent.
  • Conditioning reshapes the sample space — P(A | B) is just the probability of A measured inside the restricted world B, which is why it can differ dramatically from P(A).
  • Conditional is not causal — P(smoke | cancer) is not P(cancer | smoke). Confusing the two is called the transposed conditional, and it underlies most errors in interpreting tests and screening results.
  • Independence means zero information — A and B are independent when knowing one leaves the probability of the other unchanged; equivalently the joint probability factorises.
  • Independence is not the same as uncorrelated — zero correlation only excludes linear dependence; two variables may be independent but still dependent in a nonlinear way.
  • Chain rule — P(A₁ ∩ … ∩ Aₙ) = P(A₁) · P(A₂ | A₁) · … , which is how joint probabilities of sequences are built in HMMs and language models.
  • Law of total probability — P(B) = Σ P(B | Aᵢ)P(Aᵢ) over a partition {Aᵢ}. This supplies the denominator of Bayes' theorem.

2.3 Bayes' Theorem

P(H | D) = P(D | H) · P(H) / P(D)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
HHypothesisThe proposition being assessed — “the patient has the disease”, “the email is spam”, “the coin is fair”. In Bayesian terms it is anything whose plausibility you want to update.
DData / evidenceThe observation you just made — a positive test, a set of words, the sequence of flips. It is fixed once observed; the only thing that moves is the belief in H.
P(H)PriorBelief in H before seeing the data. It is not a guess: it encodes base rates, previous experiments or domain knowledge, and it is exactly the term people forget when they panic over a rare event.
P(D | H)LikelihoodHow plausible the observed data is if H were true. This is the term estimated from data-generating models and it is the link between statistics and machine learning.
P(H | D)PosteriorThe updated belief after seeing D. Everything you report, decide on, or act upon should be a posterior.
P(D)Evidence / marginal likelihoodThe total probability of the data under all competing hypotheses, P(D) = Σ P(D | Hᵢ)P(Hᵢ). It normalises the posterior so the probabilities sum to one and acts as the denominator in the continuous form ∫P(D|θ)P(θ)dθ.
  • Prior P(H) — the belief before evidence. With rare events the prior is tiny, and a tiny prior can survive even a very accurate test.
  • Likelihood P(D | H) — how well the hypothesis explains what you observed. Crucially this is not the probability that H is true.
  • Posterior P(H | D) — the updated belief. Posterior ∝ likelihood × prior, so the data and your starting point are traded off against each other explicitly.
  • Base-rate fallacy — ignoring P(H) and reading P(D | H) as P(H | D). This is why the disease example gives only 16% after a “95% accurate” positive test.
  • Sequential updating — today's posterior becomes tomorrow's prior, so evidence accumulates coherently without ever starting from scratch.
  • Bayesian versus frequentist — Bayes treats the parameter as a random variable with a distribution, so it can output P(θ ∈ interval); the frequentist interval instead describes the long-run behaviour of the procedure.

2.4 Random Variables

PMF p(x) = P(X = x)  ·  PDF: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx  ·  CDF F(x) = P(X ≤ x)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
XRandom variableA function that maps each outcome of the experiment to a number — the number of heads in ten flips, the latency of a request. Its randomness comes from the underlying experiment, not from the function.
xRealised valueA specific number the random variable can take. Capital X is the random quantity; lower-case x is one observed value.
p(x)Probability mass functionFor a discrete variable, the probability that X equals exactly x. It must satisfy p(x) ≥ 0 and Σ p(x) = 1 over all possible values.
f(x)Probability density functionFor a continuous variable, f(x) is not a probability — it is a density. Probability only exists over intervals, obtained by integrating the density, and f(x) itself may exceed 1 (a Uniform on [0, 0.5]).
∫ f(x) dxIntegral over an intervalThe continuous analogue of summing. It converts density into probability, and over the whole real line it equals 1.
F(x)Cumulative distribution functionF(x) = P(X ≤ x). It is non-decreasing, runs from 0 to 1, and is defined identically for discrete and continuous variables, which makes it the natural common language of distributions.
  • Discrete — the variable takes countable values (counts, categories) and is described by a PMF whose values are genuine probabilities that sum to 1.
  • Continuous — the variable takes any value in an interval and is described by a PDF. Because the probability of any single point is exactly zero, “P(X = 2.000)” is meaningless; only intervals carry probability.
  • CDF — F(x) = P(X ≤ x) works for both kinds of variable. Reading probabilities off a CDF is just subtraction: P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a).
  • Expectation and variance of a random variable — the mean and spread are properties of the distribution, computed by summing or integrating x and (x − μ)² against the PMF/PDF.
  • Transformations — a function of a random variable is itself random. E[g(X)] = Σ g(x)p(x), which is not the same as g(E[X]) unless g is linear (Jensen's inequality).
  • Independence of random variables — X and Y are independent when their joint density factorises, f(x, y) = f(x)f(y); this is what makes variances add and makes most simulation-based inference tractable.

2.5 Expectation, Variance, Covariance

E[X] = Σ x · p(x)  ·  Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])²  ·  Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²]
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
E[X]Expectation (mean)The probability-weighted average of all possible values — the center of gravity of the distribution. It need not be a value the variable can actually take (the expected number of children can be 2.4).
Σ x · p(x)Weighted sumEach value is weighted by how likely it is. The integral form ∫ x f(x) dx plays the same role for continuous variables.
μMean of XShorthand for E[X], used as the center around which variance is measured.
Var(X)VarianceThe expected squared distance from the mean — how spread out the distribution is. Squaring prevents positive and negative deviations from cancelling, but it means the unit is the square of X's unit.
E[X²]Second momentThe mean of the squared values. The computational shortcut Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² is usually faster than averaging squared deviations directly, though it can lose precision in floating point.
(E[X])²Squared meanSubtracting it removes the part of the second moment that is just the location, leaving pure dispersion.

  • Linearity of expectation — E[aX + bY] = aE[X] + bE[Y] holds always, with or without independence. This is the single most useful identity in probability and the engine behind proofs of the CLT.
  • Variance scaling — Var(aX + b) = a²Var(X): shifting a distribution changes the mean but not the spread, while scaling multiplies the spread by the square of the factor.
  • Variance of a sum — Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2Cov(X, Y). Only when the variables are uncorrelated does the covariance term vanish and variances add cleanly.
  • Standard deviation — SD(X) = √Var(X) returns the spread to the original units, which is why it is preferred for reporting even though variance is easier to manipulate algebraically.
  • Law of the unconscious statistician — E[g(X)] = Σ g(x)p(x). You never need the distribution of g(X) itself, which makes expectations of complicated functions computable.
  • Jensen's inequality — for a convex g, E[g(X)] ≥ g(E[X]); taking a mean and then a nonlinear transform is not the same as transforming first, a frequent source of modelling error.

2.6 Joint, Marginal, Conditional

Marginal p(x) = Σy p(x, y)  ·  Conditional p(y | x) = p(x, y) / p(x)  ·  Independence p(x, y) = p(x) · p(y)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
p(x, y)Joint distributionThe probability that X = x and Y = y simultaneously — the full description of how the two variables behave together, including any dependence.
p(x)Marginal distributionObtained by summing the joint over all values of y. It is called “marginal” because the totals used to be written in the margin of a contingency table. Marginalising discards information about the relationship.
p(y | x)Conditional distributionThe distribution of Y restricted to the slice where X = x. Division by p(x) renormalises that slice so it sums to 1 again.
ΣyMarginalisation operatorSum over every possible value of Y (or integrate, for continuous variables). This is the operation behind “integrating out” nuisance variables in Bayesian models.
p(x) · p(y)Product form for independenceHolds if and only if X and Y are independent, which makes the joint much cheaper to specify: n marginals instead of one full joint table.
p(x, y) = p(x) p(y | x)Chain ruleThe general factorisation that always holds. Independence is the special case where the conditional collapses to the marginal.
  • Joint — the complete picture p(x, y). Everything else about X and Y can be derived from it, which is why the joint is the fundamental object.
  • Marginal — what you get when you ignore the other variable by summing it out. Marginals are easier to describe but are compatible with many different joint distributions.
  • Conditional — the distribution of one variable inside a slice of the other, related to the joint through Bayes' rule. This is the object that classifiers, regressions and generative models actually learn.
  • Independence — the joint factorises. It massively reduces the number of parameters needed (the motivation behind naive Bayes), but the assumption is rarely exactly true.
  • Conditional independence — X and Y may be dependent marginally yet independent once you condition on Z. This is the whole point of graphical models, and it can disappear if you condition on a collider.
  • Simpson's paradox — an association that holds inside every subgroup can reverse when the groups are pooled, precisely because the marginal distribution weights the subgroups differently from each conditional.

2.7 Total Expectation and Total Variance

E[X] = E[E[X | Y]]  ·  Var(X) = E[Var(X | Y)] + Var(E[X | Y])
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
E[X | Y]Conditional expectationThe average of X inside each slice defined by Y. Since it depends on which slice you are in, it is itself a random variable — a function of Y.
E[E[X | Y]]Law of total expectationAverage the slice averages, weighting by how often each slice occurs. The result is the overall mean, which is why you can estimate a mean from group means and group sizes.
E[Var(X | Y)]Within-group varianceThe average spread of X inside groups. It measures heterogeneity that grouping does not explain.
Var(E[X | Y])Between-group varianceHow much the group means themselves vary. It measures how much of the variation grouping does explain.
Var(X)Total varianceThe unconditional spread, exactly equal to the within plus the between component. Nothing is lost and nothing is double-counted.
YGrouping variableThe variable you condition on — a cluster, a site, a treatment arm. Choosing a good Y moves as much variance as possible from the within term into the between term.
  • Total expectation — the overall average is the weighted average of the group averages. It lets you compute a global mean without ever pooling the raw observations.
  • Total variance decomposition — Var(X) = E[Var(X|Y)] + Var(E[X|Y]) splits variation into “within groups” and “between groups”, the exact accounting that ANOVA is built on.
  • Within-group — E[Var(X | Y)] is the residual noise left after grouping; it is what an unexplained-variance term represents in a regression.
  • Between-group — Var(E[X | Y]) is the signal that grouping explains. A high between/within ratio means the grouping variable is informative.
  • Basis of ANOVA and mixed models — F-tests compare the two components, and random-effects models estimate them separately so that clustered data (students in schools, users in cohorts) gets correct standard errors.
  • Why it matters in practice — ignoring group structure makes you treat correlated observations as independent, which understates standard errors and produces spuriously significant results.

2.8 Limit Theorems

(x̄ − μ) / (σ / √n) → N(0, 1) as n → ∞  ·  SE(x̄) = σ / √n
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
x̄Sample meanThe average of the n observations. It is itself a random variable: repeat the whole experiment and you get a different value.
μPopulation meanThe true center of the population. It is unknown and is exactly what inference tries to pin down.
σPopulation standard deviationThe true spread of the population. If unknown it is replaced by the sample SD s, which turns the Normal limit into a Student-t result for finite n.
nSample sizeHow many observations went into each sample mean. The limit statement is about what happens as n grows without bound.
√nSquare-root-of-n scalingThe reason averaging works: uncertainty shrinks with the square root of the sample size, so 100× the data buys only 10× the precision.
σ / √nStandard errorThe standard deviation of the sample-mean distribution — the standard measure of how far x̄ typically lands from μ. It is estimated as s/√n.
N(0, 1)Standard NormalThe limiting distribution of the standardised mean, regardless of the shape of the original population.
→Convergence in distributionThe CDF of the left-hand quantity approaches the standard Normal CDF at every point, not just its mean and variance.
  • Law of Large Numbers (LLN) — the sample mean converges to the true mean as n grows. This is why simulations work: averaging many random draws recovers the quantity you wanted, no matter how noisy any single draw is.
  • Central Limit Theorem (CLT) — the standardised sample mean converges to a standard Normal. Crucially, it says nothing about the data being Normal; it says the mean is approximately Normal.
  • Standard error σ/√n — the spread of x̄ shrinks with the square root of the sample size, which is the quantitative reason precision improves slowly and why enormous samples are needed to detect tiny effects.
  • Why Normal-based inference survives skewed data — confidence intervals and t-tests rely on x̄ being Normal, not on the observations being Normal; the CLT makes this approximately true once n is reasonably large.
  • Accuracy and rate of convergence — symmetric, light-tailed populations converge quickly (n ≈ 30 is often enough), whereas heavy-tailed or extremely skewed populations need far larger n for the same accuracy.
  • What the CLT cannot fix — it says nothing about the extremes. The mean is Normal, but the maximum is not, which is why extreme-value theory exists for floods, crashes and record events.

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
MX(t)Moment generating functionThe function of an auxiliary variable t defined as E[etX]. It is called “generating” because differentiating it repeatedly at zero produces the moments of X one after another.

  • Moment extraction — derivatives at t = 0 return E[Xᵏ] directly, which is how means and variances of named distributions are derived without painful sums.
  • Uniqueness — if two distributions share an MGF around zero they are the same distribution. This is the standard way to identify the distribution of a derived quantity.
  • Sums of independent variables — M_{X+Y}(t) = M_X(t)·M_Y(t); this is how the sum of n independent Bernoullis is shown to be Binomial, and the sum of Exponentials is shown to be Gamma.
  • Proof engine for the limit theorems — the CLT and the Poisson limit are proved by showing MGFs converge pointwise, which is technically far easier than manipulating distribution functions.
  • Limitations — MGFs need not exist (the Cauchy has none, and log-normal tails are delicate), so the characteristic function E[e^(itX)] is used in rigorous treatments because it always exists.

  • Why they matter in ML — PAC bounds, uniform-convergence results and exploration bonuses in reinforcement learning are all built on these inequalities rather than on asymptotic Normality.

Real-life Applications

  • Medical testing — Bayes' theorem converts sensitivity and specificity into the probability the patient actually has the disease, which is what the clinician must act on.
  • Spam filtering — naive Bayes multiplies word likelihoods with a spam/ham prior, and updates the score with every new token.
  • Insurance pricing — Poisson models claim frequency and Exponential or Gamma models claim severity, so premiums reflect both how often and how much.
  • A/B testing — the sampling distribution of a conversion-rate difference is Normal by the CLT, which is what makes the p-value and confidence interval valid.
  • Credit and fraud scoring — base rates are low, so posterior probabilities of default are far smaller than the model's raw accuracy suggests — the base-rate fallacy in production.
  • Reliability and warranty — waiting-time distributions (Exponential, Weibull) set maintenance schedules and warranty reserves, with the memoryless property driving replacement policies.

3.1 Point Estimation

L(θ) = P(x | θ)  ·  θ̂MLE = argmax L(θ) = argmax ℓ(θ)  ·  θ̂MAP = argmax P(x | θ) · P(θ)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
θParameterThe unknown quantity that governs the data-generating process — a true mean, a conversion rate, a regression coefficient. It is fixed but unknown; θ̂ is its estimate.
θ̂EstimatorA rule for turning data into a guess at θ. Because it is built from random data, θ̂ is itself a random variable with a bias and a variance.
xObserved dataThe sample you actually collected: x = (x₁, …, xₙ). All the information available about θ is contained here.
L(θ)LikelihoodP(x | θ) read as a function of θ with the data held fixed. It is not a probability distribution over θ — it does not integrate to 1 — which is the most important distinction in all of statistics.
ℓ(θ)Log-likelihoodlog L(θ). Since log is monotone it has the same maximiser, but it turns products of probabilities into sums, which is numerically stable and analytically far easier.
argmaxArgument of the maximumThe θ value at which the expression attains its largest value, not the maximum value itself. Setting the derivative to zero gives the MLE when the likelihood is well behaved.
P(θ)PriorThe distribution over θ before seeing data. Multiplying by the likelihood and maximising gives the MAP estimate, which shrinks the answer toward the prior.
nSample sizeThe number of independent observations in x. As n grows the likelihood sharpens and both MLE and MAP converge to the same value.
  • Maximum likelihood (MLE) — pick the parameter value under which the observed data is most probable. It is the workhorse of statistics and machine learning because it needs only a probability model.
  • Always work with the log-likelihood — products of many small probabilities underflow to zero in floating point, while sums of logs are stable; it also turns the normal likelihood into a sum of squared errors.
  • Worked examples — for Normal data the MLE of μ is exactly x̄; the MLE of σ² divides by n rather than n − 1, so it is biased downward on small samples.
  • MAP — maximise likelihood × prior. A Gaussian prior on a regression coefficient produces ridge, a Laplace prior produces lasso, so regularisation and Bayesian shrinkage are the same idea seen from two angles.
  • Method of moments — set the sample moments equal to their theoretical counterparts and solve. It is often simpler than MLE and gives a good starting point for numerical optimisation.
  • Estimators are not unique — x̄, the median and a trimmed mean all estimate μ and all are unbiased under Normality, but they differ in efficiency and robustness, so the choice is a modelling decision.
  • Asymptotic Normality — under regularity conditions the MLE is approximately Normal around the truth with variance given by the inverse Fisher information, which is what makes standard errors and Wald intervals available automatically.

3.2 Estimator Properties

MSE(θ̂) = E[(θ̂ − θ)²] = Bias(θ̂)² + Var(θ̂)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
MSE(θ̂)Mean squared errorThe average squared distance between the estimate and the truth across repeated samples. It is the natural overall measure of how good an estimator is, combining both bias and noise.
θ̂EstimatorThe random estimate produced from a sample. Different samples give different θ̂, which is why we study its distribution rather than a single value.
θTrue parameterThe fixed, unknown quantity being estimated. It does not vary; the estimate does.
E[·]Expectation over repeated samplesAverages over the hypothetical process of drawing infinitely many fresh samples of the same size n.
Bias(θ̂)BiasE[θ̂] − θ, the systematic offset. An unbiased estimator has zero bias; the sample mean is unbiased for μ while the naive variance estimator (dividing by n) is not.
Var(θ̂)Variance of the estimatorThe squared spread of θ̂ around its own mean. It shrinks as n grows, typically like 1/n, which is the source of the √n standard-error law.
  • Bias — E[θ̂] − θ, the systematic error you cannot remove by collecting more data. Variance is noise; bias is being pointed at the wrong target.
  • Variance — how much θ̂ jumps around from sample to sample. Together with bias it forms MSE, so a slightly biased estimator with much lower variance can win outright.
  • Bias–variance trade-off — MSE = Bias² + Variance is the same decomposition you will meet again in Section 6 as the prediction error of a model, just applied to an estimator instead of a forecast.
  • Consistency — θ̂ converges in probability to θ as n → ∞, so bias and variance both vanish in the limit. Consistency is the minimum you should demand of any estimator.
  • Efficiency — among unbiased estimators, the one with the smallest variance. The Cramér-Rao bound gives a lower limit given by the Fisher information, and the MLE attains it asymptotically.
  • Sufficiency — a statistic is sufficient if it captures everything the sample says about θ. Maximum likelihood estimators are functions of sufficient statistics, which is why they lose no information.
  • Robustness — the flip side of efficiency: the most efficient estimator under a model assumption can be catastrophic when that assumption fails, which is why medians and trimmed means remain popular.

3.3 Confidence Intervals

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail

αSignificance levelThe share of intervals you are willing to let miss, e.g. α = 0.05 for a 95% interval. Confidence is 1 − α and the interval uses the α/2 and 1 − α/2 quantiles.

  • Width drivers — wider with more variability (larger s), wider with more confidence (larger t*), narrower with more data (larger n). Only the first two are under your control at analysis time; sample size is decided in advance.

  • What it is not — it is not “a 95% probability that μ lies here”. That statement requires a prior and is a Bayesian credible interval, not a confidence interval.
  • Relationship to testing — a 95% interval is exactly the set of parameter values that would not be rejected by a two-sided test at α = 0.05. Intervals are the more informative object because they show the full range of plausible effect sizes.
  • Reporting good practice — state the estimate, the interval and the units. “The effect is 3.2 points (95% CI: 0.4 to 6.0)” tells the reader both the size and the precision; a p-value alone tells them neither.
  • Assumptions — the t-interval assumes independent observations and approximately Normal sample means. With small n and heavy skew, use a bootstrap interval instead.

3.4 Hypothesis Testing

t = (x̄ − μ₀) / (s / √n)  ·  p = P(|T| ≥ |t| | H₀)  ·  Power = 1 − β
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
H₀Null hypothesisThe default claim of “no effect, no difference, nothing going on” — μ = μ₀. It is the only hypothesis tests can actually falsify, and it is assumed true while the p-value is computed.
H₁Alternative hypothesisWhat you are looking for: μ ≠ μ₀ (two-sided) or μ > μ₀ / μ < μ₀ (one-sided). The direction must be fixed before you look at the data.
μ₀Hypothesised valueThe specific value of the parameter under H₀ — a baseline conversion rate, a target weight, a known process mean.
tTest statisticThe distance between the estimate and μ₀ measured in standard-error units. This standardisation is what makes the number comparable across studies and datasets.
αSignificance levelThe false-positive rate you are willing to accept, chosen in advance. It defines the rejection region and is the maximum probability of a Type I error.
βType II error rateThe probability of failing to detect a real effect of a given size — a false negative. Unlike α it depends on the true effect size.
Power (1 − β)PowerThe probability of correctly detecting an effect that really exists. Conventionally aimed at 0.80 when planning a study.

  • H₀ vs H₁ — H₀ states no effect; H₁ states the effect you are testing for. A test never proves H₀ true, it only fails to find evidence against it — “absence of evidence is not evidence of absence”.
  • p-value — the probability, assuming H₀, of data at least as extreme as what you saw. It is P(data | H₀), not P(H₀ | data); the transposition of those two is the single most common statistical error in practice.
  • Type I and Type II errors — α is the false-positive rate (rejecting a true H₀) and β is the false-negative rate (missing a real effect). Reducing one typically increases the other for a fixed sample size.
  • What increases power — larger true effect, larger n, larger α, and less noise. Required sample size grows as 1/effect², so detecting half the effect needs four times the data.
  • Significance is not importance — with a large enough sample, a trivially small difference becomes statistically significant. Always report the effect size and the confidence interval alongside the p-value.
  • p-values are unstable — a p of 0.049 and one of 0.051 represent almost the same evidence; treating the 0.05 line as a cliff turns arbitrary noise into published findings.
  • Pre-registration and peeking — choosing the hypothesis or stopping the experiment when the result looks good inflates the true false-positive rate far above the nominal α.

  • Assumptions must be checked — independence of observations, approximate Normality of the sample mean (or a large n), and for ANOVA roughly equal group variances. Violations matter most when n is small.
  • Nested model comparisons — the likelihood ratio test, the F-test and the ANOVA F are all the same idea: does the extra complexity buy enough improvement in fit to justify itself?
  • Effect size accompanies the test — a p-value says whether an effect exists; Cohen's d, a correlation ratio, or an odds ratio says whether it matters.
  • Omnibus then pairwise — when ANOVA rejects, follow up with corrected pairwise comparisons; running t-tests between all pairs without adjustment resurrects the multiple-testing problem from 3.6.

3.6 Multiple Testing

Bonferroni: α' = α / m  ·  Benjamini–Hochberg: reject H(i) if p(i) ≤ (i / m) · α
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
mNumber of hypothesesHow many tests you ran. It is the whole problem: with m = 20 independent tests at α = 0.05 you expect one false positive even when nothing is truly happening.
αTarget error rateThe overall false-positive rate you are willing to tolerate across the whole family of tests.
α'Adjusted thresholdThe Bonferroni-corrected per-test significance level, α/m. It guarantees the family-wise error rate stays at or below α but becomes brutally strict when m is large.
p(i)Ordered p-valuesThe p-values sorted from smallest to largest: p₍₁₎ is the most significant result, p₍ₘ₎ the least.
iRankThe rank of a p-value in that sorted list. BH compares each p-value against a threshold that grows with its rank, which is why it is more powerful than a flat correction.
FWERFamily-wise error rateThe probability of making at least one false rejection anywhere in the family. Bonferroni controls this directly.
FDRFalse discovery rateThe expected proportion of your rejections that are wrong. BH controls this, and since it tolerates a few false positives among many discoveries it is far more sensitive.
  • Why it matters — testing many hypotheses without adjustment guarantees spurious “discoveries”: at α = 0.05 and m = 100 independent tests, five significant results are expected from noise alone.
  • Bonferroni — divide α by the number of tests. Extremely simple and valid under any dependence structure, but conservative: it loses power quickly as m grows.
  • Benjamini–Hochberg — controls the false discovery rate instead, by comparing sorted p-values to a rank-dependent threshold. Far more powerful and now the default in genomics and large-scale screening.
  • FWER vs FDR — use FWER control when a single false positive is unacceptable (confirmatory clinical endpoints); use FDR when you can tolerate some noise in exchange for finding more true signals (exploratory screens).
  • Correlated tests — when tests are dependent, Bonferroni becomes even more conservative while BH remains approximately valid under positive dependence; permutation-based adjustments handle arbitrary dependence.
  • Publish the whole family — the correction is only meaningful if m counts every test you actually ran, including the ones you did not write up. Selective reporting silently breaks the guarantee.
  • Alternatives — Holm's step-down procedure is uniformly more powerful than Bonferroni while still controlling FWER, and is a strict improvement for almost every use case.

3.7 Resampling

Interactive · Bootstrap distribution and percentile CI

Resampling from a single skewed sample

The bootstrap only ever sees one original sample — no formula for the standard error is required. Resampling with replacement builds the sampling distribution of whichever statistic you choose, and the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of that histogram form the percentile confidence interval. Switch the statistic to Median on skewed data and watch the interval tighten.

  • Bootstrap — resample the observed data with replacement, recompute the statistic thousands of times, and read the spread of the results as the standard error. It needs no distributional assumption at all.
  • Why it works — the empirical distribution of the sample is your best estimate of the population, so resampling from it mimics the act of drawing fresh samples from the population.
  • Percentile interval — take the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles of the bootstrap distribution for a 95% interval. Other flavours (basic, studentised, BCa) correct for bias and skewness when accuracy matters.
  • Jackknife — leave one observation out at a time and recompute. It is cheaper and useful for bias estimation, but it is less reliable than the bootstrap for skewed statistics.
  • Permutation test — pool the group labels, shuffle them many times and recompute the statistic; the resulting distribution is the exact null distribution under exchangeability, giving a p-value with no parametric assumptions.
  • When to prefer resampling — small samples with non-Normal data, statistics with no closed-form standard error (medians, ratios, R², AUC), and complex designs where theory is unavailable.
  • Limitations — the bootstrap cannot rescue a biased sample, fails for extreme order statistics such as the maximum, and needs care with dependent data (use block or cluster bootstraps for time series and grouped data).

3.8 Bayesian Inference

posterior ∝ likelihood × prior  ·  Beta(a₀ + k, b₀ + n − k)  ·  posterior mean = (a₀ + k) / (a₀ + b₀ + n)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
θParameter of interestHere the unknown success probability (a conversion rate, a click-through rate). Bayes treats it as a random variable with a distribution instead of a fixed constant.
a₀, b₀Prior pseudo-countsThe two shape parameters of the Beta prior. They can be read as imaginary previous observations: a₀ successes and b₀ failures seen before the experiment started.
kObserved successesThe number of successes actually recorded in the new data.
nObserved trialsThe total number of attempts, so n − k is the number of failures.
a₀ + k, b₀ + n − kPosterior parametersThe update is pure addition. Conjugacy means the posterior stays in the Beta family, so there is no need for numerical integration.
posterior mean(a₀ + k)/(a₀ + b₀ + n)A weighted blend of the prior mean and the observed proportion, with weights proportional to the prior strength and the sample size. This is shrinkage in its purest form.

a1, b1 = a0 + k, b0 + n - k
print("Posterior Beta:", a1, b1)

  • Everything is a distribution — the output is not a point estimate and a standard error but a full posterior over θ, from which any summary (mean, median, interval, probability of exceeding a threshold) can be read.
  • Conjugacy — when the prior and likelihood come from matched families (Beta–Binomial, Gamma–Poisson, Normal–Normal) the posterior has a closed form, which is why these pairings dominate practical Bayesian work.
  • Shrinkage — with few observations the posterior sits close to the prior; as n grows the data dominates. This gives Bayesian estimates their reputation for graceful behaviour in small samples.
  • Credible intervals mean what people think confidence intervals mean — a 95% credible interval has a 95% posterior probability of containing θ, subject to the prior being correct.
  • Sequential updating — today's posterior is tomorrow's prior, so evidence accumulates cleanly and there is no need to correct for peeking at the data.
  • When exact inference is impossible — non-conjugate models need MCMC or variational inference (Section 7); the philosophy is unchanged, only the computational route differs.
  • Prior sensitivity — always check how much the conclusion depends on the prior. With weak or uninformative priors the data should dominate, and a result that flips with a reasonable prior change is not a robust finding.

3.9 Model Selection

AIC = 2k − 2 ln L̂  ·  BIC = k · ln(n) − 2 ln L̂
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
kNumber of parametersThe complexity of the model, counting every estimated coefficient plus the noise variance. It is the quantity being penalised.
L̂Maximised likelihoodThe likelihood of the data evaluated at the fitted parameters. Higher L̂ means a better fit, so −2 ln L̂ is a “lack of fit” score to be minimised.
nSample sizeAppears only in BIC, which is what makes the BIC penalty grow with the amount of data while the AIC penalty stays fixed at 2 per parameter.
AICAkaike Information CriterionAn estimate of out-of-sample predictive error. It answers “which model will predict new data best?” and is the default for predictive modelling.
BICBayesian Information CriterionAn approximation to the marginal likelihood. It answers “which model is most probable given the data?”, penalises complexity more heavily, and tends to select sparser models.
lnNatural logarithmUsed because differences in log-likelihoods are additive and numerically stable; AIC and BIC are only meaningful when compared between models fitted to the same data.
DIC / WAICBayesian predictive criteriaDirect Bayesian analogues of AIC for models fitted by MCMC, based on the posterior rather than a single maximum-likelihood point.
Bayes factorRatio of marginal likelihoodsThe evidence for one model versus another, obtained by integrating over the parameters rather than maximising. It requires a genuine prior and is sensitive to it.
  • The idea — fit alone always improves with complexity, so every criterion adds a penalty: a reward for likelihood traded against a charge for parameters.
  • AIC — 2k − 2 ln L̂ targets predictive performance and is the right choice when the goal is forecasting rather than explanation.
  • BIC — k ln n − 2 ln L̂ targets the true model and penalises harder once n > 8, so it selects simpler models and is preferred for inference.
  • Only comparable on the same data — both are reported on a relative scale; an AIC of 340 means nothing on its own, only ΔAIC against competing models does.
  • Rules of thumb — Δ ≈ 2 suggests weak evidence, Δ > 6 strong and Δ > 10 very strong support for the better model, but these numbers assume the candidate set contains the truth.
  • Alternatives — cross-validation directly estimates out-of-sample error and is preferable when the model family is complex, the sample is small or prediction is the whole point.
  • Not the same as a hypothesis test — information criteria compare many models at once and never produce a p-value, which is exactly why they are less prone to the multiple-testing problems of Section 3.6.

Real-life Applications

  • Clinical trials — t-tests, confidence intervals and power analysis are the regulatory backbone of drug approval, with pre-registered primary endpoints.
  • A/B testing — two-sample tests plus confidence intervals on the lift; sample size is fixed in advance so that the test has adequate power for the smallest worthwhile effect.
  • Quality control — ANOVA and control charts detect shifts in a manufacturing process before they become defects.
  • Marketing incrementality — geo-experiments and holdout groups separate genuine lift from the confounds that pollute pre/post comparisons.
  • Manufacturing tolerance intervals — the interval containing a stated proportion of future units, rather than just the mean, drives warranty and safety decisions.
  • Portfolio and credit risk — bootstrap and resampling methods estimate the uncertainty of risk measures whose sampling distributions have no closed form.

4.1 Linear Regression and OLS

y = Xβ + ε  ·  β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy  ·  minβ Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)²
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
yResponse vectorAn n × 1 column vector of the outcome you are predicting — price, sales, yield. Each row is one observation.
XDesign matrixAn n × (p + 1) matrix holding one row per observation and one column per predictor, with a leading column of 1s so that the intercept is estimated like any other coefficient.
βCoefficient vectorThe (p + 1) × 1 unknowns you are solving for: β₀ the intercept and β₁ … β_p the slopes. Each β_j is the expected change in y for a one-unit increase in X_j holding all other predictors fixed.
εError termThe part of y the model cannot explain. Assumed to have mean zero, constant variance and to be independent across observations — the assumptions in Section 4.2 are all about ε.
β̂Estimated coefficientsThe fitted values obtained by minimising the sum of squared residuals. The closed form exists only when XᵀX is invertible; otherwise the system is singular and you need ridge, PCA or dropping a column.
XᵀTranspose of XRows and columns swapped, used to build the normal equations XᵀXβ = Xᵀy.
(XᵀX)⁻¹Inverse of the Gram matrixThe matrix inverse that solves the normal equations. Near-singularity here is exactly what “multicollinearity” means, and it inflates the standard errors of the affected coefficients.
ŷᵢ, eᵢFitted value, residualŷᵢ = xᵢᵀβ̂ is the prediction and eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is what is left over. OLS chooses β̂ to make the sum of squared eᵢ as small as possible.
p, nPredictors, observationsThe number of columns (excluding the intercept) and the number of rows. You need n > p to fit at all, and comfortably more than that to trust the result.
  • Coefficient interpretation — β_j is the expected change in y for a one-unit increase in X_j with every other predictor held constant. Drop the “holding others fixed” clause and the coefficient becomes meaningless.
  • Ordinary least squares — choose the coefficient vector that minimises the sum of squared residuals. Squaring makes the objective smooth and differentiable, which is why a closed-form solution exists.
  • Gauss–Markov theorem — under linearity, zero-mean independent errors, constant variance and no perfect collinearity, OLS is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator, with the smallest variance among all unbiased linear estimators.
  • Geometric view — OLS projects the observed vector y onto the column space of X, so the residual vector is orthogonal to every predictor. This is why residuals always sum to zero when an intercept is included.
  • R² and adjusted R² — R² = 1 − SS_res/SS_tot is the fraction of variance explained, but it can only rise as predictors are added, even useless ones. Adjusted R² applies a penalty so that comparing models of different size is fairer.
  • Correlation is not the same as slope — a large coefficient can be statistically insignificant if the predictor varies little, and a trivial coefficient can be highly significant if the predictor spans a wide range.
  • Extrapolation is dangerous — a linear fit is a local summary. Projecting it far outside the observed range of X assumes a relationship that the data never demonstrated.

4.2 Assumptions (LINE + No Collinearity)

  • Linearity in the parameters — the model must be linear in β, not necessarily in the predictors. X², log(X) and interaction terms are all perfectly legal in a “linear” regression.
  • Independence of errors — violated by time series, repeated measures on the same unit, and clustered sampling. The coefficients stay unbiased but standard errors become far too small, producing spurious significance.
  • Homoscedasticity — constant error variance. Funnel-shaped residuals mean the model predicts some regions far more precisely than others, so ordinary standard errors and prediction intervals are wrong.
  • Normality of residuals — needed mainly for small-sample t-tests and F-tests; with large n the CLT takes over and mild non-Normality is tolerable. It is the least important of the assumptions in practice.
  • No perfect or near-perfect collinearity — when predictors carry overlapping information the matrix XᵀX becomes ill-conditioned, coefficients swing wildly in sign and magnitude, and their standard errors inflate. Diagnose with VIF > 5–10.
  • No influential outliers — a single point with high leverage can move the whole hyperplane. Check leverage and Cook's distance rather than relying on the raw residual alone.
  • Diagnose in order of consequence — independence and correct functional form hurt you most; mild non-Normality hurts you least, so triage the checks by how much they actually change your conclusions.

4.3 Diagnostics

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
R²Coefficient of determinationThe proportion of the total variation in y explained by the model. It is unit-free and bounded by 1, which makes it easy to quote and easy to over-interpret.
SSresResidual sum of squaresΣ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)², the variation left unexplained. Smaller is better and this is exactly what OLS minimises.
SStotTotal sum of squaresΣ(yᵢ − ȳ)², the total variation in the outcome. R² is literally “variation explained out of variation available”.

adjusted R²Penalised R²R² corrected for the number of predictors: it falls when a new variable does not pull its weight, which is what makes it usable for comparing models of different size.

DiCook's distanceHow much all fitted values change if observation i is deleted. It combines leverage and residual size, and values above roughly 1 flag genuinely influential points.

  • Residuals versus fitted — the single most informative plot. Curvature signals a missing nonlinear term, a funnel signals heteroscedasticity, and clusters signal a missing grouping variable.
  • Q-Q plot — compares the residual quantiles with Normal quantiles; systematic bending at the ends reveals heavy tails or skew, which matters most in small samples.
  • Scale-location plot — the square root of the standardised residuals against fitted values, making changing variance easier to see than in the residual plot.

  • R² never decreases when predictors are added — even pure noise raises it slightly. Adjusted R² and cross-validated error penalise that inflation, and AIC/BIC (Section 3.9) do the same job more formally.
  • High R² is not validation — overfit models report excellent in-sample R² and fail on new data. Split the data, or compare with cross-validated error, before believing the number.
  • Diagnostics are for prediction and inference alike — even when you only care about forecasts, uncorrected heteroscedasticity or autocorrelation makes your uncertainty statements wrong.

4.4 Logistic Regression

log(p / (1 − p)) = β₀ + β₁x₁ + …  ·  p = 1 / (1 + e−Xβ)  ·  odds ratio = eβ
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
pProbability of the eventThe model's predicted chance that y = 1 (churn, default, click). It is always between 0 and 1, which is what makes logistic regression suitable for classification.
p / (1 − p)OddsProbability of the event divided by probability of no event. Odds of 3 mean the event is three times as likely to happen as not, and odds span 0 to ∞.
log(p / (1 − p))Log-odds (logit)The natural logarithm of the odds, which maps (0, 1) onto the whole real line. This transform is exactly what lets us model a bounded probability with an unbounded linear predictor Xβ.
β₀InterceptThe log-odds of the event when every predictor is zero. Often not directly meaningful if zero is outside the data range.
β₁ … β_pCoefficientsEach β_j is the change in log-odds per one-unit increase in x_j. They are fitted by maximum likelihood, not by least squares, because the noise is Bernoulli.
eβOdds ratioThe multiplicative change in the odds per one-unit increase. β = 0 gives an odds ratio of 1 (no effect), β > 0 greater odds, β < 0 lower odds. Odds ratios are deliberately not the same as risk ratios.
XβLinear predictorThe weighted sum of the predictors. It can take any real value and is transformed by the sigmoid into a probability.
1/(1 + e−z)Sigmoid / logistic functionThe inverse of the logit. It maps the real line smoothly onto (0, 1), has slope ¼ at z = 0, and produces the characteristic S shape.

Fit by MLE. Predictions are probabilities; choose threshold by business costs.

  • Why not plain regression — linear regression on a 0/1 outcome can predict values below 0 or above 1 and assumes constant variance, both of which are false for a binary response.
  • The logit link — modelling log-odds keeps the linear structure on the right-hand side while guaranteeing the prediction stays a valid probability on the left.
  • Odds versus probability — an odds ratio of 2 means the odds double, not that the probability doubles. For a rare event the two nearly coincide; for a common one they diverge sharply.
  • Fitting by maximum likelihood — no closed form exists, so coefficients are found iteratively (Newton–Raphson or IRLS). Separation, where a predictor perfectly splits the classes, sends coefficients to infinity and needs penalisation to fix.
  • Choosing the threshold — 0.5 is only optimal when false positives and false negatives cost the same. Lowering the threshold raises recall; raising it raises precision, so the operating point should follow the cost matrix.
  • Probability calibration — the sigmoid shape often gives well-calibrated scores, but if you use class weighting or resampling you must recalibrate (Platt scaling or isotonic regression) before treating the outputs as probabilities.
  • Multiclass extensions — multinomial logistic regression uses softmax instead of the sigmoid, and proportional-odds models handle ordered categories.

4.5 Poisson Regression and GLMs

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail

log(μ)Log linkThe link function that connects the linear predictor to the mean. Coefficients therefore act multiplicatively: eβ is the multiplicative change in the expected count.

  • Check overdispersion — if the residual variance is much larger than the mean, the Poisson assumption fails; use a quasi-Poisson for corrected standard errors or a negative binomial when you need a proper likelihood.
  • Anatomy of a GLM — a distribution for the response, a linear predictor, and a link function that connects them. Estimation is by maximum likelihood and inference remains approximate Wald or likelihood-ratio based.

  • Interpretation on the natural scale — report exp(β) and say “the rate is 1.34 times higher”, not “log-rate increased by 0.29”, because stakeholders think in ratios.
  • Goodness of fit — examine deviance residuals and Pearson χ² rather than R², which has no clean meaning for count models.

  • Ridge (L2) — shrinks all coefficients smoothly and distributes credit among correlated predictors. It never removes a variable, so the model stays dense but stable.
  • Lasso (L1) — produces exact zeros, so it selects features and yields interpretable sparse models. With groups of correlated predictors it arbitrarily keeps one and discards the rest.
  • Elastic net — combines both penalties to get sparsity while keeping correlated groups together, which is why it is the default choice for wide, correlated datasets such as gene expression.
  • Choosing λ — cross-validate it, typically on a logarithmic grid. The one-standard-error rule picks the largest λ within one SE of the minimum, favouring a simpler model.
  • Always standardise first — the penalty is scale-dependent, so an unstandardised predictor measured in small units gets penalised far more heavily than an equivalent predictor measured in large units.
  • Interpretation caution — shrunken coefficients are biased on purpose and their standard errors are not the usual ones. Do not read a regularised coefficient as an unbiased effect size, and never use lasso selection as though it were ordinary model selection.

bj, ujFixed and random effectsIn mixed-effects models, fixed effects are common population slopes and random effects are group-specific deviations that shrink toward the grand mean.
  • Mixed-effects / hierarchical models — fixed effects describe the population and random effects capture group-specific deviations (students within schools, users within cohorts). They handle repeated measures correctly instead of pretending each row is independent.
  • Nonlinear regression — replaces the linear predictor with a parametric curve fitted by nonlinear least squares. Use it when theory dictates a functional form, such as a saturating dose–response or an exponential decay.
  • Quantile regression — models the conditional median or any other quantile instead of the mean. It is naturally robust to outliers and shows how a predictor affects inequality, not just the average.
  • Survival analysis — handles time-to-event data with censoring, the situation where you know the event has not happened yet but not when it will. It models hazards with Kaplan–Meier curves and the Cox model rather than treating censored times as missing.
  • Why censoring breaks ordinary methods — throwing away censored observations biases the estimate downward dramatically, and coding them as “no event” ignores the information that they survived that long.
  • Generalised additive models — smooth nonlinear relationships (splines) inside a GLM framework, giving flexibility without the interpretability losses of a black-box model.
  • Robust and heavy-tailed errors — Student-t or Huber losses replace the Normal likelihood when residuals have fat tails, so a few extreme points cannot dominate the fit.

Real-life Applications

  • House prices — OLS with diagnostics: log-transform the price to tame skew, check residuals for the funnel pattern, and watch for the leverage of trophy properties.
  • Credit default — logistic regression gives probabilities, so expected loss can be computed as probability × exposure × loss given default.
  • Insurance claims — Poisson or negative binomial for claim frequency, Gamma for severity, often combined in a frequency–severity model.
  • Marketing mix modelling — regression with adstock and saturation transforms estimates the incremental effect of each channel while controlling for seasonality and price.
  • Clinical dose–response — logistic and nonlinear models find the dose achieving a target response rate, and quantile regression shows whether a treatment changes the spread as well as the mean.
  • Customer churn and lifetime value — survival models estimate when customers leave, handling the large fraction who have not churned yet as censored rather than lost.

  • ROC-AUC — threshold-independent and interpretable as a ranking probability, but it can look excellent on a rare-event problem where the practical precision is hopeless.
  • PR-AUC — the better summary when positives are rare. Its baseline is the prevalence rather than 0.5, so a PR-AUC of 0.3 on a 2% prevalence problem is genuinely strong.

  • Fixing calibration — Platt scaling (a logistic fit on the scores) or isotonic regression (a monotone step function) recalibrate the outputs using a held-out set.
  • Why accuracy hides failures — a model that predicts the majority class for every observation scores high accuracy while catching none of the positives, a trap that only the confusion matrix exposes.
  • Report the whole picture — confusion matrix, chosen threshold with its business justification, ROC-AUC, PR-AUC and a calibration curve. Any single metric can be gamed or misread.

5.3 Multivariate Methods

  • PCA — finds orthogonal directions of maximum variance and projects the data onto the first few. Standardise features first, because an unscaled variable measured in large units will dominate the components; the price of the compression is that components are linear combinations and therefore hard to interpret.
  • Variance explained and the scree plot — each component carries a share of the total variance, and a scree plot of those shares against component number shows where the useful information stops. Keeping enough for 80–95% is the usual heuristic.
  • Factor analysis — assumes latent factors generate the observed correlations plus variable-specific noise. Unlike PCA it models measurement error, which makes it the right tool for psychometric scales and construct validation.
  • ICA — recovers statistically independent, non-Gaussian sources rather than merely uncorrelated ones. It is the basis of the classic cocktail-party problem and of EEG artefact removal.
  • Clustering — k-means is fast but assumes roughly round, similar-sized clusters and must be told k; hierarchical clustering builds a dendrogram you can cut at any level; DBSCAN finds arbitrary shapes and labels noise instead of forcing every point into a cluster; a GMM gives soft, elliptical assignments with probabilities.
  • Choosing k — the elbow method, silhouette score, gap statistic and BIC for mixtures all measure different things. Plot the metric, do not just grab the argmax, and sanity-check the clusters against domain meaning.
  • t-SNE and UMAP — nonlinear embeddings for visualisation only. The distances between well-separated clusters, the density of the blobs and the apparent cluster sizes are artefacts of the algorithm, so never read them quantitatively.
  • The curse of dimensionality — as dimensions grow, distances concentrate and neighbourhoods stop being local, which is why every one of these methods benefits from dimension reduction before clustering or distance-based classification.

Real-life Applications

  • Spam and phishing detection — naive Bayes or a linear SVM over token counts, with precision tuned to keep false alarms out of users' inboxes.
  • Fraud detection — recall-focused metrics and PR-AUC, because the positive class is a fraction of a percent and the cost of a miss far exceeds the cost of a false alarm.

  • Medical screening — high sensitivity is favoured so that few cases are missed, with the resulting false positives triaged by a second, more specific confirmatory test.
  • Credit approval — logistic regression with a documented scorecard, where the threshold is set by the expected profit of approving versus declining.
  • Search and recommendation ranking — AUC and NDCG evaluate ordering rather than fixed decisions, because the user only ever sees the top of the list.

06Statistical Learning Theory

Bias-variance, overfitting, cross-validation, VC dimension, ensembles.

6.1 Core Ideas

E[(y − ŷ)²] = Bias(ŷ)² + Var(ŷ) + σ²ε
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
yObserved outcomeA fresh test observation, generated by the true relationship plus noise.
ŷPredictionWhat the fitted model outputs. Because the training data is random, ŷ is itself random across repeated training runs.
E[(y − ŷ)²]Expected squared prediction errorThe average error you would make on new data across the whole distribution — the quantity you actually care about, as opposed to in-sample fit.
Bias(ŷ)BiasThe gap between the average prediction and the truth. It comes from the model being too rigid: a straight line fitted to a curve is biased no matter how much data you supply.
Var(ŷ)VarianceHow much the prediction swings when the training set changes. It comes from the model being too flexible, chasing the particular noise in this particular sample.
σ²εIrreducible noiseThe inherent randomness of the data-generating process. No model can remove it, so it sets a hard floor on achievable error.
f(x)True functionThe real relationship between predictors and outcome, which is never observable — only noisy samples of it are.
nTraining sizeMore data reduces variance but leaves bias unchanged, which is exactly why “just add data” fails for an underfit model.
  • Simple models — high bias, low variance. They underfit: the same wrong shape is produced every time you retrain, so the error is stable but stubbornly large.
  • Flexible models — low bias, high variance. They overfit: the training error becomes tiny while the test error balloons, and retraining on a slightly different sample produces a very different model.
  • Reading the symptom — low training error with high test error is overfitting; both high is underfitting. The gap between the two curves is your variance, and their level is your bias.
  • Data splits — train to fit, validation to tune, test to estimate final performance exactly once. Reusing the test set for decisions turns it into another validation set and destroys its purpose.
  • Cross-validation — k-fold (typically 5 or 10) estimates generalisation error using every observation for validation exactly once; stratified folds preserve class proportions; LOOCV has low bias but high variance and is expensive.
  • Time series need forward-chaining — random shuffling leaks future information into the past. Always split by time, and leave a gap between train and validation where the forecast horizon requires it.
  • Grouped data need grouped splits — if several rows come from the same patient or customer, all of that unit's rows must fall on the same side of the split, otherwise the model memorises the unit.
  • Curse of dimensionality and No Free Lunch — in high dimensions data becomes sparse and distances lose meaning, and no single algorithm dominates across all problems, so empirical validation replaces theory as the arbiter.

6.2 Theory

R(h) ≤ R̂(h) + complexity term  ·  PAC: with probability ≥ 1 − δ, error ≤ ε  ·  VC dimension of linear models in d dims = d + 1
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
R(h)True riskThe expected loss of hypothesis h on the whole population — the quantity you would love to know and cannot observe.
R̂(h)Empirical riskThe average loss on the training sample. Minimising it is empirical risk minimisation (ERM), the principle behind almost every training procedure.
complexity termCapacity penaltyHow much the model class can memorise. Bigger, more flexible classes inflate this term, which is why a larger gap opens between training and test performance.
VC dimensionVapnik–Chervonenkis dimensionThe largest set of points the model class can label arbitrarily — “shatter” — in every possible way. Linear classifiers in d dimensions have VC dimension d + 1, quantifying exactly how much capacity each extra feature adds.
nSample sizeAppears in the denominator of the complexity term, so the generalisation gap shrinks as 1/√n with more data.
ε, δAccuracy, confidencePAC stands for “probably approximately correct”: with probability at least 1 − δ the error is at most ε. The pair (ε, δ) is the precise promise a learning guarantee makes.
Rm(H)Rademacher complexityHow well the model class can fit purely random ±1 labels on a sample of size m. A class that can fit noise easily has high complexity and will generalise worse.
SRMStructural risk minimisationChoose the model that minimises empirical risk plus a capacity penalty. Regularisation, early stopping and choosing simpler architectures are all practical implementations of this idea.
  • Empirical risk minimisation — pick the hypothesis with the lowest average loss on the training data. Without a constraint on capacity, ERM will happily memorise noise.
  • VC dimension — the size of the largest set the class can shatter. It is an intuitive capacity measure: linear classifiers in d dimensions have VC dimension d + 1, so adding features raises capacity and with it the risk of overfitting.
  • PAC learning — quantifies how many samples are needed to guarantee, with probability 1 − δ, that the error is within ε of the best achievable. The required sample size grows roughly with the capacity and as 1/ε².
  • Generalisation bounds — test error is bounded by training error plus a complexity term that shrinks with n. The insight is directional rather than numerical: capacity must be paid for.
  • Rademacher complexity — measures how well the class fits pure noise. It is data-dependent, hence often tighter than VC-based bounds, and it underlies the theory of generalisation in modern learning.
  • Structural risk minimisation — minimise training error plus a capacity penalty, which is precisely what L1/L2 regularisation, weight decay and early stopping do in practice.
  • Why the theory is loose in the deep-learning era — modern networks have enough capacity to shatter random labels yet still generalise well, which classical uniform-convergence bounds cannot explain. Implicit regularisation, data structure and optimisation dynamics matter as much as raw capacity.

6.3 Practical Toolkit

  • Regularisation — L1 and L2 penalties shrink the hypothesis class explicitly; early stopping, dropout and data augmentation achieve the same effect implicitly by limiting how far the model can chase noise.
  • Data augmentation — inventing label-preserving variations of existing examples effectively enlarges the sample and encodes invariances you already believe, such as “a flipped cat is still a cat”.
  • Bagging — train the same model on bootstrap resamples and average the predictions. Averaging cancels independent errors, so variance falls while bias is essentially unchanged; random forests add feature subsampling to decorrelate the trees further.
  • Boosting — fit models sequentially, each one focusing on the errors the previous ensemble made. This primarily reduces bias, which is why it can turn hundreds of weak, high-bias stumps into a strong learner.
  • Bagging versus boosting — bagging fixes variance and is hard to overfit by adding trees; boosting fixes bias but will overfit if you keep adding rounds, so its learning rate and number of rounds must be tuned together.
  • Hyperparameter tuning — grid search is exhaustive and expensive, random search covers the space more efficiently for the same budget, and Bayesian optimisation pays off when each fit is costly. Always tune inside the cross-validation loop.
  • Nested cross-validation — an inner loop chooses hyperparameters, an outer loop estimates performance. Using a single CV loop for both gives a mildly optimistic estimate that grows with the number of configurations tried.
  • Determinism and reporting — fix random seeds, report the spread across folds rather than a single number, and record the search space so the result is reproducible.

Real-life Applications

  • Model selection — cross-validation chooses the complexity that minimises expected error on data the model has never seen, instead of the complexity that best memorises the training set.
  • Tabular machine learning — random forests and gradient boosting still dominate tabular problems because they handle mixed types, missing values and interactions with minimal preprocessing.
  • Time-series forecasting — forward-chaining cross-validation mimics real deployment, where only the past is ever available for training.
  • Kaggle competitions and production baselines — ensembles of bagged and boosted trees are the standard strong baseline that every more exotic approach must beat to justify its complexity.
  • Credit and insurance scoring — gradient boosting with monotonic constraints where regulation requires that risk never decreases with a predictor.
  • Model monitoring — learning curves and held-out error over time reveal data drift, which shows up as a widening gap between the training-time and production error.

7.1 Graphical Models

  • The compactness win — 30 binary variables would need about a billion rows of a joint table, but a network with three parents per node needs only 30 × 8 numbers. Sparsity is what makes probabilistic modelling feasible.
  • Markov random fields — undirected models that factorise over cliques, used when influence is symmetric: image pixels, spatial regions, social networks. There is no natural direction, so the “parents” idea does not apply.
  • Conditional independence — the graph encodes which variables are independent given which others. d-separation is the graphical test, and it is what lets inference ignore most of the network.
  • Colliders — a node with two incoming arrows makes its parents marginally independent yet dependent once you condition on the collider or any of its descendants. This is the graphical origin of selection bias and of “controlling for” the wrong variable.
  • Inference is the hard part — exact inference is tractable on trees and thin networks but generally exponential in treewidth. Real models use variable elimination, junction trees or approximate methods such as loopy belief propagation and sampling.
  • Structure learning — the graph itself is often unknown and must be estimated from data, which is a combinatorial search usually driven by conditional-independence tests or score functions with a complexity penalty.

7.2 Hidden Markov Models

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail

otObservationThe measurable signal at time t — a frame of audio, a price, a word. Observations are noisy glimpses of the hidden state.
aijTransition probabilityThe chance of moving from state i to state j between consecutive steps. The rows of the transition matrix A sum to 1, and the Markov property says the future depends on the past only through the current state.
bj(ot)Emission probabilityHow likely observation ot is when the hidden state is j. This is the model of the sensor or the “noise channel” linking the hidden cause to the visible effect.
πiInitial distributionThe probability of starting in state i at t = 1. Together with A and B it completely specifies the model.
αt(i)Forward variableThe probability of having seen the observations up to time t and of being in state i right now. The forward algorithm computes it recursively in O(T·K²) time instead of enumerating every state sequence.
βt(i)Backward variableThe probability of the remaining observations given that you are in state i at time t. Forward and backward variables together give smoothed state probabilities and the Baum-Welch updates.

  • The model — a hidden state evolves as a Markov chain and emits an observation at each step. You see only the emissions, so every inference problem is about reasoning backwards from effects to causes over time.
  • Evaluation — the forward algorithm — computes P(observations) by dynamic programming over the state lattice in O(T·K²) time rather than summing over Kᵗ impossible sequences.
  • Decoding — Viterbi — finds the single most likely sequence of hidden states rather than the most likely state at each time independently. The two answers can differ, and the distinction matters in speech and gene finding.
  • Learning — Baum–Welch — an expectation–maximisation algorithm that iteratively estimates A, B and π from unlabelled sequences, climbing the likelihood to a local optimum.
  • The three classic questions — evaluation (how likely is this sequence?), decoding (what states produced it?) and learning (what parameters fit best?). Every application reduces to one of the three.
  • Scaling and numerical issues — probabilities of long sequences underflow, so implementations work in log space or rescale the forward variables at each step.
  • Modern context — HMMs built the speech-recognition and bioinformatics pipelines of the 1990s and are still the theoretical foundation for understanding state-space models, though sequence tasks are now dominated by transformers and recurrent networks.

7.3 Expectation-Maximisation

E-step: Q(θ | θ⁽ᵗ⁾) = EZ | x, θ⁽ᵗ⁾[ log p(x, Z | θ) ]  ·  M-step: θ⁽ᵗ⁺¹⁾ = argmaxθ Q(θ | θ⁽ᵗ⁾)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
xObserved dataWhat you actually measured. EM is needed precisely when the likelihood of x involves summing over quantities you cannot see.
ZLatent variablesThe unobserved quantities — cluster memberships, missing values, hidden states. Treating them as random and averaging over them is the “expectation” in EM.
θParametersThe quantities being estimated: mixture weights, means and covariances, transition probabilities.
θ⁽ᵗ⁾Current parameter estimateThe values from the previous iteration. They are held fixed while the E-step computes the expected log-likelihood.
Q(θ | θ⁽ᵗ⁾)Expected complete-data log-likelihoodThe objective EM actually optimises. It is a lower bound on the observed-data log-likelihood, which is why every iteration improves the true likelihood.

  • E-step — using the current parameter estimates, compute the posterior distribution over the latent variables, i.e. the probability that each data point belongs to each latent group.
  • M-step — treat those posterior probabilities as soft assignments and re-estimate every parameter to maximise the expected complete-data log-likelihood. Because memberships are weighted rather than hard, the updates are smooth and closed form.
  • Guaranteed monotone improvement — the observed-data log-likelihood never decreases, which follows from the decomposition log p(x|θ) = Q(θ|θ⁽ᵗ⁾) + H(θ|θ⁽ᵗ⁾) + KL term. Each step climbs the hill, but only to the nearest peak.

  • Convergence is slow near the optimum — the improvement per iteration shrinks linearly rather than quadratically, so hundreds or thousands of iterations are common in practice.
  • Standard applications — Gaussian mixtures for clustering, Baum–Welch for hidden Markov models, missing-data imputation, and the M-step of any latent-variable model including latent Dirichlet allocation.
  • Alternative local optima diagnoses — recording the log-likelihood across restarts and comparing final values tells you whether the chosen solution is stable or merely one of many equally plausible answers.
  • Relation to k-means — k-means is EM with hard assignments and equal spherical covariances, which is exactly why it is faster but less general than a Gaussian mixture model.

  • Two strategies for the same problem — VI turns inference into optimisation and MCMC turns it into sampling. Both approximate an intractable posterior; neither is universally better.
  • Speed — VI is typically orders of magnitude faster because it is a deterministic optimisation, and it scales to large datasets through stochastic gradients.
  • Accuracy — MCMC is asymptotically exact and converges to the true posterior given enough time; VI is approximate and often underestimates posterior variance because it prefers a mode-seeking fit.
  • Failure modes differ — VI can confidently report a poster that is systematically wrong; MCMC can look like it has converged while the chain is stuck in one region, so always check R̂ and effective sample size.
  • Choosing between them — use VI for large-scale problems where approximate posteriors are acceptable (variational autoencoders, topic models, recommendation); use MCMC when calibrated uncertainty is the point of the analysis (clinical or scientific inference).
  • Modern middle ground — amortised inference with normalising flows, importance-weighted ELBOs and stochastic variational inference narrow the gap, and hybrid schemes often start with VI and refine with short MCMC runs.
  • Diagnostics are mandatory — for MCMC, trace plots, R̂ near 1.01 and sufficient effective sample size; for VI, sensitivity of the results to the chosen variational family, which is easy to forget precisely because nothing visibly fails.

7.5 Gaussian Processes

SymbolNameWhat it means in detail

  • Calibrated uncertainty comes free — the posterior is Gaussian at every prediction point, so you get a mean and a variance without bootstrapping or ensembling. This is the GP's defining advantage over most regression methods.
  • The kernel encodes your assumptions — squared-exponential for smooth functions, Matérn for rougher ones, periodic kernels for seasonality, and sums or products of kernels compose new structures. Choosing the kernel is the real modelling work.

  • Excellent on small data, bad on large — a GP works beautifully with tens or hundreds of points but costs O(n³) in time and O(n²) in memory, which is why sparse and inducing-point approximations exist.
  • Behaviour away from the data — the predictive variance inflates back to the prior as you move away from observed inputs, so the GP tells you honestly that it does not know.
  • Classification needs extra work — for binary outcomes the posterior is not Gaussian, so the model is fitted with the Laplace approximation or expectation propagation.

7.6 Bayesian Optimisation

  • Pick the next point by acquisition — expected improvement, upper confidence bound or probability of improvement converts the surrogate into a single score to maximise, automatically balancing exploration against exploitation.
  • Evaluate and repeat — the loop is deliberate and sequential, because each new observation changes the surrogate and therefore what the best next question is.
  • Why it beats grid search — a grid wastes most of its budget in regions already known to be poor; Bayesian optimisation concentrates the same budget where either the promise or the uncertainty is highest.
  • Practical scales — it shines for roughly 5–50 dimensions and up to a few hundred evaluations. Beyond that, tree-structured Parzen estimators and meta-learned warm starts are typically used instead.
  • Batch and constraint variants — parallel acquisition functions allow several evaluations at once, and constrained or multi-objective versions handle feasibility and trade-offs.

  • Topic modelling — LDA discovers latent themes across document collections and is still used for exploratory analysis of unstructured text.
  • Hyperparameter tuning — Bayesian optimisation finds good configurations of expensive models in far fewer trials than grid or random search.
  • Recommender systems — matrix factorisation with Bayesian priors handles sparse ratings and gives uncertainty-aware recommendations.
  • Robotics and navigation — Kalman and particle filters fuse noisy sensor streams into a coherent state estimate in real time.
  • Medical decision support — Bayesian networks encode domain knowledge explicitly and produce calibrated probabilities of diagnoses under incomplete evidence.

08Experimental Design & Causality

A/B testing, power, confounding, DAGs, propensity scores, DiD, RD.

8.1 Experiments

  • Randomisation is the whole point — assigning units to treatment by chance balances known and unknown confounders in expectation, which is what licenses a causal claim. No amount of regression adjustment achieves this.
  • One primary metric — choosing the headline metric in advance prevents the garden of forking paths, where a favourable outcome is found by trying enough measures.
  • Sample size in advance — a power calculation fixes n from the smallest effect worth detecting, α and the desired power. Choosing n after the fact is choosing the answer.
  • Randomise the right unit — users, sessions, devices or geographies. Randomising at too fine a level lets a single user appear in both arms and contaminates the comparison.
  • Planned duration, no peeking — checking continuously and stopping at the first significant result inflates the false-positive rate far above the nominal α. Use fixed horizons or always-valid sequential methods.
  • Estimate the effect with an interval — report the lift and its confidence interval rather than a bare p-value, so the decision can weigh magnitude against uncertainty.
  • Classic pitfalls — novelty and primacy effects fade over time, network interference spills treatment between arms, sample-ratio mismatch signals broken assignment, and multiple metrics require the corrections from Section 3.6.
  • Check the assignment itself — an A/A test before the real experiment confirms the pipeline produces a null result when there is nothing to find.

Interactive · Power, sample size and the smallest detectable effect

Two-sample test planning

The curve shows how power rises with the sample size per arm. Halving the smallest effect you want to detect quadruples the sample you need, because required n scales as 1/effect². That single fact explains why most experiments that try to detect a 1% lift never reach significance, and why the effect size must be chosen from business relevance, not from hope.

8.2 Bias

  • Confounding — a third variable influences both the treatment and the outcome, manufacturing an association that is not causal. Randomisation or explicit adjustment is required, and unmeasured confounders cannot be adjusted away at all.
  • Simpson's paradox — an association that holds in every subgroup can reverse when the groups are pooled, purely because group sizes are unbalanced. Always inspect the stratified view alongside the aggregate.
  • Selection bias — the sample is not representative of the population you want to conclude about: volunteer bias, non-response bias, and sampling only from the easiest to reach.
  • Survivorship bias — only the units that survived to be observed enter the data. It made “the market always recovers” look true by silently deleting the funds that closed.
  • Measurement bias — the act of measuring changes the measurement, or the instrument is systematically wrong for some groups. Poor proxies bias coefficients toward zero, a phenomenon called attenuation.
  • Immortal time and look-ahead bias — crediting a treatment with survival that occurred before the treatment started, or using information that was not available at prediction time.
  • Prevention beats cure — design choices (randomisation, blinding, complete follow-up, pre-registration) remove bias; post-hoc statistical fixes only attempt to patch what leaked in.

8.3 Causal Inference

  • ATE and CATE — ATE is the population-average effect; CATE conditions on X and is what you need for targeting. A treatment can help the ATE and harm a specific CATE.
  • DAGs — a causal diagram encodes which variables cause which, and therefore which adjustments are valid. Drawing the graph before touching the data is the single most valuable habit in applied causal work.
  • Adjust for confounders only — conditioning on a collider creates spurious association, and conditioning on a mediator removes part of the effect. Both are common in practice and both are invisible without a diagram.
  • Backdoor criterion — find a set of variables that blocks every backdoor path and contains no descendants of T. If none exists, the effect is not identifiable from these data at all.
  • The identification assumption is untestable — unconfoundedness cannot be verified, so report it explicitly and run sensitivity analyses showing how strong an unmeasured confounder would have to be to overturn the conclusion.
  • Randomised experiments remain the gold standard — they make the assumption true by construction. Observational methods are what you use when randomisation is impossible, unethical or already in the past.

8.4 Observational Methods

Propensity score: e(x) = P(T = 1 | X = x)  ·  IPW estimator: ATE = E[ TY/e(X) − (1 − T)Y/(1 − e(X)) ]

1/e(X)Inverse probability weightTreated units are weighted up by how unlikely their treatment was, creating a pseudo-population in which treatment is unrelated to X.
1/(1 − e(X))Control weightThe complementary weight for untreated units. Extreme propensities near 0 or 1 produce enormous weights and unstable estimates — this is the central practical danger of IPW.
ZInstrumental variableA variable that affects treatment but has no direct path to the outcome except through the treatment. It isolates exogenous variation in T but can only identify a local effect for the compliers.

  • Propensity scores — reduce a high-dimensional confounding problem to a single balancing score, then match, stratify or weight on it. Always check covariate balance afterwards, not just the score model's fit.
  • Matching and stratification — pair treated units with similar controls so that comparisons are like-for-like. Discarding unmatched units changes the estimand to the population with overlap, which should be stated explicitly.
  • Inverse probability weighting — reweights the sample to look like a randomised trial. Trimming extreme weights is usually necessary, and the estimate is sensitive to the propensity model's specification.
  • Instrumental variables — a valid instrument must be relevant (it moves treatment) and excludable (it affects the outcome only through treatment). The excludability assumption is untestable and is where most IV analyses fail.
  • Difference-in-differences — powerful and transparent, but everything rests on parallel trends: without treatment, the two groups would have moved together. Plot the pre-period trends to make the assumption visible.
  • Regression discontinuity — highly credible locally, because units just below and just above the cutoff are nearly identical; it estimates the effect at the threshold only and needs a bandwidth choice plus a manipulation check.
  • No method fixes a bad design — every one of these approaches trades a testable assumption for an untestable one, so report the assumption, the sensitivity analysis and the estimand being targeted.

Real-life Applications

  • A/B testing — power analysis and confidence intervals on the lift, with the sample size fixed before launch and the primary metric declared in advance.
  • Policy evaluation — difference-in-differences and regression discontinuity measure the effect of a change that was rolled out by rule rather than by lottery.
  • Clinical trials — randomisation plus blinding is what allows a causal claim about efficacy and keeps the trial's assumptions defensible to regulators.
  • Marketing attribution — holdout groups and geo-experiments separate genuine incrementality from the selection effects that corrupt last-click attribution.
  • Product feature rollouts — staged rollouts with switchback designs handle interference when users in the same market affect one another.
  • Health and economics research — propensity scores and instrumental variables are the standard tools when a randomised trial would be unethical or impossible.

9.1 Stationarity

Stationarity: E[yt] = μ,   Var(yt) = σ²,   Cov(yt, yt+k) = γk (all independent of t)  ·  AR(1): yt = φ yt−1 + εt, stationary iff |φ| < 1
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
ytValue at time tThe observation in period t. Time series data are ordered, so shuffling the rows destroys the information the model needs.
μConstant meanThe level of the process, required to be the same at every point in time. A trending series fails this immediately.
σ²Constant varianceHomoscedasticity in time. A series whose fluctuations grow (financial prices, some counts) is not stationary in variance.
γkAutocovariance at lag kThe covariance between the series and itself shifted by k steps. Stationarity requires this to depend only on the lag k, not on where in time you are looking.
φAR coefficientThe persistence of the AR(1) process. |φ| < 1 gives mean reversion and stationarity; φ = 1 is a random walk with a unit root and infinite long-run variance; |φ| > 1 is explosive.
εtWhite noiseIndependent, zero-mean, constant-variance shocks. It is the unpredictable part of the series and the only thing a good model should fail to forecast.
unit root|φ| = 1The boundary case where shocks accumulate permanently. Suspect it whenever a series trends, and test rather than eyeball — a unit root and a deterministic trend look similar but need different treatments.
  • Definition — a stationary series has a constant mean, constant variance and an autocovariance that depends only on the lag. Without it, the sample mean and correlation are not meaningful summaries of anything stable.
  • Why it matters — ARMA models, the ACF, the CLT for time series and standard errors on coefficients all assume stationarity. Apply them to a trending series and you get spurious regression: high R² between two unrelated trends.
  • Testing — the augmented Dickey–Fuller test has a unit root as its null, so a small p-value is evidence for stationarity — the opposite of the usual direction. The KPSS test reverses the null, and running both guards against the ambiguity.
  • Achieving stationarity — difference the series (for a stochastic trend), fit and remove a deterministic trend, take logarithms to stabilise variance, or apply seasonal differencing for repeating patterns.
  • Over-differencing is a real risk — differencing an already stationary series induces artificial negative autocorrelation and inflates the noise. Test before you difference.
  • Stationarity is about the joint distribution, not one sample path — you only ever see one realisation of the process, so every test is a statement about the generating process inferred from the single history you have.
  • Structural breaks mimic non-stationarity — a one-off level shift makes a series look non-stationary in the full sample even though it is stationary within each regime; test for a break before reaching for differencing.

Series, ACF and the unit-root boundary

  • ACF — the correlation of the series with its own lagged copies, measured in levels. It measures the total association at each lag, including indirect effects passed through intermediate lags.
  • PACF — the correlation at lag k after removing the effect of the intervening lags. It isolates the direct association, and it is what reveals the AR order.
  • Reading the patterns — an AR process has a PACF that cuts off after p while the ACF decays; an MA process has an ACF that cuts off after q while the PACF decays; both decaying suggests ARMA and needs information criteria to choose orders.
  • Significance bands — the dashed limits at ±1.96/√T are the pointwise 95% bounds under white noise. Looking across dozens of lags means roughly one apparent spike in twenty is expected by chance, so do not over-interpret a single marginal lag.
  • A slowly decaying ACF is the signature of non-stationarity — difference the series and re-plot before specifying any model.
  • ACF of residuals is a diagnostic — after fitting, the residual correlogram should look like white noise with no significant spikes. Any remaining structure means the model has missed something.
  • Seasonality shows up as periodic spikes — strong spikes at lags 12, 24, 36 in monthly data mean a seasonal term or seasonal differencing is required.

9.3 Models

ARIMA(p, d, q): y′t = c + Σi=1..p φi y′t−i + Σj=1..q θj εt−j + εt  ·  GARCH(1,1): σ²t = ω + αε²t−1 + βσ²t−1

  • ARIMA and SARIMA — add d differences for a stochastic trend and seasonal terms (SARIMA) for repeating patterns. Order selection uses ACF/PACF inspection plus AIC and BIC rather than a single rule.
  • Exponential smoothing — simple, Holt (level and trend) and Holt-Winters (level, trend and seasonality) weight recent observations more heavily, and each maps to a state-space model with an interpretable error structure.
  • State-space models and Kalman filters — express the series as a latent state evolving over time plus noisy observations. They handle missing values naturally, update in real time and cover ARIMA as a special case.
  • Granger causality — X Granger-causes Y if past values of X improve the forecast of Y beyond Y's own past. It is predictive precedence and nothing more; it can be produced entirely by a shared common cause.
  • GARCH — models volatility clustering, the empirical fact that large price moves bunch together. It forecasts the variance rather than the level and is the foundation of risk management and option pricing.
  • Modern alternatives — gradient boosting with lag features and dedicated architectures (Prophet, N-BEATS, temporal transformers) often beat classical ARIMA on large, multivariate or hierarchical series.

9.4 Evaluation

MAE = (1/n) Σ |yt − ŷt|  ·  RMSE = √( (1/n) Σ (yt − ŷt)² )  ·  MAPE = (100/n) Σ |yt − ŷt| / |yt|

ytActual valueThe observed value in period t, in the original units of the series.

  • Always split by time — train on the past and test on the future. Random shuffling leaks future information into the training set and produces hopelessly optimistic scores.
  • Use forward-chaining validation — expand the training window forward step by step so each forecast is evaluated using only information that would have been available at that moment.
  • Metrics measure different things — MAE is interpretable and robust, RMSE punishes large misses, MAPE is scale-free but breaks near zero. Report more than one.
  • Always beat the naive baseline — last value for a random walk, seasonal naive for seasonal data. This comparison is non-negotiable and routinely omitted in published results.
  • Evaluate at the horizon you care about — accuracy at one step ahead can be excellent while accuracy at twelve steps ahead is useless. Aggregate error over horizons hides exactly the failure that matters.
  • Check whether the truth lies inside the interval — forecast intervals need coverage diagnostics of their own; a point forecast that looks accurate can still have wildly miscalibrated uncertainty.
  • Beware of metric gaming — MAPE flatters models that under-forecast and RMSE flatters models tuned to avoid big misses; neither detects systematic bias, so plot residuals against time.

Real-life Applications

  • Sales and demand forecasting — ARIMA and Holt-Winters capture trend and seasonality for inventory planning, with the forecast horizon matched to the replenishment lead time.
  • Financial volatility — GARCH forecasts variance rather than price, driving risk limits, margin requirements and option pricing.
  • Capacity planning — seasonal models set server provisioning and call-centre staffing, where under-forecasting costs far more than over-forecasting.
  • Energy and utility load — strong daily and weekly seasonality plus weather regressors, often as hierarchical forecasts reconciled across regions.
  • Anomaly detection in monitoring — fit a model to the normal pattern and flag residuals that fall outside the prediction interval.
  • Public health surveillance — nowcasting and short-term forecasting of case counts where reporting delays and revisions are part of the modelling problem.

  • Entropy — the average surprise of a distribution, measured in bits. It sets the hard floor for lossless compression: you cannot encode data in fewer bits than its entropy.
  • Cross-entropy — the cost of encoding data from p using a code optimised for q. Since H(p, q) = H(p) + KL(p‖q), minimising it over q is equivalent to minimising the KL divergence to the truth.
  • KL divergence — the extra cost of the wrong code. It is non-negative, zero only when the distributions match, and asymmetric: using a broad q to cover a narrow p costs differently from the reverse, which is why forward and reverse KL produce different behaviours.
  • Mutual information — how much knowing one variable reduces uncertainty about the other. It is zero exactly at independence and captures nonlinear dependence that correlation misses entirely.
  • Perplexity — the exponential of entropy, interpreted as the effective number of choices. Lower is better for a language model, and it is the quantity behind the training loss curves of every neural language model.
  • The unifying identity of machine learning — minimising cross-entropy, maximising the likelihood and minimising the KL divergence to the true distribution are all the same optimisation. This is why the log-loss appears in logistic regression, softmax classifiers and neural networks alike.
  • Conditional entropy and information gain — the reduction in entropy from splitting on a variable is the information gain, used as the split criterion in ID3 decision trees and closely related to the Gini index in CART.

Real-life Applications

  • Compression — entropy sets the theoretical lower bound, which is why ZIP, gzip and modern codecs all approach it but never beat it on lossless data.
  • Language models — perplexity and cross-entropy loss measure how well a model predicts the next token, and the same quantity drives training and evaluation.
  • Feature selection — mutual information ranks predictors by how much they reduce uncertainty about the target, capturing nonlinear relationships that correlation-based ranking misses.
  • Decision trees — information gain chooses the splits, and the entropy of the leaf distributions measures remaining impurity.
  • Variational inference and VAEs — the ELBO is built from a KL divergence, and the balance between reconstruction and KL terms shapes the whole latent space.
  • Drift and dependence monitoring — KL divergence between live and training feature distributions detects data drift even when means and variances look unchanged.

11Missing Data & Data Quality

MCAR/MAR/MNAR, imputation, leakage prevention.

11.1 Mechanisms

MechanismMeaningExampleConsequence
MCARMissing completely at randomSensor randomly dropsComplete-case unbiased but loses power
MARDepends on observed variablesOlder skip income (age recorded)Imputation/weighting works
MNARDepends on missing value itselfHigh earners hide incomeNeeds model/sensitivity analysis
  • MCAR (missing completely at random) — the probability of being missing is unrelated to anything, observed or unobserved. Complete-case analysis stays unbiased, but you lose power and the missingness is still wasteful.
  • MAR (missing at random) — the probability of being missing depends on observed variables. Older respondents skip the income question, but age is recorded. This is the assumption that makes multiple imputation and inverse-probability weighting valid.
  • MNAR (missing not at random) — the probability of being missing depends on the unobserved value itself. High earners refuse to state their income, so the missingness carries exactly the information you wanted.
  • Why the labels are confusing — “at random” in MAR does not mean the missingness is arbitrary; it means it is explainable by what you did observe. The three acronyms describe the mechanism, not how much data is missing.
  • The distinction is untestable — you cannot verify MNAR from the data you have, because the required evidence is precisely what is missing. Justify the assumption from domain knowledge and always run a sensitivity analysis.
  • Consequences differ sharply — MCAR loses information; MAR can be repaired by a correct model; MNAR biases the answer no matter how sophisticated the imputation, unless you model the missingness mechanism explicitly.
  • Diagnose before repairing — compare the distribution of observed variables between complete and incomplete rows. Systematic differences indicate MAR or MNAR, which rules out simply dropping rows.

Filled circles are retained, hollow circles are missing. Under MCAR the holes are scattered at random, so complete-case analysis stays unbiased and merely wastes data. Under MAR missingness follows the observed X, so the holes cluster along that axis — the conditional model survives and imputation from X can recover the truth, but the usable range shrinks and the estimates become far less precise. Under MNAR the holes depend on the unobserved Y itself: the slope is now systematically flattened (the average over 120 simulated runs shows the bias) no matter which imputation you choose, which is why sensitivity analysis is the only honest response.

11.2 Handling Missing Data

  • Mean or median imputation — trivially simple and almost always wrong. It shrinks variance, distorts every correlation toward zero, and manufactures a spike of identical values that models will happily treat as real.
  • Regression imputation — predicts the missing value from the other variables, which preserves relationships better than a mean, but a single imputed value understates uncertainty because it pretends the guess is exact.
  • MICE — multiple imputation by chained equations fills in each variable repeatedly using the others, producing m complete datasets. Analyse each separately and pool with Rubin's rules to obtain honest standard errors that reflect the imputation uncertainty.
  • EM algorithm — treats the missing values as latent variables and alternates between their expected values and parameter re-estimation, converging to a maximum-likelihood estimate without fabricating data.
  • Inverse probability weighting — weights complete cases by 1/P(observed) so that the observed subset resembles the full sample. It targets the same goal as MICE through reweighting rather than filling in.
  • Missingness indicators — when the fact of being missing is itself informative, add a binary flag. Only do this if it is genuinely predictive, otherwise it adds noise and hurts the model.
  • Native handling in models — tree ensembles such as XGBoost and LightGBM route missing values by default, and some neural architectures learn mask-aware embeddings, which removes the imputation step entirely.
  • Never drop silently — complete-case analysis is a valid choice only under MCAR. Report how many rows were removed, which variables drove the removal, and how the conclusions change if you impute instead.

11.3 Quality Issues

  • Measurement error — noisy predictors bias slopes toward zero (attenuation) while noisy outcomes inflate variance and weaken power. Errors that are random are tolerable; systematic instrument bias is not.
  • Sampling bias — collection favours some units: online panels over-represent the digitally engaged, and hospital data over-represent the severely ill.
  • Data leakage — outside information sneaks into the features. The usual causes are scaling or imputing before splitting, features derived from the target or from the future, duplicate rows across train and test, and tuning hyperparameters on the test set.
  • Preventing leakage — fit every preprocessing step inside a Pipeline evaluated only on training folds, split by time or by group where the data demands it, and deduplicate before splitting.
  • The tell-tale sign — a validation score that is too good to be true. When a model reports 0.99 AUC on a hard problem, look for leakage before celebrating.
  • Duplicate and near-duplicate records — the same customer entered twice, or the same transaction recorded by two systems, inflates both confidence and apparent accuracy.
  • Definition and schema drift — a field quietly changes meaning (“revenue” becomes “gross revenue”) and the model silently degrades. Version your data definitions and monitor input distributions in production.

Real-life Applications

  • Surveys and censuses — income non-response is the textbook MAR/MNAR problem, handled with weighting adjustments and multiple imputation.
  • Healthcare records — MICE for missing lab values and vital signs, with missingness indicators for tests that were ordered for a reason.
  • ML pipelines — leak-proof preprocessing inside a Pipeline, fitted per fold, is the standard defence against over-optimistic validation scores.
  • Sensor networks and IoT — dropouts are usually MAR given device metadata, so imputation can be conditioned on device type, location and recent history.
  • Clinical trials and longitudinal studies — dropout is rarely MCAR, so regulators expect sensitivity analyses under explicit MNAR assumptions alongside the primary analysis.
  • Credit and insurance underwriting — missing income or credit history is informative about risk, so the missingness pattern itself becomes a feature rather than an inconvenience.

12.1 Copulas & Extreme Value Theory

Sklar: F(x, y) = C( F₁(x), F₂(y) )  ·  GPD: P(X − u ≤ y | X > u) ≈ 1 − (1 + ξy/σ)−1/ξ
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
F(x, y)Joint CDFThe probability that X ≤ x and Y ≤ y together. Modelling this directly is hard because marginals and dependence are tangled together.
F₁, F₂Marginal CDFsThe individual distributions of X and Y, each modelled separately with whatever family fits it best.
C(·, ·)CopulaA joint distribution on the uniform scale that carries only the dependence structure. Sklar's theorem says every joint distribution decomposes this way, which is what lets you model marginals and dependence independently.
uThresholdThe high level above which the tail is modelled. Choosing it trades bias against variance: too low and the asymptotic approximation fails, too high and there are too few exceedances.
ξShape parameterControls the tail heaviness of the generalised Pareto distribution. ξ > 0 gives a heavy power-law tail (financial returns), ξ = 0 an exponential tail, ξ < 0 a bounded tail (physical limits).
σScale parameterThe spread of the exceedances above the threshold.
tail dependenceλU, λLThe probability that one variable is extreme given that the other is extreme. Gaussian copulas have zero tail dependence, which is precisely why they underestimated the chance of simultaneous defaults in 2008.
  • Copulas — separate the marginal distributions from the dependence structure. You can model each variable with the family that suits it and then bolt on a dependence shape independently.
  • Tail dependence is the risk-relevant part — knowing whether extremes co-occur is what matters for portfolio loss, and it is invisible in a correlation matrix.
  • EVT — maxima and exceedances — the generalised extreme value distribution models block maxima and the generalised Pareto models values above a high threshold, giving a principled extrapolation into the tail.
  • Why EVT exists — the CLT describes the mean; the tail behaves differently and converges to its own limiting distributions. Floods, crashes and record losses need this machinery, not Normal approximations.
  • The shape parameter is the critical output — ξ > 0 implies moments that may not exist, which breaks mean-variance risk measures and justifies using expected shortfall instead.
  • Applications in finance and insurance — joint tail risk, reinsurance layer pricing, operational risk capital and catastrophe modelling.
  • Caveats — threshold and block-size choices are subjective, data in the tail is by definition scarce, and results are extremely sensitive to the extremes you happen to have observed.

12.2 Stochastic Processes

Poisson: P(N(t) = n) = (λt)n e−λt/n!  ·  Markov chain: P(Xt+1 = j | Xt = i) = Pij,   π = πP  ·  Martingale: E[Xt+1 | ℱt] = Xt
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
N(t)Count of events by time tThe number of arrivals observed up to time t. In a Poisson process the increments over disjoint intervals are independent.
λArrival rateThe average number of events per unit time. It is the only parameter, and it also sets the mean of the exponential inter-arrival times.
PijTransition probabilityThe chance of moving from state i to state j in one step. The rows of the transition matrix P sum to 1.
πStationary distributionThe long-run proportion of time spent in each state, satisfying π = πP. It exists and is unique for an irreducible, aperiodic chain.
ℱtInformation up to tEverything known at time t. The martingale condition is a statement about expectation conditional on that entire history.
martingaleFair gameA process whose expected next value equals its current value. It formalises “no exploitable pattern”, and optional stopping theorems for martingales are the basis of many sequential tests.
  • Poisson process — events arrive independently at a constant rate, so counts in disjoint intervals are independent and the waiting times between events are exponential.
  • Memorylessness — the exponential waiting time means the expected additional wait does not depend on how long you have already waited, a property that is convenient mathematically and frequently false in reality.
  • Markov chains — the future depends on the past only through the present state. The transition matrix encodes the dynamics and the stationary distribution describes the long-run behaviour.
  • Mixed and absorbing chains — an absorbing state (churned, dead) traps the process forever, which is exactly what survival and churn models represent.
  • Martingales — fair games where the expected future equals the present. They are the theoretical backbone of efficient-market arguments and of correct sequential testing.
  • Brownian motion and diffusion — the continuous-time limit of a random walk, used for physical diffusion and, in finance, for asset-price models.
  • Real-world validity — constant-rate assumptions ignore clustering, seasonality and regime changes, so real arrivals are usually modelled with inhomogeneous or self-exciting (Hawkes) processes.

12.3 Bandits & Regret

Regret: RT = T μ* − Σt=1..T μa(t)  ·  UCB: a(t) = argmaxa [ μ̂a + c √( ln t / na ) ]
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
μ*Best-arm mean rewardThe average reward of the arm you would pick if you knew the truth. It is the benchmark every policy is measured against.
μa(t)Reward of the chosen armThe true mean reward of the arm actually played at time t.
RTCumulative regretTotal reward forfeited over T rounds relative to always playing the best arm. It is the natural objective: you cannot avoid some regret, only bound its growth.
μ̂aEstimated arm meanThe average reward observed so far for arm a. Both UCB and Thompson sampling replace this point estimate with a distribution or an upper bound to force exploration.
naTimes arm a was pulledThe evidence available about that arm. It appears in the denominator, so under-explored arms get large bonuses.
cExploration constantHow aggressively uncertainty is rewarded in the upper confidence bound. Larger c explores more; regret bounds are usually stated in terms of this constant.
εε-greedy rateThe probability of playing a random arm instead of the current best. A fixed ε produces linear regret because exploration never stops; decaying ε fixes this.
sublinear regretRT = o(T)Regret that grows more slowly than the horizon, so the average per-round loss tends to zero. UCB and Thompson sampling both achieve logarithmic regret.
  • The dilemma — every pull is either a reward or a piece of information. Exploit the arm that looks best, or explore to find out whether a worse-looking arm is actually better.
  • ε-greedy — play the current best with probability 1 − ε and a random arm otherwise. Simple and effective, but a constant ε wastes a fixed fraction of all future rounds forever.
  • UCB — pick the arm with the highest upper confidence bound, which automatically explores arms that either look good or are poorly known. Its regret grows logarithmically in T.
  • Thompson sampling — sample a parameter from each arm's posterior and play the argmax. It is Bayesian, trivially parallelisable across users, and in practice very competitive.
  • Regret is the right metric — you cannot avoid paying to learn. What distinguishes a good algorithm is that the total price grows sublinearly in the horizon.
  • Contextual bandits — conditions the choice on observed features, which is the honest formulation of personalised recommendation and news ranking.
  • Practical complications — delayed rewards, non-stationary arm values, safety constraints that forbid experimenting on some users, and off-policy evaluation with biased logs.

ε-greedy vs UCB vs Thompson sampling

Regret is the reward you gave up by not always playing the best arm. The greedy policy (ε = 0) can lock onto a lucky-but-worse arm and then pay a linear price forever. 12.4 Uncertainty Quantification

Total: Var(y | x) = Eθ[ Var(y | x, θ) ] + Varθ[ E(y | x, θ) ]
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
θModel parametersThe fitted weights. They are uncertain because they were estimated from a finite sample, and that uncertainty is epistemic.
Eθ[Var(y | x, θ)]Aleatoric termThe average noise in the data-generating process itself, averaged over plausible parameters. It is irreducible: more data will not shrink it.
Varθ[E(y | x, θ)]Epistemic termHow much the prediction moves as the parameters vary. It reflects ignorance and does shrink toward zero as data accumulates.
y | xPredictive distributionThe full distribution of the outcome for a given input, not just a point estimate. Decision-makers need this to reason about downside risk.
MC dropoutApproximate epistemic uncertaintyKeep dropout active at prediction time and average many stochastic forward passes. Cheap, but only a rough approximation to a true posterior.
ensemble spreadPractical alternativeThe disagreement across independently trained models. Simple, effective and usually the strongest baseline for neural-network uncertainty.
coverageCalibration of the intervalThe fraction of times the reported interval actually contains the truth. Should match the nominal level; deep models are usually overconfident without correction.
  • Aleatoric uncertainty — irreducible noise in the data: sensor jitter, genuinely random outcomes. It cannot be reduced by collecting more data, only characterised better.
  • Epistemic uncertainty — lack of knowledge about the model or parameters. It shrinks as data grows and is large in regions of the input space you have never observed.
  • Why the split matters — epistemic uncertainty says “collect more data here”; aleatoric says “this is as good as it gets”. Acting on the wrong one wastes resources or hides real risk.
  • Tools — Bayesian models, deep ensembles, MC dropout, Gaussian processes, and quantile or conformal methods that sidestep the modelling question entirely.
  • Calibration is a separate problem — a model can rank well and still be overconfident. Check reliability diagrams and expected calibration error, not just accuracy.
  • Operational value — uncertainties enable abstention, human review, active learning and safe automation, which is where most of the business value of “knowing what you don't know” actually appears.

12.5 Conformal Prediction

Coverage guarantee: P( Y ∈ C(X) ) ≥ 1 − α  ·  C(X) = { y : s(X, y) ≤ q1−α }  ·  q1−α = the ⌈(n+1)(1−α)⌉/n empirical quantile of calibration scores
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
C(X)Prediction setA set of plausible labels rather than a single guess. For regression it is an interval; for classification a subset of classes; it can shrink and grow per example.
αMiscoverage levelThe error rate you are willing to accept, e.g. α = 0.1 for 90% coverage. Coverage is guaranteed at 1 − α, not approximately but as a finite-sample theorem.
s(X, y)Nonconformity scoreHow badly the label y fits the model at input X — the absolute residual for regression, or one minus the predicted probability for classification. Any score function works.
q1−αCalibration quantileThe appropriate empirical quantile of the scores computed on a held-out calibration set, with a finite-sample correction for the set size.
nCalibration set sizeThe number of held-out examples used to compute the quantile. Larger n gives tighter sets at the same coverage.
exchangeabilityThe core assumptionCalibration and test points must be exchangeable — essentially i.i.d. Under distribution shift the guarantee lapses, which is precisely why weighted conformal variants exist.
  • What it does — wraps any model, however opaque, and produces prediction sets with a distribution-free finite-sample coverage guarantee. The model itself is never modified.
  • The recipe — hold out a calibration set, compute a nonconformity score for each calibration point, take the appropriate empirical quantile, and include every label whose score falls below it.
  • Model-agnostic — it works on top of a neural network, a gradient-boosted tree or a simple regression, which makes it the most practical route to trustworthy intervals in production today.
  • Adaptive set size — easy examples get small sets and ambiguous examples get large ones. The set size becomes a usable signal of per-example difficulty.
  • Classification, regression and beyond — the same machinery covers intervals, class sets, and even structured outputs; variants handle time series, groups and covariate shift.
  • The critical caveat — the guarantee is marginal, averaged over the data distribution, not conditional on every x. Under distribution shift it silently degrades, so monitor coverage in production.
  • Why it is becoming a default — it delivers the calibrated uncertainty that Bayesian and ensemble methods only approximate, with almost no extra modelling effort.

12.6 Fairness & Privacy

(ε, δ)-DP: P( M(D) ∈ S ) ≤ eε · P( M(D′) ∈ S ) + δ  ·  Statistical parity: P(Ŷ = 1 | A = 0) = P(Ŷ = 1 | A = 1)  ·  Equal opportunity: P(Ŷ = 1 | Y = 1, A = 0) = P(Ŷ = 1 | Y = 1, A = 1)
SymbolNameWhat it means in detail
MRandomised mechanismThe algorithm that releases an answer — a trained model, a query result, a statistic. Randomness is essential: a deterministic mechanism cannot be differentially private.
D, D′Neighbouring datasetsTwo datasets differing in exactly one record. The definition constrains how much one individual's data can change the output distribution.
εPrivacy budgetThe multiplicative bound on how much the output distribution may shift. Smaller ε means stronger privacy and more noise; ε is a worst-case bound, so it composes additively across queries.
δFailure probabilityA small allowance for the pure ε guarantee to be violated — the probability that privacy loss exceeds ε. Usually set far below 1/n.
AProtected attributeThe group membership being audited: gender, ethnicity, age band. Fairness is always defined with respect to a specific attribute and a specific decision.
ŶPredicted decisionThe model's output — approved, hired, flagged. Fairness metrics constrain the relationship between this and the protected attribute.
YTrue outcomeThe ground-truth label. Metrics that condition on Y (equal opportunity, equalised odds) require it, which is often unavailable at decision time.
statistical parityDemographic parityEqual selection rates across groups regardless of anything else. Achievable without labels, but it can be satisfied by a useless model and conflicts with accuracy when base rates differ.
equal opportunityEqual true positive ratesEqual sensitivity across groups, so qualified members of each group are identified at the same rate. The most widely used criterion when the positive class is the benefit.
equalised oddsEqual TPR and FPRA stronger requirement covering both error types. Strictly stronger than equal opportunity.
  • The impossibility result — with unequal base rates, statistical parity, equalised odds and calibration cannot hold simultaneously except in degenerate cases. Fairness is therefore a choice, and it must be justified by the domain.
  • Which metric fits which domain — equal opportunity for screening where a missed qualified candidate is the harm; predictive parity when the score is used directly as a risk estimate.
  • Fairness is not just a metric — it also depends on which features are legitimate inputs, how labels were produced, and whether historical decisions embedded the bias being learned.
  • Differential privacy — add calibrated noise so that no single record noticeably changes the output. Smaller ε means stronger privacy, more noise and lower accuracy.
  • Composition and budgeting — privacy loss accumulates across queries, so the accountant matters as much as the mechanism. Repeatedly querying the same data at a fixed ε eventually destroys the guarantee.
  • Privacy is not anonymity in disguise — anonymised microdata is routinely re-identified by linkage, which is why DP, not de-identification, is the modern standard.
  • The tensions are real — privacy protects individuals but can harm small subgroups by adding noise asymmetrically; fairness and privacy must be evaluated together rather than assumed to align.
  • Operational artefacts — model cards, data sheets, bias audits, disaggregated performance reporting and a documented appeal path turn these principles into something auditable.

12.7 Causal ML & RL

Causal ML: uplift τ(x) = E[ Y | T = 1, X = x ] − E[ Y | T = 0, X = x ]  ·  RL return: Gt = Σk≥0 γk rt+k  ·  Qπ(s, a) = Eπ[ Gt | St = s, At = a ]

  • Uplift and CATE models — predict the effect of an intervention rather than the outcome, so marketing or treatment is targeted at the units that actually respond.
  • Meta-learners — S-, T- and X-learner architectures wrap existing supervised models to estimate conditional effects, and they are simple enough to use with any base learner.
  • Causal forests — an adaptation of random forests that splits to maximise heterogeneity in treatment effect, giving both an estimate and a confidence interval per unit.
  • Double/debiased machine learning — uses flexible ML for both the treatment model and the outcome model, then orthogonalises so that the final effect estimate is valid despite the ML components.
  • Reinforcement learning basics — states, actions, rewards, returns, value functions and policies. The objective is the expected discounted return, not the accuracy of a one-step prediction.
  • Exploration and variance reduction — RL must generate its own data, so exploration strategies and variance-reduction techniques such as baselines, GAE and n-step returns are central rather than optional.
  • Off-policy evaluation — when a new policy cannot be run online, importance sampling and doubly robust estimators estimate its value from logged data, with high-variance importance weights as the main practical obstacle.
  • Where the risks lie — distribution shift, reward misspecification and unsafe exploration are the failure modes that keep most RL deployments in simulation, and they are the reason causal and RL methods increasingly borrow each other's tools.

Real-life Applications

  • Finance and insurance — copulas and extreme-value theory model joint tail risk and price reinsurance layers, capturing dependence that correlation matrices miss.
  • Recommender systems — contextual bandits balance showing the content users will like against learning what else might work, and handle the cold-start problem directly.
  • Privacy-preserving analytics — differential privacy protects individual records in census releases and health-data platforms while still allowing aggregate analysis.
  • Marketing and retention — uplift models target only the customers whose behaviour the campaign actually changes, cutting spend and avoiding annoyance.
  • Clinical decision support — calibrated uncertainty from conformal methods and Bayesian models drives when to abstain and escalate to a human.
  • Autonomous systems and control — reinforcement learning with safety constraints and off-policy evaluation before any real-world deployment.
