1 Algorithm Description

Real-life analogy: Imagine you're a supermarket manager who wants to know which products are bought together so you can place them near each other. You have 10,000 receipts. Reading each one and asking "which pairs appear together?" is easy. But asking "which triples? quadruples? quintuples?" explodes combinatorially. The Apriori algorithm solves this by using one clever rule: "if a set of products is rare, then any larger set containing it must also be rare." This lets you prune millions of useless combinations and find the truly interesting patterns fast.

What is the Apriori Algorithm?

Apriori is a classic association rule mining algorithm. Given a dataset of transactions (each transaction is a set of items), it discovers frequent itemsets — groups of items that appear together in at least a minimum fraction of transactions — and then derives association rules like {Bread, Butter} → {Milk} with a measurable confidence.

Two output types:

  • Frequent itemsets — sets of items whose support ≥ min_support. E.g. {Bread, Butter, Milk} appears in 12% of baskets.
  • Association rules — implications A → B with confidence, lift, and other metrics. E.g. {Bread} → {Butter} has confidence 0.65.

The Key Insight — The Apriori Property (Downward Closure)

Apriori Property: If an itemset is frequent, then all of its subsets are also frequent. Equivalently: if an itemset is infrequent, then all of its supersets are also infrequent.

Why it works: Adding an item can only decrease support. So if {Bread, Caviar} appears in only 0.1% of baskets, then {Bread, Caviar, Champagne} can appear in at most 0.1% — it can't magically become more common. This gives us a way to prune huge parts of the search space.

How the Algorithm Works (Step by Step)

Step 1
Scan for 1-itemsets
Count the support of every single item. Keep only those with support ≥ min_support. These form L₁, the frequent 1-itemsets.
Step 2
Generate candidates
From L₁, generate candidate 2-itemsets C₂ by joining pairs. Then from L₂ generate C₃, and so on. Only generate candidates whose subsets are all frequent (the pruning step).
Step 3
Count support
Scan the database once per level. For each candidate itemset, count how many transactions contain it.
Step 4
Prune infrequent
Keep only candidates with support ≥ min_support. These form Lₖ, the frequent k-itemsets.
Step 5
Repeat until no candidates
Loop Steps 2–4 with k = k+1. Stop when Lₖ is empty — no more frequent itemsets exist.
Step 6
Generate rules
For each frequent itemset X with |X| ≥ 2, generate rules A → B where A ⊂ X, B = X − A. Keep only rules with confidence ≥ min_confidence.

2 Applications

Apriori was originally invented for market basket analysis, but the pattern-mining idea generalises to any domain where you have sets of co-occurring events.

🛒 Retail & E-commerce

  • Market basket analysis — find products bought together.
  • Product placement — arrange shelves so related items are near each other.
  • Cross-sell & up-sell — recommend add-ons based on the current cart.
  • Bundle pricing — identify items to package together.
  • Promotion planning — target discounts on frequently co-purchased goods.

🌐 Web & Media

  • Click-stream analysis — pages users visit together.
  • Content recommendations — articles/movies often consumed together.
  • Search query suggestions — queries that co-occur.
  • A/B test pattern discovery — which behaviours correlate.
  • Online advertising — ad placements that drive conversions together.

🏥 Healthcare & Bioinformatics

  • Symptom co-occurrence — diseases with related symptoms.
  • Drug interaction patterns — medicines prescribed together.
  • Gene expression — genes expressed together.
  • Adverse event detection — drug-event associations.
  • Medical claim analysis — procedures often performed together.

🏦 Banking & Insurance

  • Cross-selling financial products — accounts opened together.
  • Fraud detection — suspicious transaction patterns.
  • Credit risk — borrower behaviour associations.
  • Insurance bundling — policies bought together.
  • AML patterns — unusual account activity combos.

📞 Telecom & Cybersecurity

  • Service bundling — services purchased together.
  • Network intrusion detection — attack signature patterns.
  • User behaviour profiling — actions that co-occur.
  • Alarm correlation — related alerts fired together.
  • Spam pattern mining — word co-occurrences in spam.

🎓 Other Domains

  • Education — courses taken together.
  • Recommender systems — as a baseline for collaborative filtering.
  • Text mining — words appearing in the same documents.
  • Election analysis — issues voters care about together.
  • Sports analytics — plays co-occurring in winning sequences.
Why is Apriori still relevant? It's not the fastest algorithm anymore, but it's the most interpretable. Rules like {Diapers} → {Beer} with confidence 0.65 and lift 2.3 are immediately actionable — no black-box model, no trust required. In domains where decisions need to be explainable (retail, healthcare, banking), Apriori-style rules remain the gold standard.

3 Advantages

  • Simple to understand and implement — the algorithm is just a few loops. Anyone who understands "if a set is rare, supersets are rare" can follow the logic.
  • Interpretable output — rules like {Bread, Eggs} → {Milk} are human-readable and directly actionable. No black-box problem.
  • Guaranteed to find all frequent itemsets — no approximation. If it exists and meets the threshold, Apriori finds it.
  • Uses the pruning principle — the Apriori property dramatically reduces search space vs. brute-force enumeration of all 2d possible itemsets.
  • Parameterisable — min_support and min_confidence let you trade off between finding more rules and controlling runtime.
  • Works on sparse data — very effective on typical retail transactions, where each basket has only a few items out of thousands.
  • Extensible to many metrics — beyond support/confidence, you can filter rules by lift, conviction, leverage, cosine, Jaccard, Kulczynski.
  • Foundation for advanced methods — FP-Growth, Eclat, H-Mine, and many sequential pattern miners build on the Apriori idea.
  • Handles categorical co-occurrence naturally — works on any data that can be represented as sets of items per transaction.
  • Easy to parallelise — the counting step is embarrassingly parallel; each candidate itemset can be counted independently.
  • Available in every ML library — mlxtend, Weka, R's arules, Spark MLlib, and many more implement it.
  • Good baseline for recommendation systems — even simple rules often beat naive popularity-based recommendations.

4 Disadvantages

  • Multiple database scans — one scan per level. If the longest frequent itemset has k items, Apriori reads the whole database k+1 times. Very slow on large datasets.
  • Exponential candidate generation — the number of candidate itemsets can explode, especially with low support thresholds. For d distinct items, there are 2d possible itemsets.
  • Memory intensive — all candidate itemsets must be held in memory at once. Can be prohibitive for dense data.
  • Poor on dense datasets — when items appear in most transactions (e.g. text mining where documents contain most common words), Apriori's pruning barely helps.
  • Sensitive to min_support — pick it too high and you miss interesting rules; too low and the algorithm runs forever.
  • Confidence has a known flaw — a rule A → B with high confidence might actually have lower lift than the base rate of B. Always check lift too. (The classic "Diapers → Beer" example is a real illustration.)
  • Doesn't consider rare items — Apriori's support threshold biases toward common patterns. Rare but interesting rules (e.g., "customers who buy this rare item also buy...") are missed.
  • Generates too many rules — for dense data, the rule output can be overwhelming. Filtering by lift, conviction, or other metrics is essential.
  • Assumes binary presence/absence — Apriori works on sets. It ignores quantity (bought 3 vs. 1), price, and time-ordering.
  • No handling of negative items — "if a customer buys X, they do NOT buy Y" is hard to express in vanilla Apriori.
  • Not aware of taxonomy — "Bread" and "Whole Wheat Bread" are treated as unrelated unless you build a hierarchy manually.
  • Slow on high-dimensional data — many distinct items → many candidate 2-itemsets → expensive joins.
  • Doesn't directly optimise for recommendation accuracy — accuracy metrics like precision@K, NDCG must be computed post-hoc.
  • Rules are correlational, not causal — a strong rule means items co-occur, not that one causes the other. Placing butter next to bread doesn't necessarily increase butter sales.
The confidence illusion: Imagine 90% of baskets contain milk. Then a rule like {Pickles} → {Milk} might have confidence 0.88 — impressive-looking. But milk's base rate is 0.90, so pickles actually reduce milk purchases! Confidence alone is misleading. Always check lift. This is why lift and conviction are essential companions to confidence.

5 Pre-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Apriori produces association rules. To judge whether a rule is interesting and useful, we compute several metrics. The metrics fall into two groups: rule interestingness (is this pattern real?) and rule-set quality (is the whole rule set good?).

5.1 Core Rule Metrics — The Four Fundamentals

Support Frequency

What it measures: How often an itemset appears in the database. The "popularity" of a combination.

Support of an itemset X Supp( X ) = | { t ∈ T : X ⊆ t } | / | T |

Support of a rule A → B Supp( A → B ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) = P( A ∩ B )

T = the transaction database
| T | = total number of transactions
P( A ∩ B ) = probability a random transaction contains both A and B
Worked example: Out of 20 transactions, 8 contain both Bread and Butter.
Supp(Bread → Butter) = 8 / 20 = 0.40 (40%)
Interpretation: Higher = more common. Absolute value is dataset-dependent.
• Supp > 0.10 → commonly co-occurring
• Supp 0.01–0.10 → moderately frequent
• Supp < 0.01 → rare, but may still be interesting
✔ Use when Filtering rules — you only want patterns that appear often enough to be reliable and act on.
✘ Avoid when Looking for rare but high-value patterns (e.g. luxury co-purchases). Use lift or conviction instead.

Confidence Reliability

What it measures: Given that a customer bought A, how likely are they to also buy B? The "conditional probability" of B given A.

Formula Conf( A → B ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) / Supp( A ) = P( B | A )

Note asymmetry Conf( A → B ) ≠ Conf( B → A ) in general.
Worked example: Supp(Bread ∪ Butter) = 0.40, Supp(Bread) = 0.50.
Conf(Bread → Butter) = 0.40 / 0.50 = 0.80 (80%)
Interpretation: 80% of baskets with Bread also have Butter.
Interpretation scale:
• Conf > 0.8 → very strong implication
• Conf 0.5–0.8 → strong
• Conf 0.3–0.5 → moderate
• Conf < 0.3 → weak, often not useful
✔ Use when You want to know how reliably A predicts B.
✘ Avoid when B is a very common item anyway (like milk) — high confidence might just reflect B's base rate. Use lift.

Lift Interestingness

What it measures: How much more likely A and B occur together, compared to if they were statistically independent. Lift > 1 means positive association; lift = 1 means independence; lift < 1 means negative association.

Formula Lift( A → B ) = Conf( A → B ) / Supp( B )
                = Supp( A ∪ B ) / ( Supp( A ) · Supp( B ) )
                = P( A ∩ B ) / ( P( A ) · P( B ) )

Symmetric Lift( A → B ) = Lift( B → A )
Worked example: Supp(Bread ∪ Butter) = 0.40, Supp(Bread) = 0.50, Supp(Butter) = 0.45.
Lift(Bread → Butter) = 0.40 / (0.50 × 0.45) = 0.40 / 0.225 = 1.78
Interpretation: Bread and Butter co-occur 78% more often than chance.
Interpretation scale:
• Lift > 2 → strong positive association ✅
• Lift 1.5–2 → moderate positive
• Lift 1.0–1.5 → weak positive
• Lift = 1 → independent (no association)
• Lift < 1 → negative association (items avoid each other)
✔ Use when You want to filter out "trivially true" rules (like {pickles} → {milk}). This is the most important metric beyond support.
✘ Avoid when Support is very low — lift becomes unstable on small samples.

Conviction Interestingness

What it measures: How much the rule's confidence exceeds what it would be under independence — but with a form that's bounded and asymmetric. Particularly sensitive to the direction of the rule.

Formula Conv( A → B ) = ( 1 − Supp( B ) ) / ( 1 − Conf( A → B ) )

Note Range: [ 0, +∞ ). Conv = 1 means independence.
Conv > 1 → positive association.
Conv < 1 → negative association.
If Conf = 1 (perfect rule), Conv → ∞.
Worked example: Supp(Butter) = 0.45, Conf(Bread → Butter) = 0.80.
Conv = (1 − 0.45) / (1 − 0.80) = 0.55 / 0.20 = 2.75
Interpretation: Higher is better. Conviction directly measures how "surprising" the rule is compared to the baseline of B's rarity. More asymmetric than lift — Conv(A→B) ≠ Conv(B→A).

5.2 Additional Interestingness Metrics

Leverage (Piatetsky-Shapiro's Metric) Interestingness

What it measures: The difference between the observed co-occurrence and the expected co-occurrence under independence.

Formula Lev( A → B ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) − Supp( A ) · Supp( B )
                = P( A ∩ B ) − P( A ) · P( B )
Worked example: Supp(Bread ∪ Butter) = 0.40, Supp(Bread) = 0.50, Supp(Butter) = 0.45.
Lev = 0.40 − (0.50 × 0.45) = 0.40 − 0.225 = 0.175
Interpretation: Range [ −0.25, +0.25 ] in the binary case.
• Lev > 0 → positive association
• Lev = 0 → independence
• Lev < 0 → negative association

Leverage is symmetric (Lev(A→B) = Lev(B→A)) and additive across items — useful for aggregate scoring.

Cosine Similarity Interestingness

What it measures: The cosine of the angle between the two item vectors in binary space. Ranges 0–1 and is bounded — unlike lift, which can blow up.

Formula Cos( A, B ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) / √( Supp( A ) · Supp( B ) )
                = P( A ∩ B ) / √( P( A ) · P( B ) )
Worked example: Supp(Bread ∪ Butter) = 0.40, Supp(Bread) = 0.50, Supp(Butter) = 0.45.
Cos = 0.40 / √(0.50 × 0.45) = 0.40 / √0.225 = 0.40 / 0.4743 = 0.843
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher = stronger). Cos = 1 means perfect co-occurrence. Cos is bounded — never exceeds 1 — so it's robust to rare items.

Jaccard Coefficient Interestingness

What it measures: The size of the intersection over the size of the union of the two item sets.

Formula J( A, B ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) / ( Supp( A ) + Supp( B ) − Supp( A ∪ B ) )
Worked example: Supp(Bread ∪ Butter) = 0.40, Supp(Bread) = 0.50, Supp(Butter) = 0.45.
J = 0.40 / (0.50 + 0.45 − 0.40) = 0.40 / 0.55 = 0.727
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher = more overlap). Symmetric and bounded. Popular in text mining for document similarity.

Kulczynski Measure Interestingness

What it measures: The average of the two conditional probabilities. Symmetric and bounded.

Formula Kulc( A, B ) = ½ · [ P( A | B ) + P( B | A ) ]
                 = ½ · [ Conf( B → A ) + Conf( A → B ) ]
Worked example: Conf(Bread → Butter) = 0.80, Conf(Butter → Bread) = 0.89.
Kulc = (0.80 + 0.89) / 2 = 0.845
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher = stronger bidirectional association).
Kulczynski is valued because it's null-invariant — it doesn't inflate when one item is very rare, unlike confidence.

All-Confidence Rule-Set Quality

What it measures: For an itemset X, the minimum confidence across all rules derivable from X. Measures how "tightly bound" the itemset is.

Formula allConf( X ) = minA ⊂ X, A ≠ ∅ Conf( A → X \ A )
                   = Supp( X ) / maxi ∈ X Supp( {i} )
Interpretation: Higher = the itemset is more cohesive. Used to filter out itemsets where one item is much rarer than the others.

Zhang's Metric Interestingness

What it measures: A statistical measure of association that ranges from −1 to +1, treating positive and negative associations symmetrically.

Formula Zhang( A → B ) = ( Supp( A ∪ B ) − Supp( A ) Supp( B ) ) / max( Supp( A ∪ B )(1 − Supp(A)), Supp( A )( Supp( B ) − Supp( A ∪ B )) )
Interpretation: Range −1 to +1. +1 = perfect positive association, −1 = perfect negative, 0 = independence. Symmetric and null-invariant.

5.3 Rule-Set Quality Metrics

Rule Count & Rule Density Rule Set

What it measures: The total number of rules generated and how "dense" they are relative to the search space.

Rule density Density = #rules / ( 2d )    where d = number of distinct items

Rules per frequent itemset RPFI = #rules / #frequent_itemsets
Interpretation: A high rule count with low density is normal (pruning worked). A density close to 1 suggests the threshold is too low.

Redundancy Rate Rule Set

What it measures: Fraction of rules that are "covered" by a more general rule with equal or higher confidence.

Formula Redundancy = #redundant_rules / #total_rules

A rule A → B is redundant if there exists A' ⊂ A with Conf(A' → B) ≥ Conf(A → B).
Interpretation: Lower is better. Redundant rules waste space and confuse interpretation.

Rule Coverage Rule Set

What it measures: The fraction of transactions covered by at least one rule's antecedent.

Formula Coverage = | ∪r ∈ Rules { t : antecedent(r) ⊆ t } | / | T |
Interpretation: Higher = the rules apply to more of the database. A rule set covering only 5% of baskets is limited in impact.

Average Lift / Median Conviction Rule Set

What it measures: Aggregate quality of the rule set. Averaging lift or taking median conviction gives a one-number summary.

Average lift Lift̄ = (1/|R|) · Σr ∈ R Lift( r )

Median conviction Conṽ = median { Conv( r ) : r ∈ R }
Interpretation: Higher is better. Use median for conviction because it can be infinite; use mean for lift.

5.4 Recommendation-Specific Metrics (If Rules Are Used for Recommendation)

Precision@K Recommendation

What it measures: Of the top-K items recommended to a user, how many did they actually purchase?

Formula Prec@K = | RecommendedTopK ∩ ActuallyPurchased | / K
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher is better). A rule set with high precision@K produces relevant recommendations.

Recall@K Recommendation

What it measures: Of all items the user purchased, how many appeared in the top-K recommendations?

Formula Rec@K = | RecommendedTopK ∩ ActuallyPurchased | / | ActuallyPurchased |

MAP@K (Mean Average Precision) Recommendation

What it measures: Average precision across the top-K positions, accounting for rank order.

Formula MAP@K = (1/|U|) · Σu ∈ U (1/min(K, |Ru|)) · Σk=1K Prec@k · rel(k)

rel(k) = 1 if the item at position k is relevant, 0 otherwise.

NDCG@K (Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain) Recommendation

What it measures: Rank-weighted relevance score, discounted logarithmically by position. The industry standard for ranking quality.

DCG@K DCG@K = Σk=1K rel(k) / log2(k + 1)

NDCG@K NDCG@K = DCG@K / IDCG@K

IDCG@K = ideal DCG (all relevant items at top)
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1 (higher is better). NDCG@K = 1 means perfect ranking.

6 Post-Deployment KPIs — Detailed with Formulas

Once a rule set is deployed (e.g., driving recommendations, shelf placement, or promotions), you must monitor whether the rules still reflect current purchasing behaviour.

6.1 Rule Set Stability

Rule Overlap (Jaccard between Rule Sets) Stability

What it measures: How many rules from the training-time rule set remain in the production-time rule set.

Rule-set Jaccard J( Rtrain, Rprod ) = | Rtrain ∩ Rprod | / | Rtrain ∪ Rprod |

Note Two rules are "the same" only if both the antecedent and consequent are identical.
Some teams relax this to "same antecedent" or "same consequent".
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1. > 0.75 = stable. < 0.50 = the rule set has fundamentally changed — retrain or investigate.

Rule Support Drift Stability

What it measures: Change in support for each rule between training and production.

Per-rule support drift ΔSupp( r ) = | Suppprod( r ) − Supptrain( r ) |

Aggregate AvgDrift = (1/|R|) · Σr ∈ R ΔSupp( r )

Relative drift RelDrift( r ) = ΔSupp( r ) / Supptrain( r )
Interpretation: RelDrift < 0.10 = stable. > 0.30 = the pattern has weakened — consider removing the rule.

Rule Confidence Drift Stability

What it measures: Change in confidence for each rule. Confidence is what drives recommendations, so this is critical.

Formula ΔConf( r ) = Conftrain( r ) − Confprod( r )

Sign matters Positive = confidence degraded. Negative = rule got stronger.
Interpretation: ΔConf > 0.10 = significant decay, investigate. ΔConf > 0.20 = remove the rule or retrain.

Rule Lift Drift Stability

What it measures: Change in lift. Lift is the "interestingness" signal; if it falls below 1, the rule has become useless.

Formula ΔLift( r ) = Lifttrain( r ) − Liftprod( r )
Interpretation: If Liftprod drops below 1.0, the rule no longer indicates positive association — it should be removed immediately.

6.2 Business Impact Metrics

Basket Size Change Business

What it measures: Whether using the rules has increased the average number of items per basket.

Formula ΔBasketSize = AvgBasketSizepost-deploy − AvgBasketSizebaseline

Relative change RelChange = ΔBasketSize / AvgBasketSizebaseline
Interpretation: Positive = recommendations are working. Negative = recommendations are annoying users or the rules are wrong.

Recommendation Click-Through Rate (CTR) Business

What it measures: Of the recommendations shown, what fraction did users click on?

Formula CTR = #clicks / #recommendations_shown
Interpretation: Range 0 to 1. Compare to a baseline (e.g., popularity-based recommendations). Typical lift: 10–40% improvement.

Conversion Rate Uplift Business

What it measures: The increase in purchase conversion attributable to the rules.

Formula Uplift = ( CRwith_rules − CRwithout_rules ) / CRwithout_rules

CR = conversion rate = #conversions / #sessions
Interpretation: Higher is better. Typical successful uplifts are 5–25%.

Cross-Sell Revenue Business

What it measures: Additional revenue from cross-sold items that the user wouldn't have bought otherwise.

Formula CrossSellRevenue = Σ ( additional items purchased via recommendation × unit price )

Segment-Level Actionability Business

What it measures: Whether the rules' business impact differs significantly across customer segments.

F-ratio across segments F = Varbetween / Varwithin

Varbetween = Σs ws · ( KPIs − KPIoverall )²
Interpretation: Higher F = rules work well in some segments but not others — leads to segment-specific rule sets.

6.3 Operational Metrics

Rule Generation Latency Operational

What it measures: How long it takes to mine rules on the current dataset.

Formula Latency = time to mine rules given thresholds

Track p50, p95, p99 across retraining runs.
Interpretation: Apriori is slower than FP-Growth. For datasets with >1M transactions, consider upgrading to FP-Growth or using Spark's distributed Apriori.

Rule Serving Latency Operational

What it measures: Time to look up applicable rules and generate recommendations for a new basket.

Formula ServeLatency = O( |basket| × |rules_index| )

With a good hash index on antecedents: near-constant time per rule.
Target: p50 < 5 ms, p95 < 50 ms for a real-time recommendation service.

Recommendation Coverage Operational

What it measures: Fraction of sessions for which at least one recommendation could be generated.

Formula Coverage = #sessions_with_recommendation / #total_sessions
Interpretation: Higher = the rule set is applicable to more sessions. Low coverage = missing patterns or rules too specific.

Retrain Trigger Governance

What it measures: When to retrain the rule set.

Common triggers • Rule Jaccard < 0.65 (rule set has shifted)
• Median lift drop > 20%
• Coverage drops > 15%
• CTR drops > 10% vs. baseline
• Scheduled monthly/quarterly retrain
Golden rule: A rule set is a snapshot of customer behaviour at one point in time. Preferences shift, seasons change, products come and go. Monitor rule lift, confidence, and business KPIs. Retrain when the rule set stops matching reality — or on a fixed schedule (monthly for retail, quarterly for banking).

7 Mathematical Formulas — How Apriori Actually Works

The core idea: find all itemsets whose support exceeds a threshold, then derive high-confidence rules from them.

7.1 The Apriori Property (Downward Closure)

Property If X ⊆ Y, then Supp( X ) ≥ Supp( Y )

Contrapositive (the pruning rule) If Supp( X ) < min_support, then for every superset Y ⊇ X:
Supp( Y ) < min_support
→ All supersets of an infrequent itemset can be safely ignored.

7.2 Candidate Generation (Join Step)

Given Lk−1 (frequent (k−1)-itemsets), generate Ck (candidate k-itemsets).

Join rule Two (k−1)-itemsets p and q can be joined if their first k−2 items are identical:

p = { i₁, i₂, …, ik−2, ik−1 }
q = { i₁, i₂, …, ik−2, ik' }
→ Join produces: { i₁, i₂, …, ik−2, ik−1, ik' }

Condition Requires itemsets to be sorted lexicographically so that the join is well-defined.

7.3 Candidate Pruning (The Apriori Step)

Rule For each candidate c ∈ Ck:
If any (k−1)-subset of c is NOT in Lk−1, then remove c from Ck.

Reason By the Apriori property, c can only be frequent if ALL its subsets are frequent.
This pruning dramatically reduces Ck.

7.4 Support Counting

Formula Supp( X ) = | { t ∈ T : X ⊆ t } | / | T |

Equivalent count form count( X ) = | { t ∈ T : X ⊆ t } |
min_count = ceil( min_support × | T | )
Keep X if count( X ) ≥ min_count

7.5 Rule Generation from Frequent Itemsets

For each frequent itemset Z with | Z | ≥ 2: For each non-empty proper subset A ⊂ Z:
Let B = Z \ A
Compute Conf( A → B ) = Supp( Z ) / Supp( A )
If Conf( A → B ) ≥ min_confidence, output the rule A → B.

Property (fewer rules with larger consequents) Conf( A → B ) ≥ Conf( A → B' ) when B' ⊆ B
So if a rule with a small consequent fails the confidence threshold, so will all rules with larger consequents.

7.6 The Four Fundamental Metrics — Full Formulas

Support Supp( A → B ) = P( A ∩ B ) = | A ∩ B | / N

Confidence Conf( A → B ) = P( B | A ) = P( A ∩ B ) / P( A ) = Supp( A ∪ B ) / Supp( A )

Lift Lift( A → B ) = P( A ∩ B ) / ( P( A ) · P( B ) ) = Conf( A → B ) / Supp( B )

Conviction Conv( A → B ) = ( 1 − Supp( B ) ) / ( 1 − Conf( A → B ) )

7.7 Additional Metrics — Full Formulas

Leverage (Piatetsky-Shapiro) Lev( A → B ) = P( A ∩ B ) − P( A ) · P( B )

Cosine Cos( A, B ) = P( A ∩ B ) / √( P( A ) · P( B ) )

Jaccard J( A, B ) = P( A ∩ B ) / ( P( A ) + P( B ) − P( A ∩ B ) )

Kulczynski Kulc( A, B ) = ½ · [ P( A | B ) + P( B | A ) ]

All-confidence of itemset X allConf( X ) = Supp( X ) / maxi ∈ X Supp( { i } )

Zhang's metric Zhang( A → B ) = ( P( A ∩ B ) − P( A ) P( B ) ) / max( P( A ∩ B )( 1 − P( A ) ), P( A )( P( B ) − P( A ∩ B ) ) )

7.8 Complexity Analysis

Worst-case time complexity O( 2d · | T | · w )

d = number of distinct items
| T | = number of transactions
w = average transaction width

Database scans k + 1 scans, where k = length of the longest frequent itemset

Memory O( | Ck | + | Lk | ) for each level

Practical note With the Apriori property, the effective complexity is much smaller than 2d on typical sparse datasets.

7.9 Anti-Monotonicity and Other Pattern Types

Downward closure (Apriori property) Support is anti-monotone: Supp(X) ≥ Supp(Y) if X ⊆ Y

Upward closure (consequence) If X is infrequent, all supersets are infrequent.
If X is frequent, all subsets are frequent.

Maximal frequent itemset X is maximal frequent if it's frequent but no superset of X is frequent.

Closed frequent itemset X is closed if it's frequent and no proper superset has the same support.

Why maximal / closed matter Maximal itemsets are the "border" of frequency; closed itemsets retain all support information with far fewer patterns.

7.10 Generalised Sequential Patterns (GSP) and Beyond

Sequential pattern A sequence of itemsets ⟨ s₁, s₂, …, sm ⟩ where items in each sj occur together and s₁ precedes s₂, etc.

GSP support Supp( s ) = fraction of sequences containing s as a subsequence

Anti-monotonicity still holds If s is frequent, every subsequence of s is frequent.
This is what makes Apriori-style algorithms apply to sequences.

8 Glossary of Acronyms & Terms

Every abbreviation used in this study guide, explained in plain English.

Algorithm & Model Concepts

Apriori
A classic association-rule mining algorithm that finds frequent itemsets using the downward-closure property.
Association Rule
An implication A → B expressing that transactions containing A tend to also contain B.
Frequent Itemset
A set of items whose support meets or exceeds a threshold.
Candidate Itemset
A set of items that is being tested for frequency.
Apriori Property
If an itemset is frequent, all its subsets are frequent. Equivalently: if an itemset is infrequent, all its supersets are infrequent.
Downward Closure
Another name for the Apriori property.
Anti-Monotone
A property P is anti-monotone if P(X) implies P(Y) for all Y ⊆ X. Support is anti-monotone.
Maximal Frequent Itemset
A frequent itemset none of whose supersets are frequent.
Closed Frequent Itemset
A frequent itemset with no proper superset having the same support.
FP-Growth
A faster alternative to Apriori that avoids candidate generation using an FP-Tree.
Eclat
An Apriori alternative using a vertical (TID-set) data layout for fast intersection-based counting.
GSP
Generalised Sequential Pattern — an Apriori-style algorithm for sequential patterns.
PrefixSpan
A faster sequential pattern miner using prefix-projection.
HAM
Hyper-linked Array Mining — Apriori variant that uses a prefix tree.
RE-LIM
Recursive Elimination — a simple frequent-itemset miner often faster than Apriori.

Rule Metrics

Support
P(A ∩ B) — the fraction of transactions containing both A and B.
Confidence
P(B | A) — the conditional probability of B given A.
Lift
P(A ∩ B) / (P(A)·P(B)) — how much more often A and B co-occur than chance.
Conviction
(1 − P(B)) / (1 − Conf(A→B)) — asymmetric, sensitive to the direction of the rule.
Leverage
P(A ∩ B) − P(A)·P(B) — the "Piatetsky-Shapiro" measure.
Cosine
P(A ∩ B) / √(P(A)·P(B)) — bounded similarity metric.
Jaccard
|A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| — intersection over union.
Kulczynski
½ [P(A|B) + P(B|A)] — average conditional probability; null-invariant.
All-Confidence
min confidence over all rules derived from an itemset.
Zhang's Metric
A signed measure of association ranging from −1 to +1.
Interest Factor
Another name for lift.
Collective Strength
A measure of how concentrated the data is in a rule's joint presence and joint absence.
Certainty Factor
A variant of confidence that ranges from −1 to +1.
Odds Ratio
A measure of association for binary items.
Yule's Q
A measure of association similar to a correlation coefficient.

Precision@K
Of the top-K recommended items, the fraction that are relevant.
Recall@K
Of all relevant items, the fraction appearing in top-K recommendations.
MAP@K

NDCG@K
Normalised Discounted Cumulative Gain at K — rank-weighted ranking quality measure.
MRR

CTR
Click-Through Rate — clicks / impressions.
Coverage
Fraction of users or sessions receiving at least one recommendation.
Diversity
How different the recommended items are from each other.
Novelty
How unexpected the recommendations are to the user.
Serendipity
How surprising-yet-relevant the recommendations are.

Final tip: Apriori is the most interpretable association-mining algorithm ever invented. Always (1) balance min_support against runtime — lower thresholds explode the search; (2) never trust confidence alone — always check lift, since common items inflate confidence artificially; (3) prefer lift or Kulczynski for ranking rules in sparse data; (4) monitor rule Jaccard and business KPIs in production to catch drift early; and (5) if the dataset grows beyond a few hundred thousand transactions, consider FP-Growth or Spark's distributed implementation of Apriori.
